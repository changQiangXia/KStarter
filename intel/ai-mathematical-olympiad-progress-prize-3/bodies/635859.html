For seasoned Kagglers / general info: There are many improvements to AIMO2. Below is outline in the Rules, the Overview and Data tab; a unified overview of what changed is here. Please make sure to read them, in particular the Rules, as they are binding.

Popular requests from AIMO2 we delivered:

  • Impossible to guess: Final answer is a 5-digit number and you don't need to take the problem output mod 100000 yourself. Your LLM simply reads in the problem and has to provide a final answer, no further processing required. While already the 3-digit answer from AIMO2 ensured that any individual notebook couldn't feasibly guess the answer to 50 problems, there was a probability that if sufficiently many teams compete, some team could "guess itself to the top". The 5-digit answer ensure that that won't work anymore, which was a persistent worry for AIMO2 (even though it didn't materialize in dramatic fashion, as the winner, team NemoSkills from Nvidia managed to deliver a spectacular model, and their preprint has already amassed 50+ citations in just a few months since they publish their write-up in paper form. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16891). We did some statistical modelling of the competition and 10^5 hits the balance between making sure that LLMs can mostly still manage the arithmetic themselves, while ensuring that uniformly-at-random guessing baselines don't help. Going beyond 5 digits would have turned the competition into something that looks rather different from IMO-type math problems.
  • robustness: Another complaint, related to guessing, was that it might be possible to score highly by getting lucky in a different way, where your model might simply happen to output better sequences of tokens that solve the problems (i.e. picking the right trace from a set of generations). To counteract this, and reward stability, our evaluation on the private leaderboard will run twice, and you will loose half a point if you model solve the problem correctly only once.
  • no more whitelisting: You can use any model up until the last month of the competition (March 15th 2026, more precisely), as long as the license fits with our requirements. A long list of examples of what licences are / aren't allowed should give you a clear sense of what we accept and what we do not: https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/ai-mathematical-olympiad-progress-prize-2/discussion/548129 We will conduct spot checks to see whether what you submit conforms to our licensing requirements. This ensures less overhead, but still retains some type of control over the competition.
  • leveling the playing field with a compute ecosystem: We are the first large-scale competition to have secured 128 H100 GPUs and Tinker credits for you to finetune your models, free of charge. There were many complaints in AIMO2 that only big labs have a chance. We listened and we hope that this level of resources should make teams outside of big labs competitive. There will be an application process for these we'll publish later, once the competition has ramped up. There will also be more information about these organizations that we have partnered with in due course.

Other new things:

  • competition inside a competition: Instead of the Early Sharing Prize, we have several new Extra Prizes. Some of these focus on your submitted model, but some focus on other things that will support other contestants (e.g., better datasets -- the Math Corpus Prize). So if model training is not your thing, these prizes are a different route to have some fun with AIMO3.
  • team size: team size has increased to 8, so we can more accurately reflect who was involved in the creation of the model.
  • better compute: We are the first large-scale competition to offer contestants 5 hours of compute on H100s. With these, you can run much larger models than you could run for AIMO2. We hope that you will come up with something awesome that will help shrink the gap.
  • harder problems: As expected, we increased difficulty yet again to reach IMO-level. Our problems were reviewed by multiple humans -- and even for them reviewing these was quite hard work, so you LLM will need to be quite good to solve them. Naturally, we made them so hard that most open-source LLMs currently around should not solve many of them.
  • no time cap: Unlike for AIMO2, you can how choose freely how you like to spend your compute time: You can spend 4 hours and 59 minutes on one problem if you like, and the rest one the remaining problems. Problems will still be served one-by-one, in random order.
  • better dissemination: In general, on Kaggle, winners of competitions have their winning submission inside a Kaggle notebook, and converting them to run on commodity cloud compute that you can rent cheaply is quite cumbersome. To counteract this, we mandate that all winners containerize their models (where applicable) using a format the we will specify in due course, and assist in a bespoke, collective write-up of winning models.
