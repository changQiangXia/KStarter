Extra Prize Winners Announcement

We are very pleased to now announce the winners of the Extra Prizes (EPs) for AIMO3. These are: the MathCorpus Prize and the Write-Up Prize (awards based on detailed written submissions from participants); and the Hardest Problem Prize and the Longest Leader Prize (awards calculated from model submissions to the public leaderboard).

We were hugely impressed by the quality of written submissions for the MathCorpus Prize and the Write-Up Prize, many of which made novel and important contributions for AIMO3, and have wider benefits for the ML community. Several artefacts were of a standard suitable for conference submission, and tying scientific deliverables to a leaderboard represents a novel way to advance research in a coordinated and outcome-driven manner. Given that the main AIMO3 competition ended up being dominated by one notebook, it is great that the EPs have provided an alternative source of depth and innovation.

We want to thank all participants who engaged in these prizes for their hard work and high-quality outputs, as well as the openness and promptness of their replies. The process of selecting winners presented some very interesting questions and genuinely difficult decisions for the AIMO Team. We conducted a phase of public, in-depth reviews and rebuttal through Kaggle Discussions for all Math Corpus Prize and Write-Up submissions (in total approximately 60 submissions, enough to fill an entire workshop at a top conference), in order to flag items we potentially viewed as problematic, and to help contestants improve their overall quality and to approximate the review phases at major machine-learning conferences.

The MathCorpus Prize Winners and Runner-Up

The winning submission came from Yi-Chia Chen's CrystalMath. The runner-up submission came from NGUYEN's AstralMath. While only Yi-Chai Chen receives the cash prize of $30,000, NGUYEN’s submission is also worthy of broad recognition.

As well as their overall quality, these submissions stood out for how widely they were used by other Kagglers, including in the Write-Up Prize submissions. This practical adoption is an important signal that a dataset is useful to the community, which is what the MathCorpus Prize is designed to reward. 

CrystalMath was notable for building a hard-problem dataset that still retains a high quality-bar. Hard-problem datasets typically degrade quickly in correctness as difficulty rises, e.g. We have seen residual error rates of around 80% on hard items in comparable datasets. Yi-Chia's Contrast-Augmented Verification (CAV) pipeline is a novel approach to filtering for correctness on these hard problems. Our own spot checks during the review-rebuttal phase suggested a ~50% residual issue rate on hard items, materially better than comparable hard-problem datasets we examined. The improvement was borne out in the controlled fine-tuning runs the author reported, where the CAV-filtered version outperformed an unfiltered hard-pool baseline on the same difficulty band.

AstralMath is a different kind of contribution, anchored on scale and breadth. It comprises ~ 431,000 tool-use traces, generated across seven frontier and open models, and structured into stage1 and stage2 splits, with per-trace model identity, runtime and tool-call records. The accompanying AstralBench is a 50-problem hard-reference set with integer answers, providing a testing ground on a publicly available AIMO3-style dataset. The author reported substantial fine-tuning gains on multiple base models, and the rich tool-call metadata makes it well suited to training agentic, tool-using behaviours.

The MathCorpus Prize Special Commendations

We want to commend four other submissions that stood out to us, listed alphabetically, not by rank.

Jasper Dekoninck's PolyMath submission stood out for its proof-to-final-answer conversion pipeline and the work on testing revised problem statements. This makes the dataset much more useful for training on AIMO-style final-answer competitions.

JK-Piece's submission introduced a solution-hint TIR pipeline, which uses reference solutions as a hidden directional signal during trace generation and then strips the hint from the saved output. The author also published a reproducible SFT experiment with a downloadable model checkpoint, so it is easy to verify the approach end-to-end.

Laureano Arcanio's submission introduced an Inference Constraint Level (ICL) profiling framework, which records for each problem the minimal (model, max_tokens, timeout) configuration that solves it. We thought that this was a novel lens, which opens up approaches to dynamic compute-budget allocation, and may have wider uses (e.g. assessing problem difficulty when setting exams).

Tong Hui Kang's submission layered an annotation system over its curated traces, with structured reflection tags plus paragraph-level alternate continuations classified better / neutral / worse. The format is also good, shipping in both CSV and pre-tokenised JSONL with a public web visualisation and a published fine-tuned gpt-oss-120b LoRA checkpoint.

The Write-Up Prize Winners and Runner-Up

The winning submissions are Geremie Yeo and Chan Ka Vu's "AIMO3: A Collection of Delightful Side Quests" and natnitarach's "Model Capability Dominates". Each receives a $15,000 cash prize. The runner-up is team Hail Mary – aka Sebastian Gil and Kimberley Duran – with "A Practitioner's Plateau on gpt-oss-120b-MXFP4", which was a strong contender for one of the top two spots.

Geremie and Chan's writeup stood out as a ML and systems-focused contribution, rather than a wrapper around a public notebook. The work produced: the first published FP8 and NVFP4 quants of Nemotron Cascade 2 (already widely picked up on HuggingFace); a custom vLLM fork that makes prefix caching and speculative decoding co-exist on hybrid-Mamba models (a long-standing open issue in both vLLM and SGLang); and the first EAGLE-3 speculator for Cascade 2. All training code and models are open-source. The impact of their contribution extends beyond AIMO3. The vLLM fork in particular is a great contribution to the wider open-source ecosystem, fixing a missing feature that helps any hybrid-Mamba deployment. A write-up with this kind of broader future value stood out strongly.

natnitarach's writeup is closer in style to a research paper than a competition writeup. It carefully tests whether assigning diverse reasoning prompts to different voters improves majority voting on IMO-level problems, and finds that it does not. Across three models and over 20 controlled experiments, model capability dominates prompt-level optimisations by roughly 4x. This frames the performance gap as an issue of selection, not prompting strategy. The submission ships as an 18-page arXiv paper with end-to-end reproducibility from a single notebook.

Hail Mary's submission documents 16 candidate pipeline modifications, none of which beat baseline after Benjamini-Hochberg multiple-testing correction on a 199-problem validation set. The commitment to statistical hygiene stood out, with reproducible hypotheses and falsification criteria via git-commit hashes. Three common failure modes were extracted and packaged as runnable pytest detectors, letting future competitors check their work before burning submission slots.

The Write-Up Prize Special Commendations

We want to commend four other submissions that stood out to us, listed alphabetically not by rank.

Aaditya Paudel's AEN submission uses a three-role runtime architecture (audit, answer synthesis, proof/reasoning) coordinated by a central controller, with a curriculum-based prompt construction system. This submission does not have an AIMO3 leaderboard score (the final submission got caught in the closing-window queue), but the strong gains on the author's own AIME runs and the unusually high effort visible in the code notebook warranted commendation.

ice-beam and davendw's submission combines parallel and sequential test-time scaling with a global problem-difficulty estimator that decides whether continued recursive self-aggregation is worth the remaining time budget. The system moved the public LB mean from 38 to 41 while tightening run-to-run variance.

ippeiogawa's "Blackboard and Fork" submission introduces a shared tool-call ring buffer that lets parallel rollouts propagate intermediate discoveries to each other, together with a forked self-correction critique loop on top of gpt-oss-120b. The write-up is easy to read and the experiments are clean.

ofidaner's submission reframes SFT not as a way to teach the model new capabilities, but as a way to narrow its existing reasoning toward strategies that work. We particularly liked the honesty of the negative-results reporting across 10 or so ablation dimensions, most of which produced regressions. That careful documentation of what does not work is likely to be one of the more useful things here for future AIMO competitors.

Hardest Problem Prize

The winning contestant was Md. Hamid Hosen, whose submission was the only one to solve the problem internally referred to as ACUTES in both evaluation attempts, and he will be receiving a $30,000 prize. His submission also achieved a generally strong overall score of 43.5. Under the rules of the competition, this performance qualified him for the Hardest Problem Prize, which was awarded for solving the problem that had been solved by the fewest other contestants.
It is important to note, however, that ACUTES was not necessarily the most difficult problem on the private leaderboard. Other problems, such as the one internally referred to as ROLLER, were not solved by any contestant in both evaluation attempts. We will share more about this problems in due time. Given the strong performance of large language models on many mathematical tasks, and the fact that many of the roughly 4,000 participating teams employed similar models, these results suggest that certain high-school-level mathematical problems remain highly resistant even under very large-scale sampling regimes, including settings with pass@N values as high as N = 4,000. This highlights persistent limitations in LLM-based mathematical reasoning, particularly on problems requiring robust and reliable solution strategies rather than isolated successful attempts.

The Longest Leader Prize

The winning team was OSSMath — formerly known as “just a test”, comprised of yiyangzheng and shreyansh01m — which secured the prize, worth $20,000, by spending the longest cumulative period at the top of the leaderboard, as stipulated by the competition rules until February 2nd, the prize deadline. In this respect, the prize superseded the Early Sharing Prize of previous AIMO competitions by shifting the emphasis from being the first to make a strong contribution to demonstrating sustained competitive dominance. Simply submitting a high-performing notebook once was therefore insufficient; teams needed to continue improving, adapting, and defending their position over time. The prize mechanism also encouraged team mergers as a strategy for maintaining or attaining the leading position, resulting in notable leaderboard dynamics, on a separate Longest Leaderboard created to track time spent at the top, regarding which teams chose to merge and how those alliances affected the race for first place.
