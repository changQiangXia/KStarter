This thread collects some information about the two Writeup Prizes. First, similar to the Math Corpus Prize, please post a comment here with a link to tag your Kaggle Writeup post so that it becomes eligible for the Writeup Prizes.

  • the deadline for the Kaggle Writeup is one week after the competition ended = one week after the date listed on the Overview tab (which was 15th April). So the Kaggle Discussion post with the write-up needs to be made by 22nd April 23:59 UTC. Since it takes a while to prepare a good write-up, please prepare them in due time. We may ask some follow-up questions, that emulate a reviewer-author rebuttal phase usually encountered at machine learning conferences. But for that to happen, your write-up needs to be in place by the deadline. In particular, if I noticed that the submission are very heterogeneous, I may suggest specific dataset to standardize comparison, or questionnaires to be completed during the reviewer-author rebuttal phase.

  • Please use Kaggle Writeups, and not simply a Kaggle Discussion post.

  • As stated in the rules, submissions that won on Kaggle, will not be eligible for the Writeup Prize. Winners are subject to documentation requirements that will already lead de facto to something that will be close to a Writeup Prize submission (similar to how past AIMO winners documented their winning submissions).

  • The write-up should be based on your submission for the private LB, if you made one. Since scores may arrive only after 22nd of April, you will have a chance to update your write-up during emulation of the reviewer-author rebuttal phase to add the private LB scores. Note that on the public LB you may have observed that a more "boring" submission gets better scores than one that attempts a novel machine learning approach. In this case, you will need to make a decision of whether you want to optimize your chances to won on of the Writeup Prizes or you'd rather want to go for a Kaggle Prize. If you were not able to submit to the private LB (e.g., because of queueing), or your submission to the private LB was essentially void in other ways (e.g. you submitted the demo notebook that scores 0), you can base the Writeup Prize on the submission of your choice to the public LB. List clearly whether the writeup is based on the private LB or the public LB, and mention the score that your model achieved (if you based your model on a private LB submission, it will also have a public LB score, so mention that, too). Because a write-up based on a submission without a private leaderboard score is more likely to reflect overfitting, extra care to clearly highlight performance, and experiments should be carried out to compensate for the missing information that the private LB would have provided.

  • Since the rules state the writeup "should mirror an academic publication about your submission", it would be optimal if you could ensure that your write-up is not tightly bound to the Kaggle platform, to facilitate easy reproduction and dissemination of information. This means, for example, to

    1. EITHER to upload a (potentially slightly amended) version of your submission to HuggingFace, which includes the model, and any scaffolding on top (such as majority voting, i.e. the "harness") and provide a link to that model, as HuggingFace allows including scripts that contain the harness on top of the barel model;
    2. OR use a publicly accessible Google Colab notebook, inside which you download whatever bare model your submissions is based on from HuggingFace (where you might upload your own bare model, in case your finetuned it; or you might simply choose to download an existing LLM, such as the populare gpt-oss-120b, if your submission was based on that), so that the entire scaffolding you do on top of the model (e.g. majority voting) plays out inside that Google Colab notebook, and share a publicly accessible link to that notebook (this option may be the faster one, given that you merely need to adapt your Kaggle notebook). In exceptional cases it can be accepted to upload solely a Kaggle notebook (e.g. for financial reasons, if it were too expensive to run Google Colab).

    Consequence 1 of this is that you will need to open-source in some form the submission on which you based your writeup by 22nd April and keep them only throughout.
    Consequence 2 of this is that it is acceptable that the submission about which write in the writeup is not identical to the Kaggle submission; you still need to link to the Kaggle notebook in your write-up that you submitted, not solely the HuggingFace model, but you are allow to make changes for the HuggingFace model. In terms of changes that you are allowed to make: beyond minor changes needed to transfer the submission to HuggingFace, you are allowed to remove restrictions that you made to ensure that it ran under 5 hours. In that case, if you share graphs with performance of the model, at least one graph needs to compare the "restricted" version of the model that ran on Kaggle, to the unrestricted one, so reader can assess how strong performance gains are when one increases context size, remove quantization, etc. We need this in order to be able to have an apples-to-apples comparison of how the restricted version of the model performs, as not everyone might be able to afford hardware to extensively test unrestricted model versions, but likely many will have data on the performance of their restricted version. For more information about what you can remove for the unrestricted version, please see https://aimoprize.com/updates/2025-09-05-the-gap-is-shrinking where the AIMO2 winners remove Kaggle restrictions.

-- per our rubric, 60/100 points go towards reproducibility. One way to ensure reproducibility is by also including in either the HuggingFace repo, or in the Google Colab notebook the code needed to generate all figures from your submission.

  • The point-based assessment criteria in which we assess your write-up obviously apply just as well to finetuning approaches (which dominated AIMO2) as well inference-based approaches (which dominated AIMO3). E.g. for ablation studies, you could investigate things like: did you try other prompts and other inference methods? How well did they work? Did you use any internal benchmarks, if so which?

  • Adding "why you did X"-type explanations to your write-up will definitely help! Often times research articles fail to tell then why, even very famous ones, such as Attention is All you Need. The innovation just "falls out of the sky". Even if it is a highly convoluted process that lead one to some innovation, it helps a lot to give the reader a glimpse into the various things that were tried that did not work. Ablation studies are a formal way to illuminate the way, but it is clear you cannot do ablation studies for everything. But you have to use your judgement of how much of the "why" you choose to share. (As an off-topic remark, there is the analogous concept of "motivated proof" in mathematics, that also goes into the why rather than the what (its relevance for machine learning is highlighted in a preprint of mine https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15184 .)

  • A good template for a write-up are papers are top conferences that received praise (see Openreview reviewer discussion, see of example this paper that had only positive reviews, though there are also other good papers with a different layout that you might use a good template). These often are well written, motivate their approach well, have clear figure, and a good structure with the main paper telling the story and the appendix containing many details. Ideally, the level of your write-up should be on the level of an arXiv preprint that would have a decent chance of success to be accepted for a good conference. It is encouraged that you ultimately upload a version of your write-up on arXiv, potentially based on subsequent experiments you may do during the phase when we emulate reviewer-author rebuttals, not doing so will not affect how your write-up is judged.
