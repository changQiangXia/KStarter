Topic #539612: A few tips for beginners
  Author: Optimistix
  Posted: 2024-10-09 21:12:09.953000
  Votes: 55  Comments: 63

People new to the tabular playground series (TPS) often tend to have the same issues and doubts, leading to the same kind of questions and posts every month. Based on my experience of questions that newbies typically ask, I've put together a few tips to help newcomers (as well as others, since they may have to answer fewer questions), hope it's helpful:




 Read the 'Rules', 'Overview' and 'Data' pages:  The webpage for the competition displays 9 links, including ones for 'Discussion' &amp; 'Leaderboard'. It's a good idea to read all of them, but it's especially important to read the 'Rules', 'Overview' and 'Data' pages, to understand what the competition is all about.


 Pay attention to the competition metric:  The 'Overview' page specifies the metric to be used, e.g. this month, it's area under the ROC curve. For those unfamiliar with the metric, there's also a link explaining it. Yet many users keep posting about the accuracy their models are achieving, regardless of what the actual metric being used is. Reading the 'Overview' page will save you from wasting your time on optimizing irrelevant (or worse, misleading) metrics.


 Expect noise:  The TPS datasets are generated by a deep learning model trained on a smaller tabular dataset (often, but not always, 'real'). It's cleaned to some extent, but there's always synthetic noise, and there may also be artifacts that are deliberately included. So you should expect various manifestations of noise, including (e.g.) differences in distributions of variables between training &amp; test data, categories that are present in one but not the other, and so on.


 Obtain the original data, &amp; consider whether to use it:  The 'Data' page includes a link to the aforementioned original dataset - it's a good idea to check it out, and test whether using it helps improve performance or not (the 'Data' page also encourages you to do this).


 Read the Discussion posts:  There are a lot of very clever and experienced Kagglers who participate in the TPS, and generously share their insights, including code, links to relevant content, and so on. Reading the discussion posts regularly will help you stay on top of the relevant things to know about the current competition, what works, what doesn't, etc. 


 Learn from the public notebooks:  Similarly, many people share their code via public notebooks. You can discover some high quality work, and code/ideas/results that you can build upon. There's a fair bit of not-so-high quality stuff as well - you'll have to lear how to separate signal from noise in this context as well.


 Ask for help, but try to find the answer by yourself first:  As mentioned, our fellow Kagglers are generous, and ready to help others with their time and effort. But we should always try to figure things out by ourselves first - it's fair to others, and also the best way for us to learn and improve. Try not to end up creating more work for yourself &amp; especially others, just because you didn't take a few minutes to first think things through yourself.


 Resist the temptation of "blind blending":  Finally, there are always going to be a few (or many!) public notebooks that score high simply by blending others' submissions. Blending in general is a good idea when done systematically; but such notebooks are often based on guessing the weights, which is why I call it "blind blending". This often leads to short-term pleasure &amp; long-term pain, as you may come crashing down at the end of the month, when the private LB is revealed, and there's a major shakeup. So it's a good idea to avoid such blending, though it is easier said than done - seeing one's name high up on the LB is a strong tempation, &amp; we've all succumbed at some point or the other.


That's it for now - I'll update the post if I (or others) think of additional relevant points. All the best, and Happy Kaggling!
