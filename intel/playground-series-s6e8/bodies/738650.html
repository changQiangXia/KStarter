7th Place — Way too many models, one simple stack

Hey everyone 👋

This competition got a bit out of hand for me. I started with the usual tree models, then added neural nets, public OOFs, rare tabular architectures and a lot of weak models. In the end, six regularized Logistic Regressions had to sort out the mess.

The submission I actually trusted

My best visible score was 0.97139, which put me 6th on the public leaderboard. But this was not my primary strategy. It was mostly a fun satisfaction hedge to climb the visible board and use my second final slot on something different.

My real solution was the strict-OOF submission:

  • OOF: 0.97087888
  • Public LB: 0.97127
  • Private LB: 0.97095
  • improvement on 10/10 folds
  • paired stratified bootstrap 95% CI: [+0.000020, +0.000039]

Validation first

I froze one ten-fold stratified split and reused it for every local run and every meta-model I controlled. Every serious model needed a complete OOF prediction, the matching test prediction and clear provenance.

I was also careful at level 2. Honest level-1 OOFs are not enough if you search blend weights on the same OOF rows used to report the score. Final weights were therefore learned on nine folds and evaluated on the tenth.

After removing duplicates, incomplete pairs and questionable meta-predictions, the final clean pool contained 556 streams: 206 local and 350 public. Another 42 local streams were excluded. I did not personally train 556 models: a big part of the work was collecting and honestly evaluating what the community shared.

From 556 verified prediction streams to the strict-OOF primary.

What worked in the data

The best feature idea was an artifact of the synthetic generator. Some continuous-looking values repeated many times and had surprisingly stable target rates. Exact-value target encoding was therefore extremely strong even when the raw feature itself was weak.

The useful feature families were mainly:

  • fold-safe exact-value target encoding on nine columns;
  • train+test frequencies;
  • pair and feature-on-feature statistics;
  • decimal and rounding artifacts.

Moving from four to nine target-encoded columns added roughly +0.00191 on XGBoost. Many sensible domain ratios, reconstructed totals and kNN features did almost nothing after that.

Here, “level 1” means that the model only consumed engineered input features, not predictions from other models. RealMLP and TabM can still average several members internally. I excluded pseudo-labeled transductive variants from this table. I also screened DCN and TabPFN-style variants, but they did not produce a competitive, fully comparable ten-fold local OOF.

Instead of keeping only the best solo models, I asked whether each family fixed different errors. A group of 50 individually weak public models added +0.000060 to the blend, while a random same-sized control added only +0.000013.

For the stack, I rank-normalized every prediction and trained six cross-fitted Logistic Regressions:

C = 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3

I averaged their prediction ranks and ranked the result again. That was my rank-LogReg C-bag.

One funny result: C=0.01 was the weakest member alone, but removing it reduced the C-bag by 0.00000189 (0.97085557 → 0.97085368). Even a weak model can bring a useful error direction.

The last gains

The blend progressed like this:

Stage OOF
first strong diverse blend 0.970315
560-stream mega-blend 0.970737
593-stream mega-blend 0.970763
+ RealMLP + Depth-9 XGBoost 0.970820
+ negative residual correctors 0.970849
final primary 0.970879

The negative weights were one of my favourite findings. Some weaker stacks represented directions my main blend was overusing. Subtracting 25% of a public-only stack and 10% of a Rank-Gauss stack improved OOF from 0.970820 to 0.970849.

During the final hours, I refitted the clean 556-stream C-bag on a GPU and blended it with the previous final. The ten outer-fold weights assigned to the C-bag were all between 0.50 and 0.55, so I used their median 0.525 on test:

47.5% previous final + 52.5% clean C-bag

This produced the final 0.97087888 OOF / 0.97127 public LB submission.

The dash is intentional: the hedge included a test-only public blend, so it had no honest matching OOF. The first was the leaderboard hedge; the second was the solution I trusted from local validation.

The public hedge

The 0.97139 hedge was a Gaussian-copula blend of my weak-model ensemble, Souvik's blend2, a small cstdy component and my 593-stream mega-blend. I selected it because it kept the best visible score while reducing rank correlation with my primary to 0.99670.

Again, this was the fun leaderboard submission. The 0.97087888 OOF model was the strategy I actually trusted. But the fun hedge won this time: it scored 0.97112 private and gave me 7th place. Even funnier — or more painful — two last-hour variants I did not select scored 0.97113 private, which would have put me in 6th place. Classic Kaggle.

Thanks

Thanks to Chris Deotte, Michael Qiu, Kodai, Naji, cstdy, Szymon Kłapiński, Souvik D. Biswas, Adarsh Aleti, and everyone who shared OOF/test pairs. Honest local testing would not have been possible without those contributions.

My main takeaway:

Keep every honest OOF, care about different errors more than solo scores, and never let the fold you are evaluating choose its own blend.
