Topic #652262: Kaggle messed up this dataset on purpose
  Author: Tilii
  Posted: 2025-12-06 00:12:26.686000
  Votes: 68  Comments: 45

We have been at if for a few days, so we have some idea what's happening. Not even sure if I should be writing on this topic so early, as it can potentially dampen the enthusiasm of future users to take part in this competition.

This insightful post by the ever-industrious @mpwolke got me thinking. There are some dizzyingly high predictive values in the paper that is referenced in that thread, which made me think that diabetes seems relatively east to predict.

How could that be squared with the lousy models we are making in this competition? Getting AUC of ~0.73 is generally okay, but nothing to write home about. An accuracy value of ~0.69 is not worth bragging about either. In case you haven't checked, our models have log-loss of ~0.58. The best possible log-loss value is 0, and a totally random prediction (a probability of 0.5 for each data point) scores 0.693, so our predictions are much closer to random than perfect. If you still need to visualize how lousy our models are in this competition, here is a cumulative distribution function from one of my other posts. Those lines should be hugging the horizontal blue line at zero in the left part of the plot, and the flat line at 1 in the right part of the plot. Yet they are nowhere near that.



So why are our models so bad if diabetes can generally be predicted well? Part of the answer was given in the post with which I started above: a feature called HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) has been removed in the dataset we have, even though it is present in original data. More clues can be found here, here and here. All of them boil down to the same thing: there is a significant distribution shift between the original data, which is synthetic to begin with, and our double-synthetic dataset.

I decided to test what kind of a prediction can be made from the original data. I fired up XGB, LGB and CatB with pretty standard parameters, assigned categorical and numerical variables, but other than that didn't do anything else. Their AUC CV scores, in the same order as above: 0.99980, 0.99982 and 0.99989. The original dataset can be classified almost perfectly, and Kaggle decided to muck that up. You will see density distributions below for our data and for the original. All the features that show good separation between 0 and 1 classes were removed: glucose_fasting, glucose_postprandial, hba1c, diabetes_risk_score. In S5E9 we dealt with a dataset to which noise was added deliberately to make it more difficult, and it seems that Inversion and company decided to follow suit. waist_to_hip_ratio was seemingly discretized in our data (by removing or replacing 0.80, 0.90, 1.00, etc values) rather than being continuous. systolic_bp, which was left-side clipped in the original, was made to be narrower and lacks some of the high values. Same for ldl_cholesterol, triglycerides and screen_time_hours_per_day, although the last one was never a major contributor. I'll let you go through the graphs below and extract your own conclusions. At this point it seems that the original dataset should not be used at all, either by concatenation to train folds, or as a modifier for target encoding.

Kaggle seems to be finding new ways to throw junky data at us, in addition to what @cdeotte uncovered in his famous post. Well done, Kaggle!
