Overview

We developed a 7.5M-parameter recurrent actor–critic network and trained separate Slowking and Dragapult specialists through distributed population self-play. The complete training lineage began from random initialization and used no behavior cloning or human demonstrations.

At each decision, the model received the current engine observation and the available legal actions. It produced a score for every legal action and selected the highest-scoring one during evaluation and submission.

The final agents used direct neural-network inference. They did not use runtime search, engine rollouts, or hand-written action overrides. During development, we deliberately avoided fixing individual mistakes with card-specific rules. Instead, we tried to improve the information available to the network and the way it learned from self-play.

Development

Our early agents could complete games but frequently made decisions that were difficult to explain. Some mistakes came from problems in the interface between the game engine and the neural network. Information that appeared obvious in a replay was not always represented clearly enough for the model.

We revised the observation and legal-action encodings over several versions. The final model represented the current game state, the legal actions, and relevant card identities while using recurrent memory to retain information from earlier decisions. Additional learned components allowed an action to be evaluated in relation to the surrounding board and other available choices.

The final architecture contained both an actor and a critic. The actor scored legal actions, while the critic estimated the expected final outcome for training. Several auxiliary objectives also encouraged the network to recognize visible changes caused by actions. These objectives provided additional learning signals but did not directly define which strategic choice was best.

Multi-part effects were handled one decision at a time. After the model selected an option, the engine returned the next prompt with the previous choice recorded, and the model selected again. This allowed the same network to handle a changing number of legal cards, attacks, targets, and other options.

Self-play training

Training used many CPU actors to play games through the official engine while a single GPU learner updated the network. Because the actors and learner operated continuously, the training system included corrections for trajectories generated by slightly older policy versions.

We also maintained a population of frozen historical policies. The current policy played both against recent versions and against selected older versions. This reduced simple forgetting and made it harder for training to collapse into repeatedly exploiting only the latest opponent.

Specialist training mixed the submitted deck with a broader field of decks collected from public competition data. Both seats contributed training examples, and the agent encountered mirrors as well as different opposing strategies. The purpose was to learn the submitted deck deeply without training only for one fixed opponent.

The only strategic reward was the final game result. Damage and Prizes were not rewarded separately because gaining either does not necessarily mean that the overall decision was good. Across the corrected model and the two specialists, training processed approximately 5.5 billion policy decisions using distributed CPU self-play and a single RTX 4090 learner. Based on measured throughput, this required roughly five GPU-days, although exact GPU-hours were not recorded and training speed varied between stages.

Slowking and Dragapult

We submitted separate specialists for Slowking and Dragapult because the decks presented meaningfully different decision patterns.

Slowking revolves around preparing the deck and selecting an appropriate attack for the current position. Its best choice can change with the opponent’s board, remaining Prizes, available Energy, and the resources needed for later turns. This made it an interesting test of whether the policy could learn situational attack selection rather than always preferring the same high-damage option.

Dragapult requires the agent to decide how to distribute damage and convert that damage into future Prizes. Immediate damage does not always identify the best action because the location of damage counters can determine which knockouts become available later. This placed more pressure on multi-turn planning.

We trained the two specialists sequentially using the same general architecture. Slowking warm-started from the completed Dragapult lineage rather than beginning independently from random initialization.

Evaluation

We used controlled offline games to distinguish policy improvement from favorable deck assignment or seating. Candidate and reference policies were given identical decks, and paired schedules swapped their seats. These tests showed substantial improvement on both submitted decks.

We also tested whether additional inference-time evaluation would improve the final policy. In our experiments, the evaluated variants performed worse than direct action selection. We therefore kept the submitted agents simple and selected the highest-scoring action directly.

During self-play, actions were sampled from the policy distribution to encourage exploration. During evaluation and submission, we used greedy selection for more consistent execution. We considered this preferable because the remaining failures were usually tactical mistakes rather than evidence that opponents were exploiting predictable behavior.

What we learned

Despite their improvement, neither specialist played perfectly. Both could occasionally miss strong actions, mishandle resources, or fail to complete a plan that appeared clear when reviewing the replay.

This became the central lesson of the project: a policy can learn many patterns associated with winning without reliably reproducing every game calculation or strategic dependency. A higher win rate does not necessarily mean that every underlying mechanic has been learned correctly.

At the same time, the final result showed that large-scale population self-play could train useful specialist policies without demonstrations, runtime search, or a growing collection of hand-written tactical patches.
