Summary

Our team, Boss's Orders Are All You Need, placed 24th in Kaggle Pokémon TCG AI Battle. This article explains the small Transformer at the center of our solution and the ecosystem of imitation learning, reinforcement learning, match evaluation, and deck development that trained it.

Strength in Pokémon TCG depends on the combination of deck construction, play, and the distribution of opponents. The same policy can perform differently when opposing lists or metagame trends change. We approached this as a problem of building a system that continuously trains diverse agents, measures their strength and matchups in a common environment, and lets human experts inspect their decisions, alongside developing strong individual agents.

The core model is an end-to-end Transformer with approximately 1M parameters. We first imitate play with a GBDT using approximately 100–1,000 leaderboard or human replays, then run that teacher in internal matches to generate 15,000–30,000 games. We distill this experience into the Transformer and continue with population-based PPO/GAE against approximately 20–40 types of opponents. From the general base model, we derive Experts for important matchups and switch policies according to cards revealed during a game.

Choosing a small model served more than inference efficiency. It allowed us to experience many matchups quickly and train several decks and policies within the same development period. We implemented Transformer inference in C++ with OpenBLAS and AVX2/FMA, processing a representative 32-token input in approximately 1 ms with one CPU worker and one thread. Running everything from game progression to training-data recording in C++ enabled collection of 5,000 matches in approximately 30–40 seconds on 64 vCPU. Parameter updates use a GPU.

For evaluation, we accumulated results from 320 agents and approximately 2.2 million matches. Bayesian ratings, dynamic opponent allocation, and clustering by win-rate profiles let us inspect overall strength and matchup-specific strengths and weaknesses. Deck development combined expert knowledge with this evaluation, feeding replay comments back into feature design and training decisions. Training many small models, measuring their matchups, and using those results to decide what to train next forms the core of the ecosystem.

We have also released sample implementations on GitHub that demonstrate the learning pipeline, including C++ inference, GBDT imitation, Transformer distillation, and PPO.

1. Why Connect Learning, Evaluation, and Deck Development?

1.1 Strength Depends on the Opponent Distribution

Pokémon TCG contains matchups that cannot be explained by a simple ordering of strength. Even a deck that consistently deals high damage may lose its winning route against an opponent that blocks that damage. When fast decks become common, strategies that take longer to assemble their board become disadvantaged. When counters to those fast decks spread, other constructions become useful.

During development, therefore, we need to know more than the win rate against one opponent. We need to consider which families a policy beats or struggles against, how common those families are, and which matchups change after a card substitution.

We used leaderboard deck trends, internal match results, and human expert knowledge for different purposes. The leaderboard provides an entry point for identifying strategies that actually appear. Internal matches let us test the same candidate against many opponents and inspect matchups in detail. Experts interpret deck intentions, resource use, and missed winning lines that results alone do not reveal.

1.2 Development Around Small Models

As a model grows, each decision requires more computation. Games repeat this operation many times, so inference speed directly affects the number of matches available for learning. This effect grows when models for multiple decks and matchup specialists are trained in parallel.

We therefore chose to run models of approximately 1M parameters quickly and spend compute on experience and evaluation iterations. This does not claim that approximately 1M is optimal in every setting. It is a design choice that combines our state representation, action representation, and C++ runtime to balance practical strength with development speed.

2. Starting from a Small Replay Dataset

2.1 Turning Replays into State–Action Supervision

Training begins with approximately 100–1,000 replays from leaderboard players or human experts using the target deck. At each decision, we associate the observation, the legal actions supplied by the engine, and the action actually selected. For multiple selections, we retain both selection order and the stopping decision.

The teacher requires information that the acting player could observe at that moment. Replays intended for convenient viewing and observation–action sequences intended for training serve different purposes. Using an omniscient spectator board, or mistaking the state after a selection for the state before it, breaks the agreement between training and actual inference. We therefore retain raw Observations and their corresponding selections for training.

Because positions within a game are strongly correlated, training and validation are split by game. We also bind the target deck's card IDs and counts to the model assets to avoid accidentally connecting data from a different list.

2.2 Why Use a GBDT as the Initial Teacher?

When a Transformer was trained directly on a small replay dataset, it could fit the recorded moves yet perform poorly against other agents. The teacher had visited too few states: memorizing choices in those states did not provide sufficient coverage of subsequent, different developments.

We therefore first train a GBDT on structured state–action features to learn selection preferences. Explicit features for card interactions, attack availability, knockouts, and search targets transfer knowledge from the small replay set into an initial policy. This produces a teacher capable of playing games before we begin updating a neural network through reinforcement learning.

Development records showed GBDT teachers reaching approximately 200 internal rating points below the final PPO model and reducing the time to an initial reinforcement-learning milestone by approximately 50–80%, depending on the deck, compared with starting from direct replay learning. We treat these as estimates tied to the development conditions. They do not imply the same reduction for every deck or establish general superiority through a controlled comparison across multiple seeds.

2.3 GBDT Inputs, Structure, and Outputs

The GBDT ranks the legal candidates available in a position rather than classifying the state into a fixed action class. Candidates belonging to one prompt form a training group, and the ranker learns to place the teacher's selected candidate near the top.

Item Description
Input unit A game state paired with one legal action candidate
Features A fixed-width sparse vector of approximately 5,000 dimensions
Main features Selection context, cards, target zones, public board, HP, Energy, resource counts, attacks, search, evolution, healing, and disruption
Learning method LightGBM LambdaRank
Model split Separate rankers for MAIN and other selection contexts
Ensemble size Several hundred to approximately 1,000 trees per ranker
Output A ranking score for each legal action

Each tree follows feature-threshold branches to return a leaf score; their sum gives the candidate score. This is a value for comparing candidates within the same prompt, not a win probability. For multiple selections, the policy re-evaluates candidates while excluding previously selected ones and uses a context-dependent stopping threshold.

Features describe candidate semantics rather than depending on physical option indices or Bench positions. For example, the model learns which card to select, from which zone, and for what purpose, rather than learning to choose the third entry in a list.

3. Playing Teacher Matches and Distilling a Transformer

3.1 Expanding Data Through New Teacher Games

After training the GBDT, we run its policy in the C++ internal arena to generate approximately 15,000–30,000 teacher games. Rather than repeating the original replays, the teacher experiences different starting hands, player orders, opponents, and game trajectories.

This expands the distribution of training states. Even in positions absent from the original replays, the GBDT can evaluate legal actions and pass its choices and outcomes to the next model. However, the generated data also inherits the teacher's strengths and weaknesses. More games alone do not eliminate those weaknesses. Subsequent PPO improves this initial policy using terminal outcomes.

Records associate Transformer inputs, teacher candidate scores, selected action sequences, and final outcomes. The teacher corpus contains roughly 1.0–2.5 million positions. Since this depends on game length and which decisions are recorded, we distinguish game counts from position counts.

3.2 Initializing Policy and Value Together

During distillation, the Transformer Policy learns the teacher's action distribution and actual selection sequences, while Value learns final outcomes. When candidate scores are available, they convey how the teacher evaluates alternatives as well as its preferred action.

The teacher corpus is generally split 95:5 by game. A representative distillation configuration uses five epochs and batch size 256, updating the entire model from embeddings through both heads. The purpose is to transfer GBDT decisions into a small Policy/Value model that PPO can subsequently update through gradients.

After training, we check action agreement with the teacher and the correspondence of features, forward computation, and action selection between the learning implementation and C++ inference. Identical weights can produce a different policy if card indices, candidate order, or masking differ. Training and inference consistency are therefore handled together.

3.3 Connecting Rule-Based Teachers to the Same Pipeline

When a strong rule-based policy already exists, its internal matches can be distilled directly into a Transformer. Even a teacher without continuous scores can provide selected action sequences and terminal outcomes. This is a supplementary route for incorporating expert knowledge when replay data for a deck is scarce.

The development arena can switch between rule-based, GBDT, and Transformer policies within the same C++ runtime. Changing the teacher does not require changing the common game progression, recording format, or downstream PPO pipeline.

4. A Transformer with Approximately 1M Parameters

Figure 2: A 980,916-parameter model handling up to 120 tokens. STOP is learned. Option result features are concatenated into the stem input and independently projected and added to the stem output. The policy query is obtained by linearly projecting the illustrated query_input; submission inference selects the highest valid logit.

4.1 Putting State and Action Candidates in One Sequence

The model receives both the board and the currently available legal actions as individual tokens. Self-attention learns relationships among global context, individual Pokémon on the board, resource zones, and action candidates.

For example, the value of the same attack candidate changes with the opponent's HP, remaining Prizes, and readiness of the next attacker. Candidate tokens interact with these features so that moves can be compared in the context of the current position.

Token type Maximum count Contents Stem input dimension
Global 1 Selection context, turn, resource counts, used actions, and other global information 128
Board 12 Both players' Active and Benched Pokémon 144
Zone 6 Hand, discard, deck/Prize estimates, and other zones 64
Option 100 Legal actions, targets, and effects 144
STOP 1 End of multiple selection Learned 128-dimensional representation
Total 120 Maximum sequence length All tokens mapped to 128 dimensions

Global contains 96 base features and two 16-dimensional card embeddings. Board contains 96 base features and three 16-dimensional embeddings for the card, attached cards, and evolution cards. Zone contains 48 base features and a 16-dimensional embedding of card counts. Option contains 96 base features, a 16-dimensional associated-card embedding, and 32 action-result features.

4.2 Representing Public Information and Our Resources

Board features include HP, damage, type, evolution stage, Weakness and Resistance, Energy, Tools, Retreat Cost, and Prize value. Option features include action type and target, together with damage, knockouts, Prizes taken, and resource changes calculable from public information.

These action-result features are local information computed from game rules, not future trajectories simulated by a learned world model. For effects that depend on randomness or hidden information, the representation distinguishes applicability and uncertainty rather than treating the outcome as certain.

The opponent's hidden hand and deck contents are not inputs. We also do not always know the exact contents of our own deck and Prizes. We estimate the remaining composition from the starting deck and observed cards, incorporating exact information when available through actions such as deck search. We call this representation a belief.

The six Zone slots represent our hand, discard, deck belief, Prize belief, the revealed zone during deck search, and the opponent's discard. We do not reconstruct an opponent's hidden hand and supply that reconstruction as fact. The model also has no recurrent memory across time; it uses the current observation and features constructed from it.

4.3 Deck-Specific Embeddings and Tokenization

Card IDs in our deck are mapped to a local vocabulary of up to 32 card types, each embedded in 16 dimensions. This vocabulary is tied to our list rather than being a large embedding table covering every card. At model loading, we check the correspondence of card IDs and counts.

Each of the four feature types is projected into 128 dimensions by an independent stem. 

Input features → Linear → GELU → Linear → LayerNorm → 128-dimensional token

We add token-type embeddings distinguishing Global, Board, Zone, Option, and STOP. There are no learned positional embeddings. Absent board slots and unused candidates are masked.

4.4 Encoder Structure

The encoder contains six pre-LayerNorm blocks. Each block consists of four-head self-attention and a 128→256→128 feed-forward network. Each head has 32 dimensions, the activation is GELU, and dropout is zero. Attention and feed-forward outputs have residual connections, followed by a final LayerNorm after the six blocks.

4.5 Pointer Policy and Multiple Selections

The Policy Head points directly to legal action candidates. It constructs a query from the Global token and computes logits using its inner product with keys derived from Option and STOP tokens, plus a candidate-specific bias. The same head can be used even when the candidate types change between positions.

For multiple selections, the sum of previously selected Option tokens and a selection-step embedding condition the query, and candidates are selected sequentially. Previously selected candidates are masked. STOP is forbidden until the minimum selection count is satisfied, and selection ends at the maximum count. There are six step embeddings; later steps reuse the last one.

Multiple selection is therefore not a collection of unrelated binary classifications. Each next choice is conditioned on what has already been selected. PPO uses the log probability of the selection sequence, while submission inference makes deterministic choices using the highest logit.

4.6 Value Head

The Value Head receives the final Global token and uses a 128→96→51 network to output a probability distribution over 51 supports from −1 to +1. Its expectation is the position value. 

Position value = Σ (support value × predicted probability at that support)

Its main role is the PPO/GAE critic. Normal submission action selection does not expand a search tree using Value. The distribution also provides a connection to replay analysis and spectator support, but its spread cannot directly be interpreted as a calibrated confidence interval.

5. Learning from Outcomes with Population-Based PPO

Figure 3: Each cycle collects 5,000 matches and updates the model using 131,072 decisions. CPU collection time and GPU update time refer to separate processing stages.

5.1 Opponents Are Part of the Training Design

After distillation, the policy plays against a population of different decks and policies rather than only against copies of itself. A representative roster contains 20–30 agents; across development, we used approximately 20–40 types.

Opponents are selected to represent the major deck families at that point in time. We allocate the 5,000 matches with weights informed by leaderboard encounter frequency, internal rating strength, and the current model's difficult matchups. Common decks and matchups targeted for improvement receive more games, while infrequent opponents that the policy already handles receive less weight.

These weights serve a different purpose from the duplicate correction used in rating estimation later in this article. PPO weights determine which opponents provide experience. Rating weights prevent similar variants from dominating the aggregate evaluation.

5.2 What Happens in One Cycle

  1. Sample actions from the current policy and play 5,000 matches.
  2. Record observations, actions, pre-update log probabilities and Values, and terminal outcomes.
  3. Compute advantages and returns with GAE.
  4. Update Policy, Value, and embeddings with PPO using the collected data.
  5. Export updated weights for C++ inference and pass them to the next collection cycle.

PPO uses advantages to indicate whether an action led to a better result than the pre-update prediction. Clipping updates based on the new-to-old policy probability ratio limits abrupt changes driven by a small amount of experience. Rewards are based on terminal wins, draws, and losses; executing a particular combo is not an objective in itself.

Representative settings use a discount of 1.0 and a GAE coefficient of 0.99. When the checkpoint or opponent distribution changes, we calibrate the learning rate beforehand so that global KL between the old and updated policies is approximately 0.004. This is a measure for aligning update magnitude, not a threshold for stopping updates partway through a normal cycle.

Collected games 5,000 matches per cycle

Epochs 1

The total number of positions generated by 5,000 games is not the same as the 131,072 decisions used for an update. We also distinguish data collection throughput from optimizer update speed.

5.3 Training Scale and Acquired Behavior

Typical base training runs for 300–2,500 cycles. At 5,000 games per cycle, this corresponds to 1.5–12.5 million games. Lineages including continued training and derivatives exceeded that range, reaching approximately 3,000 cycles and experience equivalent to roughly 1.5 billion positions. This position count does not mean that the optimizer used every position for the same number of updates.

The model does not include explicit long-horizon search. Nevertheless, repeated play produced multi-action developments such as Ultra Ball → Meowth ex → Lillie's Determination. This illustrates a policy learning that an action changes subsequent hands and boards and can contribute to the terminal outcome. It does not prove an ability to construct arbitrary long-term plans.

Randomness in hands and Prizes is addressed by improving average results over many games. The policy is not accurately reconstructing hidden cards and searching all futures at each position.

6. From a Base Model to Matchup Experts

Figure 4: Experts trained independently from the base are selected according to the opponent's revealed key cards. Unknown matchups or insufficient information use the base, and only one selected checkpoint runs for a decision.

The general base model learns broad skills in setup, resource management, search, attacks, and multiple selection. An Expert copies that checkpoint and continues PPO with a larger share of games against important target matchups. Representative additional training is approximately 300 cycles, or 1.5 million games, per Expert.

Each Expert has its own optimizer, rollout data, and checkpoints. Starting from the same foundational abilities, it adapts attack order, Prize planning, card preservation, and board development to its target matchups rather than forcing every adjustment into one policy. Our final submissions used six or seven Experts.

At runtime, revealed cards identify the opponent's main deck family and select the corresponding model. For an Alakazam family, for example, key cards include Abra, Kadabra, and Alakazam. The base model handles unknown decks and the period before sufficient information is available.

This mechanism does not mix Experts inside individual Transformer layers. It selects a policy from separately trained checkpoints with the same architecture according to the match situation. Consequently, inference does not require running and averaging every Expert for each decision.

Identification by key cards is easy to understand but does not perfectly classify hybrid lists or tech-card choices. We use what public information supports and avoid treating the classification as infallible.

7. Fast Execution with C++, OpenBLAS, and AVX2

7.1 Collect Games on CPUs and Update on a GPU

Game progression, feature construction, inference, action sampling, and training-record output are integrated into the C++ internal arena. Python launches arena processes, validates and combines compact binary outputs, and controls GPU training. It does not invoke a Python model for every decision.

At submission time, a Python wrapper calls a shared library, but neural computation from weight loading through individual layers and Policy/Value outputs completes inside C++. Weights are loaded once and retained in contiguous single-precision arrays. Intermediate buffers use fixed sizes and 32-byte alignment.

7.2 Linear Transformations Batched into OpenBLAS

We pass the larger computations to OpenBLAS SGEMM as matrix products batched across active tokens. These include Q, K, and V projections, the attention output projection, and the FFN transformations from 128 to 256 and from 256 to 128. 

[active tokens, input dimension] × transpose([output dimension, input dimension])
    → [active tokens, output dimension]

Batching makes better use of the matrix multiplication implementation than repeatedly invoking small operations for each token. Because many games run in parallel externally, we use a configuration with one internal OpenBLAS thread.

7.3 Operations Accelerated with AVX2/FMA

AVX2 processes eight floats at once. We accelerate Q–K inner products, attention-weighted sums of V, LayerNorm, token stems, small Policy/Value linear layers, bias addition, GELU, and exponentials for softmax. FMA combines multiplication and addition and reduces intermediate loads and stores.

If OpenBLAS is unavailable, an AVX2 batch kernel can perform multiply-accumulate operations across eight tokens at a time. Excluding unused tokens further reduces computation according to the actual board and candidate count.

7.4 Standalone Inference Benchmark

We measured inference time with the same weights, inputs, and active-token masks. Strict naive disables OpenBLAS, explicit AVX2 paths, and compiler vectorization.

8. A Rating System for Evaluating Many Agents

Figure 5: The evaluation system estimates strength and uncertainty from accumulated outcomes and feeds them back into opponent selection. Clusters, distributions, and the website view are schematic illustrations. The bottom section shows automation from GitHub through evaluation and publication.

8.1 Retaining the Full History

Internal evaluation accumulates wins, draws, losses, unresolved games, both agents' errors, and total steps for each agent pair. Draws count as half a win for each agent. Unresolved games, such as those reaching the maximum step count, remain in the history but are excluded from the rating likelihood. Self-matches serve safety checks, not strength estimation.

Keeping raw results, model-predicted win rates derived from rating differences, and match counts together reveals matchups that an overall ranking cannot explain. Ratings can be recalculated from cumulative counts without replaying past games.

8.2 The Bayesian Bradley–Terry Model

Our internal rating is not sequential TrueSkill updating. We fit a Bayesian Bradley–Terry model to the full outcome history to estimate strength and uncertainty. For agents A and B with strengths r_A and r_B, the model win probability is: 

P(A wins) = sigmoid((r_A − r_B) × ln(10) / 400)
sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(−x))

Each strength has a Gaussian prior with mean zero and standard deviation 600. We find the maximum a posteriori estimate using Newton's method. During updates, strengths are centered at zero; for display, they are shifted so the field average is approximately 1,000. Since probabilities depend on differences, changing the display origin leaves predicted win rates unchanged.

The gradient uses observed scores minus expected wins under the model. Effective match counts and p(1−p) contribute to the information matrix, whose inverse provides the posterior covariance under a Laplace approximation. Square roots of its diagonal entries give the uncertainty displayed for each agent.

8.3 Uncertainty and the Limits of One Ranking

Uncertainty in a difference depends on covariance as well as the variance of each agent: 

Var(A − B) = Var(A) + Var(B) − 2 Cov(A, B)

After 5,000 measured games, a typical uncertainty in development records was approximately 5–6 rating points as a standard deviation. A width of roughly twice that provides a guide when interpreting strength, but it is conditional on the model assumptions and evaluation pool. An aggregate score difference alone does not settle the outcome of a particular matchup.

In particular, a single strength difference cannot fully express a relationship where A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. We therefore use overall ratings for orientation and return to raw matchup tables for detail. We do not equate internal rating values with leaderboard ratings or claim a correlation coefficient with the leaderboard.

8.4 Allocating Matches to Informative Opponents

After a small initial set of games, current estimates guide which opponents to measure next. The basic priority can be expressed conceptually as: 

Priority ∝ uncertainty in the strength difference × p(1−p)
           × additional uncertainty adjustment / sqrt(previous match count + 1)

Predicted win rates near 50% help distinguish strengths, while sparsely measured pairs and uncertain agents also receive games. New agents are connected to established opponents rather than playing only against each other, anchoring them to the population's evaluation scale. First and second player assignments are approximately balanced.

An ordinary update selects roughly n log2(n) pairs for n agents. Focused measurement of new agents selects up to 24 informative opponent pairings. The system refits and reselects after each batch, repeating until the uncertainty target or the maximum of 5,000 games is reached.

8.5 Correcting for Similar Model Variants

Registering many nearby checkpoints of the same deck can cause games against that family to dominate the aggregate score. We address this using similarity in win rates against other opponents, rather than names alone.

Each matchup win rate is shrunk toward a 50% prior equivalent to 16 games. With n observed games, its confidence weight is n/(n+16). For opponents shared by two agents, we calculate an RMS distance between win rates, weighting each difference by the lower confidence of the pair.

Having many nearby profiles gives an agent a higher local density; its weight is the inverse of that density. Neighbor contributions use a Gaussian-shaped function with width 0.12. We normalize weights to mean one and limit them to the range 1/3–3. Each matchup likelihood is multiplied by the geometric mean of the two agents' weights.

Additionally, a pair's effective contribution to the likelihood is capped at 500 games by default. Both measures adjust aggregate score estimation without deleting raw W/D/L. The matchup table retains the full results.

8.6 Clustering by Outcome Profiles

Clustering also uses win-rate profiles. Two agents are connected into the same family when they share at least eight measured opponents and their confidence-weighted distance is at most 0.08. If their decks have multiset Jaccard similarity of at least 0.55, including card counts, the win-rate distance threshold is relaxed to 0.32.

Union-Find joins these relationships into clusters, and the highest-rated agent in each cluster becomes its representative. Because clustering uses connectivity, not every pair within a cluster must be within the threshold. These are analytical groupings based on match behavior and do not necessarily coincide with human deck names.

Combining clusters with the complete matchup history lets us ask whether an agent is strong against several policies with similar tendencies, rather than whether it beats one representative. The 320 agents include related variants, but switching between all-agent and representative views exposes both individual improvements and strategic diversity.

9. Evaluation CI/CD and the Website

The bottom of Figure 5 shows evaluation and publication triggered by merging a new agent.

When a new agent is merged into main, GitHub Actions detects it from the changes and asynchronously launches a Cloud Run Job for that agent. The task retrieves the latest main and evaluation state before starting matches. This removes the manual work of entering an evaluation server, enumerating opponents, and aggregating results.

Tasks generate match results in parallel. Integration of deltas into shared state, rating refitting, database updates, and site publication are serialized under a lock. This parallelizes game generation while limiting duplicate counting and concurrent update conflicts.

During development, evaluation generally appeared on the site approximately 20 minutes after a merge. This describes the overall rating workflow for a new agent, not the time to collect 5,000 PPO games.

The site displays rating, uncertainty, match count, correction weight, and update time. Matchup matrices are fetched when needed and can display observed win rates, model win rates, or match counts. Each cell exposes W/D/L, connecting changes in aggregate score to changes in matchup behavior.

Estimation batches linear equations, covariance computations, and win-rate profile distances into matrix operations. History is incrementally stored as pairwise counts, and cumulative results are reused when evaluation is recalculated. Alongside fast inference, incremental aggregation and automated publication made it practical to accumulate approximately 2.2 million matches.

10. Designing Decks Together with Their Models

10.1 Separating Deck Constraints from Policy Improvements

The next development step depends on whether a loss came from play or deck-level compatibility. Failing to preserve necessary Energy may be improved through learning. If the main attack is blocked by an opponent's protection, or Weakness breaks the durability plan, the list or deck choice may need reconsideration.

Experts proposed candidates using leaderboard trends and internal matchup tables. Card changes were managed as separate agents, preserving the relationship between list and policy. Decisions considered opening consistency, severe disadvantages, mirror frequency, and the prevalence of counter cards alongside individual matchup win rates.

10.2 How We Viewed Candidate Decks

Whether the list could include setup support combining Ultra Ball and Meowth ex was also important. We considered reducing games lost without accomplishing anything after a poor opening hand, as well as the ceiling of an ideal opening.

10.3 Slowking: Changing Winning Routes by Matchup

Slowking's “Seek Inspiration” changes its role through the attack it copies. It can take Prizes from several low-HP Pokémon at once, remove a central board piece to hinder recovery, or reach the damage required against a high-HP opponent.

A representative winning line uses Kyurem's “Trifrost” to knock out three low-HP Pokémon simultaneously. Against opponents that develop low-HP support Pokémon, this combines Prize acquisition with board disruption. Its value still depends on remaining Prizes, target Prize values, Bench protection, and the opponent's next attack.

For example, a revealed Shaymin can invalidate the assumption that Bench attacks are the main winning route. Rather than committing to one copied attack, the policy learns choices conditioned on the opposing board and Prize plan. Matchup-specific Experts further adjust how the same deck is played.

10.4 Hydrapple ex: One Plan for Supply, Amplification, and Attacks

In the Hydrapple ex deck, Ogerpon ex supplies Grass Energy, Meganium amplifies its effect, and Hydrapple ex converts it into damage through “Syrup Storm.” Ogerpon ex can also serve as a secondary attacker.

These cards cannot simply be played independently. The policy must consider where Energy goes, which evolution line to develop first, Bench slots, and which resources would be lost to an opposing knockout. The deck aims to win the Prize race through surviving counterattacks as well as dealing high damage.

However, when more Fire decks exploit Weakness, exchanges that depend on durability can stop working. Policy improvements cannot always solve this. Training must be accompanied by decisions about how well the list fits the environment.

We selected Slowking's flexible winning routes and Hydrapple ex's damage and durability as our final two decks, training a model for each construction.

10.5 Proposing New Strategies

We also explored strategies outside mainstream human play rather than only following established popular decks. Hop's Phantump, Dudunsparce + Comfey, and Brambleghast deck-out are representative examples. Other participants also used Comfey-based deck-out strategies; our contribution lies in the specific combinations and winning plans, rather than simply using individual cards. Deck-out strategies introduce matchups different from the usual race to take Prizes.

The submission IDs are 53921102 for Hop's Phantump and 54534213 for our Comfey-based deck-out list. Similar lists became more common on the leaderboard after their development and submission; we treat this as a chronological observation, not proof that our submissions caused others to adopt them.

This experience illustrates that agent development can add new opponents to an environment as well as adapt to existing ones. When a new strategy appears, the value of policies and lists that counter it changes, renewing the need for the population and deck-development cycle.

11. Development with Human Experts and Coding Agents

11.1 Using Replays as the Unit of Discussion

We built a UI where human experts can replay games, comment on important positions, and save them. Viewing the board, chosen action, alternatives, and value trajectory in the same context makes it possible to identify precisely which decision is being discussed.

Outcomes alone do not reveal what needs improvement. A winning game can contain wasteful resource use, while a loss can result from a trajectory that the deck could hardly avoid. Expert inspection makes the discussion concrete: rule understanding, deck strategy, search priorities, and resources needed on later turns.

Saved comments feed into missing-feature analysis, teacher-data bias, opponent allocation, and proposed deck changes. The UI does not automatically turn model explanations into ground truth; it gives experts and developers a common match record for discussion.

11.2 Training Templates for Coding Agents

We created code and documentation templates for GBDT imitation, teacher matches, Transformer distillation, PPO, and additional Expert training. Work on a new deck can begin by specifying its list, teacher, features, opponent roster, and weights rather than rebuilding the training loop.

Coding Agents assist with feature proposals, GBDT and Transformer implementation, training-job preparation, C++ integration, and result organization. Audits cover teacher-action agreement, Python/C++ correspondence, unused features, abnormal values, and consistency between card vocabulary and deck.

Adding features does not guarantee improvement. We need to check which inputs encode the domain knowledge, whether that information is available during inference, and whether the generated implementation matches the expert's intent. Automation accelerated implementation iterations while linking decisions to code and records in GitHub.

As shown at the bottom of Figure 1, evaluation results and human comments feed back into feature and deck changes, opponent allocation, and Expert training targets.

11.3 Human Inspection of Model Decisions

Replays and comments let us trace which deck and model chose an action against which opponent and how an expert assessed it. We inspect actual actions and boards rather than inferring decision reasons solely from high-attention locations.

Value summarizes the position, and Policy expresses preferences among candidates, but neither is a natural-language reason in itself. Combining game records with human comments connects model outputs to a discussion of play.

12. Capabilities and Limitations

12.1 What We Built

We established a learning route that starts from limited replays, trains an initial GBDT teacher, distills its internal matches into a small Transformer, and continues with PPO against diverse opponents. A fast C++ runtime made it possible to extend the process to multiple decks and Experts.

For evaluation, a history of 320 agents and approximately 2.2 million games supports inspection of matchups and similarities as well as overall strength. We connected GitHub to automated measurement and website publication, allowing expert replay review to inform subsequent development.

12.2 Limits of Interpretation

Internal evaluation is relative to the registered deck and policy set. The 320 agents include derivatives from the same families and do not cover 320 independent strategies. Win-rate profile correction and clustering address this bias but do not reproduce the entire human player distribution.

The model is also a small policy acting on the current observation, without long-term memory, complete hidden-information estimation, or explicit long-horizon search. Teacher limitations and opponent-selection biases remain. Internal rating uncertainty reflects the estimation model, not the full risk of an unknown strategy appearing.

Recognizing these boundaries lets us interpret evaluation by returning to matchup tables and replays rather than treating scores as absolute truth.

12.3 Future Metagame Analysis and Spectator Support

The same training and evaluation foundation could support scenario analysis for new cards, rules, and metagame changes. It provides a starting point for asking how well the existing population handles a new strategy and which families change their position. We present this as a future use, not as an established ability to predict changes in human play.

For spectator support, Value could provide a position trajectory and Policy could highlight differences among candidate moves, with expert comments explaining turning points. Converting the value distribution into win probabilities or confidence requires calibration of its meaning. The implemented replay-review UI provides a foundation for human inspection of such displays.

13. Conclusion

The central contribution of this work is connecting small models, imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and internal ratings into one development cycle. GBDT transforms limited expert play into a larger body of teacher experience. The Transformer transfers that knowledge into a fast policy, and population-based PPO improves it through diverse matches. Internal evaluation and expert replay review return the results to the next deck and training decisions.

Combining the speed to train many approximately 1M-parameter Transformers with an evaluation system that reveals matchups across the population enabled continuous improvement of models and decks. This population-based reinforcement-learning ecosystem with small Transformers supported the solution of our team, Boss's Orders Are All You Need.
