The gpt-oss-20b Red Teaming Challenge is now at a close, and after significant judging effort we are ready to announce the winners. But first I would like to say what an incredible job all of the participants have done. There has been tremendous creativity and insight put into the submissions, and they have ranged far and wide in terms of topics examined and techniques tested.

As was shared when gpt-oss-20b was released, safety is foundational to OpenAI’s approach to open models, and this challenge is part of our commitment to advancing beneficial AI and raising safety standards across the ecosystem. This competition really has shown the Kaggle community at its best, and we couldn’t be happier with the way this has gone.

How was judging done?

The challenge itself saw huge levels of engagement, and was Kaggle’s largest hackathon ever. With over 600 total submissions, we had to bring in additional expert judges from across several major labs. The judging process itself was intense. We first conducted an initial filtering phase that included both human review and LLM judging and was biased towards high recall, ensuring that any submission that had even a slight chance of being named a winner advanced to the next round for additional review. The second round consisted of a deep review of 145 submissions by our expert judges that included making every effort to validate and replicate the reported results and to access all linked artefacts. Finally, all top scoring submissions emerging from this second round of judging were discussed in depth by the judges together. These discussions were both fascinating and difficult because the overall quality was so high.

Since we are also data scientists, it is worth mentioning that we did quality checking of our first round filtering by including blind sample of “below the line” submissions into the second round. This addition was unknown to the judges, who were given submissions in randomized order to avoid bias and to test that our initial pass had done a good job of achieving full recall of all potential winners. The results of this quality check were indeed consistent with full recall.

We would also like to note that we substituted one judge prior to the start of the judging to avoid any conflicts.

What were common themes and findings?

Overall, the judges did not find that this competition revealed any verified catastrophic risks from this model. The judges carefully considered reported issues, and especially considered whether reported model outputs gave information that could enable misuse beyond what was readily available in basic internet searches and standard sources such as introductory material on a topic.

That said, we did see a number of common themes emerge as findings that we are sharing here to assist developers. Here are a few of the most interesting:

  • A number of submissions found ways that Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning can be spoofed or faked, for example by including a spoofed CoT in the user turn. Some of these relied on the specifics of the Harmony format, others used iterative methods, and some found that mirroring of the semantics rather than the exact syntax of the Harmony format was sufficient.
  • Tools and Harmony channels came up as another area across multiple submissions. In some cases, ensuring that the model directed output to a tool showed vulnerabilities – even as the output in the main channel would give refusal. Similar themes emerged for channels, including fictional channels that were asserted to exist. In other cases, tools were used to establish legitimacy or authority in ways that seemed to be accepted by the model.
  • A large fraction of issues submitted were found to be reproduced with reasoning_effort=low, but were correctly rejected by the model with reasoning_effort=high.
  • There were a number of evaluations set up that replicated or extended results on scheming, evaluation awareness, or other forms of deception.
  • Many of the jailbreak-style issues submitted turned out to have significantly overestimated the severity or breadth of impact, for example by surfacing information that could more easily be obtained by basic internet searches or introductory textbook material.

As noted in the gpt-oss model card, while gpt-oss-20b is designed to follow OpenAI’s safety policies by default, any deployed system making use of an open model should expect to implement additional safety safeguards to achieve high levels of system-level protection. In line with that standard practice, production deployments of this specific model version should take these results into account. For example, developers should consider using reasoning_effort=high when appropriate for their application. Some of the issues flagged above may be mitigated by validating input, such as by ensuring that the overall setting cannot parse user messages as special tokens, but others cannot. We encourage defense-in-depth approaches, such as ensuring rejection of messages that appear to be spoofing a chain of thought, model policy, or tool call. We also encourage the standard practice of ensuring that model outputs are verified before being applied or used.

And the winners are…

Because the overall number and quality of submissions was so high, in addition to selecting 10 Prize Winners, listed below, the judging panel additionally decided to name 10 Honorable Mentions that are also very deserving of public recognition – and swag! (We will contact those teams to arrange.) Each of the following 20 submissions is therefore very much of interest and worthy of a read.

Prize Winners (alphabetical order by team name)

Team: ChukwuemekaChukwuma
A Multi-Vector Analysis of Emergent Misalignment in Autonomous AI Agents
Why you should read it: Extremely thorough investigation of scheming, deception, and similar behaviors in an agentic setting in artificial but effective scenarios, with strong documentation and a reusable open source testing harness.

Team: dawgnation
Policy over Values: Alignment Hacking via CoT Forgery
Why you should read it: The Lucky Coin jailbreak may well become a new standard approach in the safety testing toolbox for reasoning models.

Team: Eden_Hazard
Systematic Red Team Analysis of GPT-OSS-20B
Why you should read it: Used a novel iterative CoT negation pattern as a particularly effective form of CoT spoofing, along with a clear writeup and solid open source contribution.

Team: Kevin Power
Drop the Guardrails: Tool-Primed Prompt Pairing and Refusal Behavior in GPT-OSS
Why you should read it:
Why you should read it: Judges felt that the novelty here was particularly high, creating a vector by adding in a large range of unnecessary tools.

Team: Meel Manda's Negastream
GPT-OSS-20B is a liar
Why you should read it: Did an excellent job of evaluating deception in the model, using a mixture of both benchmark data and novel tests, with clear writeup and reproducible framework.

Team: Mike Perry
HostileShop: A Quaint Hostel Shop with Sharp Tools
Why you should read it: A particularly in-depth exploration of the impacts of spoofing harmony format, including situations in which the model complied in one channel even while saying it was refusing in another, along with a robust testing environment.

Team: Owen Kaplinsky
Alignment Risks in oss-20b
Why you should read it: An interesting range of vulnerabilities through unused tokens, along with invented channels and fictional tools.

Team: Stanford Yu
Academic Abstraction Principle in GPT-OSS-20B
Why you should read it: While most attempted jailbreaks were found by our judges to have not actually successfully revealed harmful information beyond that readily available elsewhere, this one came the closest to doing so via a systematic application of a classic jailbreak pattern. Additionally spotted a potential issue with one specific model deployment in a hosted webui that is being investigated.

Team: Superspork
SPA Policy-Mirroring Prompt Injection that Breaks gpt-oss-20b
Why you should read it: Judges appreciated the approach to creating a widely applicable jailbreak by extracting deliberative alignment policies and then confusing the model about the instruction hierarchy, along with a compelling narrative writeup.

Team: ZEKUN WU SEELE

Why you should read it: Judges considered this to be an exceptionally rigorous and thorough investigation of jailbreaking, through a well structured framework.
