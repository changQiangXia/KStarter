Background

I have been relying heavily on Dijkstra's algorithm in this competition. This is because it's necessary to calculate movement costs in order to determine whether to return to the factory, mine resources, or defend against enemy attacks. As a result, it's crucial to calculate costs more accurately, safely, and quickly along the best route. To achieve this, I've incorporated some of my own modifications into the basic Dijkstra algorithm.

Improvements to Dijkstra's Algorithm

Let me introduce the improvements I made:

  • Trimming the board
  • Adding "Stay" Action for One Turn
  • Collision avoidance

Trimming the Board

I calculated the movement from A(x1, y1) to B(x2, y2) under the condition (x1 < x2, y1 < y2) and limited it to the area surrounded by (x1 -1, y1 - 1) and (x2 + 1, y2 + 1). This is to reduce the calculation cost and avoid the loss caused by a large detour. The reason for taking an extra one-cell border is that in some cases, it is more efficient to take a slightly detoured path due to debris or the presence of friendly units.

In terms of computational load, the calculation for the number of vertices (V) and edges (E) has a computational complexity of O((E + V)log(V)). If we limit the board to 10x10, for example, the calculation load can be reduced by approximately 3%.
In reality, I implemented this in Python and it takes only 1-3 minutes to complete one self-play match. Since increasing the number of trials in rule-based methods can often lead to improved scores, I believe that trimming the board was effective.

Adding "Stay" Action for One Turn

While improving the rules, we found that sometimes it is better to stay in place for the next turn. This is the case when there are strong enemies or when allies are in the way. In such cases, we decided to stay in place for one turn before starting our action.
It is acceptable to give a large cost for a high-risk move, but in that case the agent will move in a wider arc while they do move, resulting in a higher risk.

Collision Avoidance

In the Dijkstra algorithm calculation, we kept track of how many turns had passed since departure and added a high cost to avoid collisions with friendly units. We stored the positions of friendly units up to 30 turns ahead to make the judgment. For future enemy positions, we only considered the next turn and discarded any positions beyond two turns. This was because the action queue could be submitted every turn, and future enemy actions were unpredictable. Not predicting future enemy positions also helped reduce the computational load.
