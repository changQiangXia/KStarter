Game Summary

The LuxAI 2 challenge is a 2-player zero-sum game where both players compete to terraform Mars by building the most lichen in 1000 time steps. They start with a few factories and enough resources to build a few units. During the game, the units can collect two types of resources—ore for building new units, ice for keeping the factories alive and watering lichen. Lichen, besides counting for the score at the end of the game, also generates power that units need to perform actions. In general, power was a very scarce resource in this game, so efficient power usage was essential.

There are two types of units: a light unit which is more power-efficient, and a heavy unit able to dig more per turn and therefore has higher resource utilization. Units execute actions and require power to perform these actions.

If two units collide, the stronger one survives. If they are the same type and one unit is stationary, that one is lost. If they are both moving, the unit with the most power survives. When your units collide with each other, the same rules apply. Therefore avoiding collisions between your units is extremely important. This wasn't easy because you control your unit not by sending its next action but by updating an action queue which can easily consist of 50 actions. One could update the action queue for every unit at every step, but this is quite costly, as it costs power every time you update the queue.

Final design

I switched my design around a few times during the competition, but in the end, I ended up with the following design.

Factories decide on strategic focus

Each unit is assigned to the factory that spawns them, and the factory will decide, based on its current resources, the game time step, and the goals that it already scheduled, what currently is the highest priority strategy for its units to focus on. Some examples of these strategies are to increase lichen, collect ore, attack the opponent or defend a lichen tile. Based on these strategies, an attempt is made to schedule the most suitable unit for this task. The unit selection picks the unit that, at a glance, appears to have the highest value per step (explained in more detail in the get_best_case_value_per_step section). If none of the factory's units can be scheduled to aid in this strategy, an attempt will be made to schedule a unit on the next highest priority strategy. After a factory schedules a unit, the factory recalculates the priorities of the strategies again to decide where to schedule the next unit. This loop continues until all units are scheduled, or no unit can be scheduled on any relevant strategy.

Units carry out strategic focus with goals

I ended up with 11 different goals for units. These goals either directly contribute to fulfilling a strategy or help the unit survive( e.g. fleeing from opponents). For example, these goals include collecting ice at a specific coordinate (x, y), clearing out rubble at a specific coordinate or destroying a specific opponent's lichen tile. The abstract base class of these goals have the following methods: get_best_case_value_per_step, generate_action_plan and get_value_per_step_of_action_plan.

get_best_case_value_per_step

To evaluate goals, I used the following simple formula:
value_per_step = (power_benefit – power_cost) / nr_steps

This method, at a glance, estimates this formula for the best-case scenario of scheduling a unit on a goal without computing an exact action plan. To estimate the power benefit of each goal, I created a formulate which contained some parameters I could tweak. Then to decide on the parameters, I tuned them based on the performance on the leaderboard.

To quickly compute the best-case scenario, we assume that everything is optimal. E.g. to collect ice for a unit, we would calculate the distance it would take to go there without being obstructed by any unit, there to be no rubble on the way, and the unit max number of digging the ice out is based on the state to be day meaning that power is generated each step and then goes back to the factory to return the power.

These best-case values for goals are used to select the most suitable unit for a strategy. E.g. if we want to increase ore to build units, all units belonging to a factory that are not scheduled on a goal yet, are asked to compute their best-case values of mining ore on the ore tiles around them. The unit with the best value is selected, and for this unit, we will carry out the expensive method, generate_action_plan.

generate_action_plan

To get from a goal to an action plan, a specific set of actions, you will need some type of planning algorithm. I used A* as the basis. A* is an algorithm similar to the more famous Dijkstra's algorithm but can better prioritize the most promising node if you can set accurate lower bounds on the remaining cost to reach the endpoint. In a grid world, as in this game, a reasonable lower bound can be obtained based on the distance to the final node. Goals are broken down into some subgoals (e.g. move to a factory and pick up power, collect ice and transfer ice to the factory) to reduce the planning horizon, which improves computational performance significantly. However, the found solution can be slightly worse than the optimal solution.

Another crucial task to solve is ensuring that your units don't collide. This is especially hard in this game, as we are not planning one step at a time per unit, but we update action queues containing multiple actions. For our planning algorithm, this means we must also consider other units' action plans. In the end, I used a reservation table for this. Which works in a first come, first serve manner. The first unit to be scheduled will reserve the time coordinates (x, y and t=time) corresponding to its action plan. Then any future unit planning will not be allowed to be on those coordinates and will generate its action plans accordingly.

get_value_per_step_of_action_plan

Given that we now have the exact action plan of the unit, we can calculate the value per step of this plan precisely. It was essential to do this, as in the middle game, often there are more units than worthwhile goals to carry out. If you always schedule your units on goals, you end up using too much power, and then your units will eventually have too little power left for the most important goals. Therefore, goals with negative values are never scheduled.

Pitfalls

All units performing the same strategy

At the start, every unit thought for themselves what the most worthwhile goal was, and they were not assigned to a factory. Using a sum-assignment problem solver, units were assigned to specific goals. This has one big drawback. Often all units ended up performing the same goal. E.g. if the parameters of ice mining were slightly more favourable than ore mining and there were many ice resources around the factory, the units would only collect ice, neglecting the ore resources completely. Then I would end up with a lot of ice but no ore to build new units. Similarly, if ore mining was slightly more favourable, I might end up with many units but no ice and, therefore, no lichen to generate power for these units.

Therefore, some self-balancing rules were required in the planning algorithm. I ended up scheduling units to perform goals based on their factories' needs. After the factory schedules a unit on a goal, it recomputes its most important strategies using the last scheduled unit's goal. For example, suppose the factory scheduled the last unit to collect ore. In that case, it will soon build new units, so it will now prioritize the strategy of generating power for these future units.

Optimal goal resolution

The biggest pitfall I spent a lot of time on at the start was attempting to compute the perfect joint plan at every step. I was using conflict-based planning for this. The idea of this algorithm is that first, every unit comes up independently with its action plan to complete its goal. After this, we validate whether the joint plan is valid, i.e. does it not contain any collisions? If any units' paths collide, we split the problem into two sub-problems, one where the first unit is granted the time coordinate and one where he isn't. Then, in my implementation, I would reconsider the best distribution of goals given this restriction (if a unit is not granted its optimal path, it might be better to have the unit perform a similar goal on an unblocked path). Then the unit that was not granted its optimal path will generate a new action plan. If this is invalid again, we split the problem in two another time. In a branch-and-bound style, we focus on sub-problems whose optimal value is most promising and repeat this process until a node is found with the best-case joint value and without collisions.

At the start, this worked out well and created joint action plans which were always optimal. But as I started to build more efficient economies and my unit size increased, collisions increased, and no collision-free joint plans were found in time. To solve this, I spent significant time optimizing the code to generate action plans under the time limit. But it appeared impossible to get this to work. Therefore, in the end, I switched to using a reservation table, which, although might not grant the perfect solution, is magnitudes faster and allowed me to focus computational resources and, more importantly, my available programming time for this project on more important parts.

Under-utilizing logging

I underestimated the complexity of building a bot to control 100+ units. At the start, when a bug popped up, I figured out the problem using the debugger and carried on. But there were two big disadvantages in mainly using the debugger vs logging. One was that matches often took more than 20 minutes, so replicating this state in debug mode was time-consuming. Also, there were some recurring problems which are harder to detect in debug mode. For example, I had some situations where some units were scheduled on a goal, but then another unit got scheduled on the same goal. The original unit would be unscheduled but would later schedule themselves on this goal. Detecting this takes a decent amount of effort using a debugger, especially when other units are scheduled in between as well. But this is very easy to detect by logging every scheduling and unscheduling decision.

More parameter optimization and better evaluation

The best way to know if your bot is improving is by comparing it against opponents on the leaderboard. Therefore it is essential to make good use of your five daily submissions. Unfortunately, I focused too much on adding in all the functionality needed for the bot, planning to focus on optimizing the parameters at the end. But in the end, I realized there was a lot more I wanted to test than the number of uploads I had left. Looking back, I should have started using all my uploads from the start, as it takes little effort to tune a few parameters each day.

To make the best use of these uploads, it helps to evaluate them as accurately as possible. Kaggle's rating system jumps around a lot, especially before the final submission date. For leaderboard reasons, this might be optimal, but for accurate performance evaluation in a day of games, there might be better options. I haven't found much information on the Kaggle rating system, but I believe it works similarly to the Elo and Glicko rating systems. In these systems, the impact of recent games is more important than that of old games. This makes a lot of sense for the original domains of these methods, sports. But for static bots, you can be more accurate by using a method that assumes a stationary level of the competitor.

Improvements if I had the time

Changing the goal-scheduling method

During the competition, I set up my scheduling in two different ways, both with their shortcomings. When units schedule purely by themselves, they might end up performing goals irrelevant to the factory. On the other hand, when factories focus only on the most critical strategy, this might lead to poorly accomplishing the most important strategy instead of efficiently accomplishing the second most important strategy. I would have wanted to combine the two methods by multiplying the goal's value by the strategy's importance. The importance of the strategy should then be a number whose average value is approximately 1.

More focus on attack/defence goals

Initially, I spent most of my time getting the planning algorithms to work correctly, quickly enough and without collisions. Later, I struggled with balancing my economy. There was also a lot of small functionality to build in, e.g. differentiating between a unit's private action plan and the action queue it sends to the environment and precisely calculating the power of this action plan, including the moments it will update the action queue.

In the end, I ran out of time to properly implement the defence goals and also some attacking goals. I did implement clearing out the opponent's lichen decently, but defending ice tiles close to my base or defending my lichen was rushed and did not work properly, nor was it prioritized appropriately by the factories. Neither did I have the time to implement camping important opponent resources or capturing opponent's units low on energy and far away from their factory, where they are safe. Lacking these capabilities hampered the performance of my bot severely.
