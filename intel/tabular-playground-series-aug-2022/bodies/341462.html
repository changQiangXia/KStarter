Granted, the data we are working with is totally synthetic, but the competition creators were nice enough to provide us with a highly detailed backstory about what the data is and why we want to analyze it:

…an opportunity to help the fictional company Keep It Dry improve its main product Super Soaker. The product is used in factories to absorb spills and leaks.
The company has just completed a large testing study for different product prototypes. Can you use this data to build a model that predicts product failures?

And further:

Each product is used in a simulated real-world environment experiment, and and absorbs a certain amount of fluid (loading) to see whether or not it fails.
Your task is to use the data to predict individual product failures of new codes with their individual lab test results.

So our client has hired us to help them evaluate potential new products and determine whether they’ll be profitable. (They don’t say that outright, but it’s always about the profit.) In this case, though they undoubtedly call them something like “premium absorptive pads”, what they are selling are high-tech sponges. We want to know if these sponges will keep on absorbing, even when already saturated (i.e. at high loading values). By the way, I consider a distinct possibility that the “factories” described above are just a red herring. I would not be at all surprised to find that we’re actually helping in the process of designing diapers – and am just as glad that I don’t have to be around to observe the “failures”.

If they were helpful clients, they’d not only deliver us a pile of data, but also tell us what that data means. Unfortunately, the sponge market is really cutthroat and they don’t want their competitors to know about their product plans. After all, datasets like these do have a tendency to leak onto the internet – not unlike a failing sponge. We’re left to conjecture, but that’s what Kagglers are good at.

The product_code is, of course, just that – an arbitrary tag that identifies a particular product design. The attributes are design characteristics: properties that describe the intended make-up of a particular product. We can make guesses as to what they actually mean.

Clearly – unless the client is jerking us around – the first two attributes specify materials used in the product. Given that there are always two of them, I’d guess that our sponges consist of two layers, with potentially different materials in each. (Some products use the same material in each layer, Product B uses material #5 in both layers, while potential product H uses material #7 in both layers.

For the next attributes, we have to go out on a limb, but I’m going to guess that it corresponds to the number of distinct sub-layers of each main layer. (I’ll call them strata to distinguish them from the main layers.) You might assume that more strata will always be more effective, but they probably have to make the strata thinner in order to be able to add more. Besides, more strata will probably be more expensive, and we want to keep costs down. (You can see that they’re really trying to “cheap out” with product candidate F -- with 10 total strata as opposed to a more typical 12-16.)

The measurements are much more opaque. We know that they are actual experimental data since they follow probabilistic distributions rather than having fixed values. However, I don’t entirely trust the client on the names. I suspect that some of these “measurements” are actually experimental parameters. The first 3 in particular are integers that look like they are likely event counts and, interestingly, include a nice round “0” as the minimum value. While they could be observations, they could just be something like “how many times did we drop the sponge while testing it” or, more intentionally, “how many times did we wring out excess water”. In these cases, we might expect the failure rate to depend upon the measurement rather than simply being correlated. A subtle distinction, to be sure, but it might matter.

The real-valued measurements could be almost anything, but it does appear that they come in distinct pairs – perhaps the same measurement performed on each of the layers. We have several that range from 8-16, while others are more like 12-22 or 9-22. Unlike the integers, none of them have values anywhere near 0. Again, some of these might be experimental parameters rather than outcomes: some of the values could make sense as ambient temperatures, which might certainly affect absorption rates.

measurement_17, obviously, is a completely different kettle of fish. Values range from 196-1313. I’m not prepared to guess what real-world values would present this sort of range, but we could probably profit mightily if we could figure it out.

Finally, there is loading, which is the one clearly defined experimental parameter. Basically, it seems to specify how much water they poured onto (or into) the sponge to see if it failed. Anyone who’s done EDA or checked out feature importance will have noticed that it’s linearly correlated with the failure rate. This isn’t surprising, and if all we manage to learn is this correlation, we’ve failed our client. They don’t need machine learning experts to figure out that a sponge can only hold so much water before it starts dripping. Furthermore, this correlation is of absolutely no help in figuring out whether a new product will drip more or less.

Once we’ve theorized about what the data means we can start thinking about how that knowledge can be exploited. One obvious possibility comes from the “strata” theory. We could certainly guess that failure rates might correspond more closely to the total number of strata than to their distribution between layers. I haven’t run the experiment to see if it does make a difference, but I definitely will.

Also, if there are indeed paired features in the real-valued data, these become good candidates for combination features – either sums or differences might reveal new correlations.

Beyond this, of course, it’s nice to just stop and think that we could be making a difference in the real world. Who among hasn’t hoped to someday build a better sponge?
