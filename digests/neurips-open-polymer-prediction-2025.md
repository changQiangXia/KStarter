## write-up 正文（6 篇）

### 1st Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/neurips-open-polymer-prediction-2025/discussion/607947

Overview

My solution is an ensemble of BERT, AutoGluon, and Uni-Mol models. I believe the unique aspects that allowed me to win were:

Post-processing the predicted glass transition temperatures to account for a distribution shift between the training & leaderboard datasets.

Pretraining the BERT models on a pseudolabeled subset of PI1M.

I also got major gains from training on external labeled datasets and some tabular feature engineering tricks, but doubt those aspects were particularly unique.

Scores for each of the models in my final ensemble are included below. These are raw scores, without the postprocessing trick. Post-processing

tl;dr - the key trick was submission_df["Tg"] += (submission_df["Tg"].std() * 0.5644)

The long version:

I did not trust that the LB test data was fully fixed after the last update, so I did some probing to check for distribution shifts by adjusting the predictions for each property by +/- 0.1 * (the standard deviation of the predictions for that property). This revealed something fishy was going on for Tg, but I didn't try to investigate or aggressively exploit it immediately because I figured it might be random noise, and if something was genuinely broken someone else would probably complain. Then, in the last week I started probing to figure out the optimal offset, which revealed something was seriously wrong with the test data. A plot of those results is included below. This is partially based on some supplemental probing I did via late submissions after the competition, the results used to decide how my post-processing should work were less complete.

There's no way the raw Tg predictions of the ensemble I was applying the offset to were off by that much, even for the public LB data with sparse labels, so the incomplete probing with coefficients <= 0.5 were sufficient to convince me something was broken in the test data. It was so bad that I didn't even think the low row count and potential for sparse labels were sufficient for random noise to be a plausible explanation; a p-value I calculated based on some sketchy assumptions came out to < 0.01, so I viewed this as statistical evidence that there over a 99% chance the test dataset's labels were systematically incorrect, random noise was not a plausible explanation.

I was thinking that if the labels were off by a constant bias term (due to a bug in the host's data preparation pipeline), the relationship between the "bias coefficient" (the 0.5644 magic constant in the tl;dr) and the LB scores would likely be some sort of "V" shaped curve, with linear functions on either side of some optimal point, and with slopes that had opposite signs but identical magnitudes. Based on the public LB scores with bias coefficients <= 0.5 or == 1.0, it was possible to fit a V shaped curve that fit the data near-perfectly, and the predicted optimal coefficient was 0.5644, which indeed scored better on the public LB than anything I had tried thus far. I also tried stepwise functions that apply higher or lower offsets to the raw predictions in certain ranges, and ones with linear slopes that depend on the Tg predictions, but couldn't quickly get any of those approaches to score better than a simple constant bias that's the same for all predictions, so that's what I went with for one of my final submissions. The other final submission just produced raw Tg predictions, in case the problem was fixed in the private LB data (which it wasn't).

Now that the private LB scores are revealed, it is pretty obvious that whatever caused the training vs. public LB distribution shift was even worse for the private LB data, but I couldn't see that until the end. As a result, the post-processing I chose was somewhat suboptimal, but I didn't get blindsided anywhere near as badly as most other competitors.

Handling dirty training labels

The leaderboard's test dataset wasn't the only one with dirty labels, all of the external datasets I trained on had their own unique undesirable quirks too. Typically a mixture of random label noise, labels that had a strange non-linear relationship with the ground truth, labels that were off by some constant bias factor, and/or outlier samples that were way out of distribution. I dealt with this using a mixture of 5 strategies, outlined below. A combination of the first 3 strategies was sufficient to extract something useful from most datasets, the last 2 were only used in rare cases.

Label rescaling: I predicted the properties of each polymer using a fairly strong ensemble trained on only the host's original training data (one whose predictions had a very nice linear relationship with the "ground truth" during cross validation testing), then trained an isotonic regression model to predict that ensemble's predictions based on the raw dataset labels, then used that isotonic regression models predictions as rescaled labels. Idea being that if the dataset's raw labels are off by some constant bias factor or have a strange non-linear relationship with the ground truth, the isotonic regression model would lear to adjust for that and produce cleaner labels. To reduce overfitting, the labels used to train my final were frequently a weighted average of the raw labels and rescaled, with weights tuned by optuna on a per-dataset basis.

Error-based data filtering: After the rescaling step above, my code computes the absolute difference between each label and an ensemble's predictions, then discards all samples for which that error exceeds some threshold. These thresholds were typically expressed as a ratio of the sample error to the mean absolute error observed for the ensemble when testing against the original host dataset, so the thresholds for all properties were in a similar range, which made it easier to define a search space for Optuna to explore when finding the optimal configuration.

Sample weighting: Even with the cleaning steps above, some datasets were still lower-quality than others. As a result, I had optuna tune the sample weights on a per-dataset basis, so models could pay less attention to the training examples from lower-quality datasets.

Semi-manual filter rule: The "data.csv" file in RadonPy's GitHub repo has some thermal conductivity values that I thought were suspiciously high (based on a scatter plot of an ensemble's predictions vs the dataset's labels). As a result, I created a variant of it which drops all the rows with Tc values above 0.402. Optuna commonly favored this version over the original when tuning the external data usage configuration, so I think this filtering was beneficial for most models.

Model stacking: I didn't use the cleaning approaches above for the properties or molecular descriptors based on MD simulations I ran myself. Partially because the data I generated myself had supplemental metrics that I wanted to make use of, and partially because I had a gut feeling that some of my labels might be so dirty that no amount of filtering, rescaling, and reweighting would be able to extract anything useful from them. As a result, I instead trained an ensemble of 41 XGBoost models to predict the simulation results, then used the predictions of those models as supplemental features that got fed to AutoGluon as inputs in the tabular part of my solution. Idea being that the 2nd level models could learn to make predictions based on whatever wacky non-linear relationships might be present in the data. For the tabular models, this gave me a CV wMAE score improvement of ~0.0005 vs. not using the simulation results at all, so I think it was useful.

To keep cross-validation results consistent when tuning the external data configurations, and prevent unrealistic test samples from impacting my CV scores, cross-validation scores were computed using only samples from the original host data for testing purposes. Broadly speaking, the CV strategy was to train on 100% of the selected external data + 80% of the host data, then test on 20% of the host data, then repeat that process 5 times with different host data held in reserve each time.

I didn't keep careful track of how much items 1 - 4 above helped, but Optuna liked them enough to enable them with some rather aggressive configurations, so I think they were at least beneficial in local cross-validation testing.

Deduplication

Many of the datasets contain polymers that are duplicates or near-duplicates of one another. I dealt with this using a mixture of two strategies, with more aggressive deduplication to avoid leakage between the training and test sets than duplicates within the training data.

Eliminating duplicates within the training data: I converted all SMILES strings to their canonical format before checking for duplicate SMILES. If two training examples had identical canonical SMILEs, the one with the lower sample weight (selected by Optuna based on the dataset it originated from) was dropped. This causes duplicates from datasets with lower-quality labels to be dropped in the event of conflicting labels. And because all SMILES are converted to their canonical format before deduplication, minor notation discrepancies are not sufficient to fool this deduplication logic.

Avoiding train vs. test leakage: For each test fold, Tanimoto similarity scores between all possible pairs of training and test monomers were computed, and any training example with a similarity score above 0.99 vs. any monomer in the test dataset was dropped to eliminate near duplicates. In retrospect, while near-duplicates with different canonical forms are present, they don't have much cross-validation score impact if I let them through, so I think just checking for exact-duplicate canonical SMILES would likely be adequate. This filtering mostly stems from a bug which caused me to see unrealistically "good" CV scores and become even more paranoid than usual in regards to eliminating data leakage; I kept it around even after the bug was fixed.

BERT

My BERT models were trained in 2 stages, after whatever pretraining was done to compute the pretrained weights on the HuggingFace hub.

Pretraining on PI1M: I used an ensemble of BERT, Uni-Mol, AutoGluon, and D-MPNN models to predict the properties of 50,000 hypothetical polymers from PI1M, then pretrained other BERT models on a classification task to predict which pairs of polymers had higher or lower property values than one another. Loss values were ignored for pairs with similar property values, which reduced the degree to which the models were exposed to label noise related to inaccuracies in the raw ensemble predictions. For efficiency, this was a multi-task classification objective; models were pretrained to compute classification results for all 5 properties at once, so I didn't have to train separate foundation models for each property. This was inspired by the paper "RankUp: Boosting Semi-Supervised Regression with an Auxiliary Ranking Classifier", but I found using it for pretraining with precomputed pseudolabels to work better than the online version described in the original paper. It improved the scores of my BERT models by ~0.004 LB, ~0.01 CV in comparison to just using pretrained model weights from HuggingFace (tried 5 different third-party foundation models, and adding this additional pretraining stage boosted all of their scores). Some of the models finetuned after this pretraining tuned out more accurate than the original teacher ensembles used to compute the pseudolables, so I believe it was more effective than standard knowledge distillation.

Finetuning: Most aspects of this were very standard for a BERT finetuning script - AdamW optimizer, no layers frozen, one cycle learning rate schedule with linear annealing, automatic mixed precision, learning rate + batch size + epoch count tuned using optuna, gradient norms clipped to 1, etc. The only things that felt a bit unusual or domain-specific were the data augmentation strategy and use of separate learning rate schedules (with different max_lr values) for the regression head and the backbone. Data augmentation is discussed more in its own section later. As for the dual max_lr trick (to set the head's learning rate to a value higher than the one used for the backbone), I heard of people doing that before, and had heard it is commonly beneficial for small datasets, but had never used it myself. For most foundation models and target properties in this competition, I found it slightly beneficial to set the head learning rates an order of magnitude higher than the backbone learning rates. The training datasets were relatively small, so that wasn't too surprising.

Data augmentation: 

Training-time augmentation: During both pretraining and finetuning, I used Chem.MolToSmiles(..., canonical=False, doRandom=True, isomericSmiles=True) to create 10 non-canonical SMILES per input SMILES to increase the amount of training data ~10x. I also tried randomly using Kukulized SMILES, removing stereochemistry, and making the hydrogens & bonds more explicit, but those augmentations were not beneficial.

Test-time augmentation: My inference code generates 50 predictions per SMILE using the same data augmentation strategy used during training, just with the amount of random variation increased 5x. The final predictions for each BERT model were the median of those 50 predictions.

Test time augmentation alone provided a LB improvement of ~0.01 in one early experiment. My notes are too badly fragmented to say exactly how much the training data augmentation helped without additional ablation testing, but I'm under the impression it was very beneficial too, and a training data augmentation factor of 10x was optimal to within a margin of +/- 30% (unlike test time augmentation, more is not better).

Foundation model selection:

Chemistry-specific foundation models aren't very good: In one round of experiments without pseudolabeled training data, the best CV wMAE score achieved with ChemBERTa was 0.0634, the best score achieved with polyBERT was 0.592, and the best score achieved with ModernBERT-base was 0.0584. So, the general-purpose model beat anything chemistry or polymer specific.

Bigger models aren't better: Using tuning and testing methodology identical to #1, above, ModernBERT-large scored 0.0587 whereas -base scored 0.0584. So scaling up made the score worse. DeBERTa-v3-large's scores also sucked, so I don't think this behavior was isolated to the ModernBERT family.

Pretraining on code is better than natural language: The DeBERTa cross-validation results were so bad I didn't bother recording them or submitting any DeBERTa models, but I have vague recollections DeBERTa-v3-large was roughly as bad as ChemBERTa, substantially worse than ModernBERT-base or ModernBERT-large. While thinking about why that might be, I recalled that the ModernBERT family also scores disproportionately well on programming-related benchmarks, and had a relatively large amount of code in its pretraining dataset, so this caused me to wonder if a model trained on an even higher ratio of code to natural language would score even better, which led me to trying CodeBERT. CodeBERT wound up being ~tied for being my best single model. Slightly better than ModernBERT-base in cross-validation testing and on the public leaderboard, slightly worse on the private leaderboard, but generally close enough to be within margin of error, and substantially better than anything purely trained on chemistry or English data.

Data generation: As mentioned above, I ran molecular dynamics simulations for over 1,000 hypothetical polymers from PI1M, and used it to train models whose predictions were used as supplemental features for other tabular models, which improved my CV scores slightly (~0.0005 wMAE). I took a lot of shortcuts to save CPU time, this was really complicated to setup, and I had no prior MD experience, so it is a small miracle the results were useful at all. The main steps in my data generation pipeline are outlined below.

Picking the conformation search config: I had a fast unstable configuration that crashes or hangs for ~half of the polymers and a slower configuration that takes ~5x longer (i.e. ~5 hours per polymer instead of ~1 hour). I trained a LightGBM classification model to predict which polymers would cause the crashes based on their RDKit molecular descriptors, some of the higher-importance backbone vs. sidechain features, and the results of ETKDGv3 based conformer generation & MMFFOptimization (which quickly produces inaccurate conformers - the random errors and potential energy statistics from this step were useful clues to predict problems that would be encountered by downstream code). This allowed my data generation code to downsample the difficult polymers and jump straight to using the fast unstable config for the easy ones. The "fast unstable" config is based on psi4's Hartree-Fock geometry optimization method, the "slow stable" config is based on "b97-3c". There might be a one-size-fits-all configuration that gets the best of both worlds, but this was the best I could come up with in under a week, as a complete chemistry/molecular dynamics novice.

Conformation search: RadonPy makes this simple, other than the configuration selection step above.

Polymerization: The degree of polymerization was automatically adjusted to have ~600 atoms per chain regardless of the monomer size. RadonPy makes this fairly simple.

Charge assignment: RadonPy makes this simple.

Amorphous cell generation: I used 10 chains per cell with a starting density of 0.15. RadonPy makes this simple.

Equilibrium simulation: This works by running LAMMPS as a subprocess. I built LAMMPS from source because I couldn't find a good precompiled executable with GPU support. @hengck23's LAMMPS starter was immensely useful when figuring out how to run it, although I did tinker with the settings a bit in order to get more realistic density results (which more closely matched the host data), improve throughput, and fix some miscellaneous errors. No longer recall all the differences - the problems were less 'interesting' than figuring out a viable conformation search config, so they barely registered in memory.

Property & molecular descriptor estimation: This is a bunch of LLM-slop which ingests the raw simulation results and uses MDAnalsysis, RdKit, and a few hundred lines of custom logic to estimate FFV, Density, Rg, all of the 3D descriptors available in RDKit (for both the monomer and a full polymer change), and a bunch of statistics that ChatGPT thought would be useful that I never took the time to fully understand. My general philosophy was to compute all the features I could get my hands on, then compute feature importance values and let Optuna sort out which ones are worth keeping. The top-5 most important (according to XGBoost and LightGBM) were the FFV, density, NPR1, NPR2, and Eccentricity values, but Optuna found it beneficial to keep way more than that. It was sort of all-or nothing; for 3/5 targets it chose to keep all 41, for the other 2 (density and Rg) it decided to drop them all entirely. Importance based feature selection was not particularly useful when the results were used in conjuction with AutoGluon, possibly because the top features were poisonous in some way (maybe they caused overfitting for some targets?), but I did observe some gain when using it in conjunction with GBDT models.

AutoGluon vs. "traditional methods": AutoGluon's "best" quality preset with a 2 hour limit for each property was able to beat an ensemble of XGBoost, LightGBM, and TabM models that I tuned with Optuna and ~20x that amount of compute (not counting data preprocessing tuning, which was in the ballpark of ~1 day per downstream prediction library I paired it with, or all the other models I tried before settling on XGB + LGBM + TabM for the relatively manual ensemble). Its wMAE score was ~2% better than the relatively manual ensemble, good enough that it was not useful to make an ensemble of AutoGluon + my more manually constructed ensemble. The "best" quality AutoGluon runs are very slow, so to let the data tuning run in a reasonable timeframe with a decent trial count I paired it with "good" quality AG runs with a 5 minute time limit. This was my first time using AutoGluon, and I found it very impressive. The level of effort invested in the manual ensemble was maybe only a third of what I used to win the UM MCTS competition, so it is plausible I could beat it if I really pulled out all the stops and wasn't distracted by non-tabular experiments, but I was absolutely gob-smacked by AutoGluon's efficiency. The "extreme" quality present introduced in AG 1.4.0 is supposedly even more accurate and efficient, but I did not observe any benefits when I tried switching over to it (possibly because the feature counts were too high for it to use all of its models - I saw some errors about that), so the tabular predictor in my final ensemble was created with the "best" quality preset.

3D models (Uni-Mol 2 84M)

This was the simplest of all the models accurate enough to make it into my final ensemble. Little more than tossing merged, cleaned, datasets for each target property into unimol_tools.MolTrain and using Optuna to tune the learning rate, epoch count, and batch size. It didn't require any feature engineering or a custom training loop. Overview

My winning method is a fusion scheme that combines a Graph Attention Network (GATv2Conv) with selected Morgan Fingerprint features. I believe the main factors for my success are:

1,GATv2's attention mechanism can already capture complex, high-level geometric and chemical environment information. The simple, bit-based Morgan Fingerprints provide complementary, low-level substructure information without creating conflicting signals.

2,Recognizing that the data might have biases, I used the validation set as a baseline to calibrate the data and used 5-fold cross-validation to mitigate the risks posed by potential data anomalies.

Residual Connections are implemented via simple element-wise addition, enabling stable training of the 6-layer deep network.

Robust Ensembling with 5-Fold Cross-Validation:

The final submission is a simple average ensemble of the predictions from the 5 models. Each model is given an equal weight of 20%.

Optimizer: We use AdamW, which often provides better regularization and performance on deep learning models compared to the standard Adam.

Training Methods

Custom Weighted MAE Loss with AdamW Optimizer.

We implemented a custom loss function in PyTorch that perfectly mirrors the official wMAE competition metric. This ensures the model directly optimizes for the final score.

AdamW optimizer was used with a constant learning rate of 1e-4.

Robust 5-Fold Cross-Validation Ensemble.

The final solution is a simple average ensemble of 5 models trained on different 80/20 splits of the data.

Batch size of 64 was used during training.

Two-Stage Training with Early Stopping & Refitting.

Stage 1: Find Best Epoch. We train on 80% of the data and use the remaining 20% as a validation set for Early Stopping (patience=40), preventing overfitting.

Stage 2: Refit on Full Data. A new model is then re-trained from scratch on 100% of the fold's data for exactly the best number of epochs found in Stage 1. This maximizes data utilization for each final model.

Post-Hoc Linear Calibration.

After training, a simple LinearRegression model is fitted for each target on the validation set's predictions. This effectively corrects any systematic bias from the GNN model, providing a final small but crucial boost in performance.

Important&interesting Findings

Finding 1: Polymer Chain Extension is Superior to Isomer Generation

We experimented with two distinct data augmentation strategies.

Strategy A (Chain Extension): Simulating polymerization by extending monomer repeat units (A -> A-A-A). This was highly effective.

Strategy B (Isomer Generation): Creating multiple different but chemically valid SMILES strings (isomers) for the same monomer. This provided no performance gain.

Finding 2:The "Sweet Spot" of Batch Size - Bigger is Not Always Better

Despite having enough GPU memory for larger batch sizes (128, 256), we found that a smaller batch size of 64 consistently yielded the best results.

Larger batch sizes led to a degradation in the final PB score.

Finding 3: Surprising Synergy Between GNN Architectures and Feature Types

We tested different combinations of GNN backbones and auxiliary features. The results revealed a strong and unexpected interaction effect.

GATv2 + Morgan Fingerprints was the clear winner.

Interestingly, RDKit Descriptors hurt the performance of GATv2 a lot, but helped the performance of NNConv a little.

Hypothesis:

GATv2's attention mechanism already captures complex, high-level geometric and chemical environments. The simple, bit-based Morgan Fingerprints provide complementary, low-level substructure information without creating conflicting signals.

NNConv, being a simpler message-passing network, may benefit more from the pre-computed, human-engineered RDKit Descriptors, which provide it with high-level chemical concepts it struggles to learn on its own.

### 2nd Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/neurips-open-polymer-prediction-2025/discussion/608984

Initial Observations

I started by analyzing the best-scoring public notebooks at the time and the public leaderboard, leading to the following observations:

There were many different models among the top-scoring public notebooks.

The top public notebooks weren't far behind the top performers on the public leaderboard.

Complex models didn't score significantly better than simpler ones.

There was a benefit to ensembling.

Choosing a Strategy

I entered this competition without domain expertise in chemistry. Furthermore, since many different models, both simple and complex, were getting very similar results, I did not see it as productive to create my own models. Rather, I would rely on what seemed promising from public notebooks as a starting point. I used several different public notebooks as a base over the course of the competition.

I'm not going to go into details about the models I ended up using, because I was treating them as black boxes. I focused my early efforts on optimizing the ensembling step at the end.

The Data Anomaly

After the test data was changed, it was noticed that adding 273.15 to Tg would greatly increase public leaderboard score. This was attributed to a units issue (Celsius and Kelvin). But upon my own investigation, I noticed that adding 300 would yield an even higher score than 273.15. After the units issue was fixed, I found that adding 30 still yielded an improved score. This became the focus of my efforts. I first checked the other targets to see if they had a similar issue, but found that they did not. So, I focused on finding the best adjustment for Tg. While I tried many things, in the end I found a simple +40 shift worked best.

Multiply by 9/5, Then Add 32?

Towards the end of the competition, I was trying to determine why a shift was effective and realized it could be another units issue. I tried the Celsius -> Fahrenheit conversion formula ((9/5)x + 32), but it reduced the public leaderboard score, so I abandoned the idea. However, after the competition ended I noticed that this submission had a private LB score of 0.068, significantly better than the 1st place submission.

While this looks a units issue, this is not necessarily the case. A transformation being effective does not mean it is optimal. After the competition ended, I found that using (9/5x) + 45 performs even better (0.066 private LB score).

Conclusion

After the competition ended, I went back to my very first submission, which used an ExtraTreesRegressor and would have placed around 1300th on the private LB. I added the (9/5) x + 32 transformation and reran it. The resulting private LB score — 0.077 — was the same as my final submission (which used the less effective +40 transformation with an ensemble of models).

So In the end, despite there being five properties to predict, performance in this competition was determined by a distribution shift in just one property. This distribution shift was present in the public leaderboard data, but it was stronger in the private leaderboard data.
