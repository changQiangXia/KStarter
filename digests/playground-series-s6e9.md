### 2nd Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e9/discussion/744815

Thanks to Kaggle for another fun Playground episode. Team Alicia finished 2nd of 3,575 teams on the private leaderboard (0.94588). We chose our models and both final submissions with nested cross-validation; the public LB served only as a noise guard and as one noisy input to the final pair choice (the separate public-LB experiment below was never selected).

Conventions. 1u = 1e-5 ROC AUC. Level 1 = single models with out-of-fold (OOF) predictions on our 5 folds; level 2 = stacks of level-1 OOFs; level 3 = the final blend, whose inputs are per-fold rank-logits, logit((rank − 0.5)/n). "Nested" = fitted on 4 folds, scored on the 5th. "Honest" = not fitted to the public LB.

TL;DR

Result. Private 2nd, 0.94588 (1st: 0.94602). Final A passed our strict nested-CV gate; Final B, an all-in refit, failed it (+0.58u at 1.15 fold-SE, vs the +1.5u / 2.5-SE bar) and was a documented judgement call. In selection, public scores served only as a noise guard and as noisy observations in a pair model; our one deliberate public-LB experiment was never selected.

Validation first. Across our honest submissions on our fixed folds, the nested gain ranked the private scores with Spearman 0.991; the public score managed 0.793.

TabPFN-3.5 with the full labelled context. No subsampling: ~428k context rows per fold, all 668,665 for test. AUC rose about +18.6u per doubling of context with no sign of saturating (fold 0, one seed).

Label-free surrogate features. distilgpt2, fine-tuned only on the original 10k rows, gives each row a Yes-vs-No log-likelihood ratio (LLR), most useful as a TabPFN input.

AUC-direct level-3 blend. A sigmoid pairwise-AUC surrogate over all ~2.6e10 pairs, computed exactly with histograms and an FFT: +1.62u over our log-loss stack.

A cautionary contrast. Files we deliberately fitted to the public LB reached 0.94945 public (1st) but only 0.94313 private.

Data in brief

Shape. 668,665 train / 286,571 test rows, 13 features (7 numeric, 6 categorical), 17.46% positives, no train/test shift (adversarial AUC 0.50006).

Origin. Synthesised from Omkar Kadam's CC0 "EV Adoption Behavior and Range Anxiety" dataset (10,000 rows). Chris Deotte's EDA recovered the purchase recipe: buy if 1.2·income/100k + 0.6·concern + 2·subsidy − 1·[Medium anxiety] − 3·[High anxiety] + noise > 5.5 (AUC 0.9377 alone). 97.9% of competition incomes occur in the original data.

Generator artifacts. broccoli beef argued on the forum that an LLM generated the rows, emitting numbers as tokens. Most of our strongest level-1 members (TabPFN, the GAR-based models, the token GBDTs) therefore use GPT-2 BPE token features of the exact CSV strings of income and commute.

1. Validation first

Folds and holdout. A fixed fold file splits 534,932 "dev" rows into 5 folds. The other 133,733 rows are a sealed holdout, never scored or used for selection; their labels enter only the final full-train refits and TabPFN's test context (their covariates also enter label-free features).

Screening subset. A 50,000-row subset S was used once for screening, then excluded. Every stack gain below is nested on the 484,932 dev rows outside S (mean gain, fold-SE = sd/√5, positive folds), unless marked fold 0 or single model.

Pre-registration. From 28 September every candidate was logged before its result existed (54 entries).

Gates. Standard: ≥ +1.0u, ≥ 2 fold-SE, ≥ 4/5 folds, all weights > 0, plus test-drift checks. Strict (for the final round, when many families ran in parallel): ≥ +1.5u and ≥ 2.5 fold-SE. No seed selection.

Placebo. New features ran next to a same-width, row-permuted placebo; a placebo of 24–58 columns cost −3 to −8u, so real columns had to beat that dilution.

Eight entries passed a gate, plus one documented exception (1.8 SE vs the 2-SE gate); after the AUC-direct blend, about 40 more candidates all failed.

Why our finals did not chase the public LB. The paired public-noise SD of two submissions is about 109·√(1−ρ) u, i.e. 3.45u at ρ = 0.999, the size of our gains. Three of our stacks scored the same public 0.94687 while nested dev rose by +9.44u; private rose from 0.94578 to 0.94584.

3. TabPFN-3.5 at full context

Setup.

tabpfn==9.0.0, default TabPFN-3.5 classifier checkpoint tabpfn-v3.5-20260909.safetensors; n_estimators=2, ignore_pretraining_limits=True, fit_mode='fit_with_cache' with automatic KV-cache precision, 32,768 test rows per batch. In a 50k-context check this was bit-identical to fit_mode='fit_preprocessors' and used less memory.

No context subsampling. Each OOF prediction is one fit whose context is all ~428k labelled rows of the other four folds (427,945 for fold 0); each test prediction is one fit on all 668,665 labelled rows.

Runtime. About 3.5 min per fold on a Colab RTX PRO 6000 and 21–32 min on a local GPU, where the 668k-context test fit took 3,334–3,696 s at 22.97 GB. A Kaggle T4 fits a 428k context only with an int8 KV cache (4,378–5,405 s per fold) and cannot hold the 668k test fit.

Inputs mattered more than inference settings.

Base input. 43 columns: 13 raw, 24 GPT-2 BPE token features of the income and commute strings, 6 income digits. Tokens and digits moved fold-0 AUC from 0.944752 to 0.946039.

LLR columns. Each better LLR input gave a better member. One LLR column added +4.7u to the single model; two columns, one per surrogate recipe (TabPFN-2LLR), added +2.80u to the stack at a fixed combiner (4.9 SE, 5/5).

Label-free GAR columns. Paul's 85 label-free columns (key counts, the generator formula, 79 transductive group-size and leave-one-out-mean columns over income, commute and token keys) gave TabPFN-MIX: +28.6u over TabPFN-2LLR on fold 0 (single model; identical runs on different GPUs differ by ~3u) and +2.37u to the stack (5/5).

No saturation. +18.6u per doubling (log-linear fit); the last step alone added +14.2u (paired bootstrap +14.5u, SE 3.2).

Test context. Extrapolating to 668,665 rows gives the single model +7u to +22u (central +12u). A pre-registered simulation of this mismatch (weights fitted with a half-context member, applied to the full-context one) gave +0.42u (5/5), so we kept full-context test predictions.

Real labels only. Pseudo-labelled context rows added nothing (−0.13u in the stack); original-dataset rows slightly hurt (about −2u single). More seeds or estimators never paid inside the stack; only new label-free inputs did.

Two leaks the protocol caught. A TabPFN stacker over member OOFs plus raw columns looked +13.1u better under the standard OOF protocol but was −54u leak-free; as a level-2 learner it scored an impossible +162u, because it attends to context rows whose OOFs came from models trained with the query fold's labels. Never feed model OOFs to a neighbour-reading learner.

4. Generator-surrogate LLR features

Idea. If the rows were sampled from a language model fine-tuned on the original 10,000 rows, a similar model trained only on the original data should approximate the generator's own label probability without seeing a competition label.

Recipe.

Model. distilgpt2, fine-tuned GReaT-style (Borisov et al., ICLR 2023) in our own pure-PyTorch code: each sample is the 14 original columns in a fresh random order, written as "column is value"; the optimiser mimics the HF-Trainer default (AdamW, lr 5e-5, linear decay). Recipe A: 100 epochs at batch 32 (about 4.1 h on a local GPU). Recipe B: 100 epochs at batch 8 (about 21 min on a Colab RTX PRO 6000).

Scoring. LLR = log p(row | "Will_Buy_EV is Yes,") − log p(row | "Will_Buy_EV is No,"), averaged over K = 4 fixed random column orders.

What we learned.

Small screening sets mislead. With 10k rows per fold every configuration looked negative (bootstrap SE 2–6u per fold). On the full evaluation outside S, the primary variant (fixed after screening, before the full evaluation) gave +1.86u on an earlier level-2 stack and +3.81u on stack A, 5/5 folds in both.

Average across recipes, not seeds. Single seeds gave +4.04 to +6.25u on stack A. A third recipe-A seed lowered the two-seed average (+6.02u) by −0.42u, but mixing recipes kept helping: plus one recipe-B seed +7.29u → with three recipe-B seeds in total +9.71u → all 28 surrogates +10.69u.

It re-learns the recipe. A diagnostic 30-epoch surrogate's LLR correlated 0.994 with the recipe score; the rest looks like LM-specific distortion.

Use it as an input, not a column. The first stack with TabPFN-LLR and the LLR column gained +10.48u over stack A, and the LLR later fed TabPFN-2LLR, GAR-LLR and TabPFN-MIX. By the end the raw column was redundant: final weights 0.003 and 0.002, and dropping it from the log-loss stack would have gained +1.00u.

5. The AUC-direct level-3 blend

Our earlier level-3 combiner was a non-negative logistic regression (log-loss + ½‖w‖², w ≥ 0). In a pre-registered sweep of 28 combiner objectives, run by a Codex agent, only one AUC-direct variant (and its bagged twin) passed the strict nested gate:

$$
\min_{w\ge 0,\ \sum_k w_k=1}\ L(w)=\frac{1}{n_+n_-}\sum_{i\in\text{pos}}\sum_{j\in\text{neg}}\sigma\left(-\frac{s_i-s_j}{\tau}\right),\qquad s=Xw,\quad \tau=0.1
$$

X holds the members' per-fold rank-logits. 1 − L is a smoothed pairwise AUC (τ → 0 recovers 1 − AUC). AUC ignores shifts, so there is no intercept, and Σw = 1 fixes the scale, so τ is in rank-logit units. A training split has ~2.6e10 pairs, so we never sample them: positives and negatives are binned with linear interpolation onto a 0.0005 grid over [−16, 16] (64,001 points), and one FFT convolution with the sigmoid kernel gives the exact all-pairs value of the interpolated objective, and its gradient, in O(n + N log N). SLSQP starts from the normalised log-loss weights.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import fftconvolve
from scipy.special import expit
from scipy.optimize import minimize

TAU, H, LO, N = 0.1, 0.0005, -16, 64001 # grid g_m = LO + m*H covers [-16, 16]
# per-fold dev rank-logits lie within +-12.27 (test +-13.26), and w >= 0 with sum(w) = 1 keeps s inside that range;
# the clip only guards against other inputs
def hist(s): # linear-interpolation histogram, sums to 1
 q = np.clip((s - LO) / H, 0, N - 1 - 1e-9)
 lo = np.floor(q).astype(int); fr = q - lo
 h = np.bincount(lo, weights=1 - fr, minlength=N) + np.bincount(lo + 1, weights=fr, minlength=N)
 return h[:N] / len(s)

KERNEL = expit(-np.arange(-N + 1, N) * H / TAU) # sigma(-lag / tau), lag = pos bin - neg bin

def auc_loss(w, X, y): # mean over all (pos, neg) pairs of sigma(-(s_pos - s_neg) / tau)
 s = X @ w
 F = fftconvolve(hist(s[y == 0]), KERNEL)[N - 1:2 * N - 1]
 return hist(s[y == 1]) @ F

def fit(X, y, w0): # w0: e.g. the non-negative log-loss weights
 res = minimize(auc_loss, w0 / w0.sum(), args=(X, y), method="SLSQP",
 bounds=[(0, 1)] * X.shape[1],
 constraints={"type": "eq", "fun": lambda w: w.sum() - 1})
 return res.x

On synthetic rank-logit data the sketch matches the brute-force all-pairs loss to within 1e-8 (interpolation error). It leaves SLSQP to finite differences; our runs used the exact gradient of the interpolated objective (∇L = Xᵀc, from grid differences of the convolved histograms).

Overview

Our final solution was a logistic regression ensemble of 115 prediction columns. Some columns were already ensembles themselves, so this is the number of inputs to the final stack, rather than the total number of base models.

The main components were:

Target encoding of exact values and rounded values, especially income.

Different model families, including XGBoost, LightGBM, RealMLP and TabM.

Soft pseudo-labels from teacher models.

Additional models using income token groups and features from a source-trained language model.

L2-regularized logistic regression on the models' logit predictions.

Feature Engineering

Target encoding and income rounding

Target encoding was very effective in this competition. Our best reference using only the 13 raw columns had a CV of 0.943113. A RealMLP using exact-value target encoding and rounded income reached 0.945371. After further changes to the income encodings and smoothing, together with two rounds of hyperparameter optimization, it reached 0.945840.

Income was particularly interesting. The target rate varied between individual income values, and nearby values also contained useful information. We therefore used several levels of rounding, from coarse groups to the exact income value. Coarser groups helped when there were too few observations for a specific value.

We also used value-level encodings for commute distance, age and categorical features. All supervised encodings were calculated out of fold, so a validation row's own target was excluded from its encoding.

Original data

The original dataset contained 10,000 rows. About 97.94% of the income values in the competition data were also present in the original data.

In our experiments, simply adding the original rows as labeled training data did not help. However, statistics from the original data were useful, including group-level target statistics and information about which values occurred there. These were also part of mahog's LightGBM feature engineering.

For these experiments, we added all 286,571 test rows to each fold's training data, using soft predictions from a teacher whose training excluded that validation fold.

Soft labels worked better than hard labels. Hard pseudo-labels performed worse than adding no pseudo-labels, and randomly permuting the soft labels made the result much worse. This suggested that the teacher's probability estimates were useful for distillation.

What Did Not Work

Some approaches that did not improve our tested configurations were:

Missingness and distribution-drift features, duplicate-row searches and id-based features.

Adding the original data directly as labeled training rows.

Additional crossed-key, pair, digit and frequency features on top of our existing per-value target-encoding features.

Broad cross-row aggregation features and further generic hyperparameter searches late in the competition.

Nonlinear stacking models.

Finer, random or overlapping interaction groups.

Stronger XGBoost pseudo-label teachers.

Removing models by prediction correlation, or grouping them by model family before stacking.

These results depend on the features already present. For example, mahog's income digit-cross target encoding did help his models trained on hard labels.

id alone scored 0.49999 AUC. We also checked a previously published or selected high-income boundary that contained 393/393 positives in the reused labels. Setting those predictions to one changed AUC by only +0.0000000743. It made almost no difference to the ranking of positive and negative examples.

How We Worked

We shared OOF and test predictions using the same folds and row order. This helped us combine models trained in different environments and check whether an improvement was consistent across folds.

### 9th Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e9/discussion/744923

Thanks to Kaggle and everyone who shared their work during this competition. I finished 9th on the private leaderboard with ROC-AUC 0.94575, after placing 93rd on the public leaderboard with 0.94679.

The solution evolved from a CPU XGBoost baseline into an ensemble built around generator-aware numerical representations, fold-local target statistics, ridge logistic regression and residual boosting. The final combiner used 54 prediction streams and L2-regularized logistic regression.

Public notebooks were an important part of this solution. Below I distinguish the published starting points from the models and experiments I added. The largest late improvement started with a published token/LR/residual ensemble; I then extended its feature basis, trained additional models and rebuilt the stack.

Overview

Result: private 0.94575, public 0.94679; final winning file was V39.

Features: digits, frequencies, multiscale numerical groups, fold-local target rates, GPT-2 token hierarchies and income–context statistics.

Models: ridge logistic regression, XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, with additional teacher, auxiliary and neural prediction streams.

Combiner: standardized raw logits and percentile ranks, followed by L2 logistic regression; V39 used C=0.1.

Main lesson: new representations and residual learning helped more than repeatedly adjusting a highly correlated model pool.

Validation limitation: the late meta-model checks were conditional on an existing OOF library, not a fully nested retraining of the complete pipeline.

1. Understanding the data

The task was to predict Will_Buy_EV, evaluated with ROC-AUC. The competition data contained 668,665 training rows and 286,571 test rows, and was related to the original 10,000-row EV Adoption Behavior and Range Anxiety dataset.

Income was especially useful when represented in several ways. Its magnitude carried signal, but so did exact values, rounding, digits and group frequencies. Commute distance benefited from similar treatment.

The original-data purchase recipe also provided a useful prior. Following @cdeotte's analysis, recipe-based scores could be used as an initial margin, leaving a boosting model to learn deviations from that prior.

Later, GPT-2 token hierarchies provided another representation of the numerical strings. Using tokenizer-derived features here did not mean training a GPT-2 language model. The resulting features were inputs to logistic and tabular models. I did not use TabPFN in this solution.

2. Early gains: numerical representations and target statistics

The initial CPU XGBoost baseline scored 0.94330 public / 0.94278 private. The largest early improvement came from a package of feature engineering and blending:

Digit decomposition and trailing-zero features.

Exact-value frequencies and rounded numerical groups.

Income resolutions such as 100, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 5,000.

Target encoding with fold-safe training features.

A rank blend of complementary feature views.

V2 reached 0.94574 public / 0.94483 private, a private increase of 0.00205 over the baseline. This accounts for approximately 69% of the baseline-to-final private improvement. It is the gain of the whole V2 package, rather than an isolated attribution to target encoding.

V3 added XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost with 10 folds and two seeds. V4 expanded exact/coarse target encodings with multiple smoothing values and competition-versus-original frequency features. V5 combined multiple feature views and recipe-initialized boosting.

By V5, the private score was 0.94545. Increasing the number of models and folds subsequently gave diminishing returns.

3. The late breakthrough: logistic initialization and residual boosting

The most useful late direction came from @heuljax's generator-aware ridge logistic regression and @goodpjw2008's LR-margin GBDT OOF stack.

The idea was to construct a strong logistic prediction from token hierarchies, target-rate chains and context features, then fit boosting models with that prediction as their initial logit:

Numerical strings and raw context
 → token/group statistics
 → ridge logistic initial logit
 → residual XGBoost / LightGBM / CatBoost
 → OOF prediction library
 → regularized logistic stack

V37 was the exact published @goodpjw2008 submission file, after local validation and OOF replay. It scored 0.94675 public / 0.94566 private. That improvement should be credited to the published method, rather than described as my original model training.

For V38, I extended and trained the pipeline:

A 368-feature basis, including full income–context rates and income-neighborhood statistics at seven widths and two smoothing levels.

Seed-42, 10-fold residual XGBoost models at depths 3 and 5, plus LightGBM.

Seed-2027, 10-fold CatBoost models at depths 5 and 7, with 13 additional token/context categorical features.

Fold-local target statistics, with five inner folds for training-row encodings.

Initial training logits generated through three inner GLM fits, with feature statistics recomputed inside those fits.

5. Validation: useful checks and their limits

Target-dependent feature calculations were kept inside the relevant training folds. Training-row statistics and teacher/initial-margin predictions used inner-fold computations where required. Submission IDs, row order, missing values, finite predictions and the [0,1] range were checked before upload.

The late stacking experiments also used repeated held-out meta comparisons and paired comparisons with a refitted baseline combiner.

However, the base models were not retrained for every meta holdout. Those checks therefore measure performance conditional on the available OOF library. Reusing the same labels for many model and combiner experiments also creates selection optimism.

The full-library OOF values above should not be presented as independent estimates of the entire final pipeline. Early OOF measurements, test-only public blends and late meta scores did not all share one validation protocol.

Another observation was that prediction diversity shrank after fold averaging: for one pair of GLM streams, the standard deviation of their rank difference fell by roughly 86% from OOF to test. Consequently, a useful OOF difference need not remain equally large in test predictions.

7. Takeaways

Represent the data-generation traces. Exact numerical values, digits, token groups and frequencies were valuable alongside raw magnitudes.

Build a strong prior, then learn its residual. Logistic initialization was a useful foundation for shallow boosting models.

Evaluate contribution to the existing ensemble. A stronger single model is not automatically a useful stack member.

Keep validation claims precise. Conditional meta checks, full-library OOF replay and fully nested validation are different measurements.

Treat tiny public changes cautiously. Some public gains reversed on private, while several modest public improvements generalized better.

What Are We Predicting?

Line up all the people from "most likely to buy" to "least likely to buy" (our model decides the order).

Pick one real buyer and one real non-buyer at random.

AUC is the chance that the buyer is standing ahead of the non-buyer in our line.

A coin flip gets 0.5 and a perfect model gets 1.0. Our model gets about 0.946, which means it puts the buyer first about 95 times out of 100.

Hiding Our Score

A leaderboard is very motivating. When you see someone ahead of you, you work harder! We didn't want to give the other teams that extra encouragement for the entire month, so we hid our real score.

[图 7: upside_down.png]

The trick is upside-down predictions. Replace every prediction p with 1 - p, and the line of people is turned around, so AUC becomes 1 - AUC. A file that really scores 0.947 shows up as 0.053.

Kaggle's leaderboard only shows each team's best score, so our upside-down files never appeared.

We could still read our real score: 1 - 0.053 = 0.947.

So for most of the month, the leaderboard showed our second-day score (0.94672), while our real best was already 0.94701. On the last day we submitted our real final models. We jumped to 0.94705 on public, then 1st place on private.

Helpful Tips For Your Own AI Team

One fold file for everyone. Every agent trains every model on the same 5 folds (a saved folds.npy). Then any model from any agent can be compared fairly and added to the ensemble.

Judge a model by how much it helps the team, not by its own score.

If two friends always give the same answer, asking both of them doesn't help. A friend who thinks differently does.

Paul shared a great rule of thumb here: look for models scoring above 0.94 that are less than 0.99 correlated with what you already have.

Make agents check each other. Before trusting a big improvement, ask a second agent to review the first agent's code. Fresh eyes catch leaks and bugs.

Write handoff notes (see "The Baton Is The Notes"). They help the next agent, and they help you next month.

Trust your CV, not the public LB. We were 3rd on public and 1st on private. Team Alicia's writeup has a great experiment showing what happens when you chase the public LB.

Part 4: My Advice

4.1 - Do Not Blind Blend If You Care About the Final Result

Blind blending a few models is not that harmful if your candidates are honest.
