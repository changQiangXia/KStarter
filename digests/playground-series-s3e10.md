### A compilation of winning solutions from the first 10 episodes

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e10/discussion/394981

See part 2 where I organize the below ideas into structured topics.

You probably have seen some of the 'Collection of helpful ideas' posts that I put together in every episode. Those summaries are separate and don't include the winning solutions that are posted a couple of days after the competition ends. By putting solutions together, not just from one episode but all, valuable lessons will be learned and winning strategies might be emerged.

I try to add a tag line that is unique for each episode. Let's get started.

Episode 1 - California Housing - RMSE metric.

Spatial feature engineering is the key here. All winners' solutions used geo-features generated from longitude and lattiude such as distance to city or the coast line.

1st place used auto ML framework AutoGluon. @kirderf

2nd place used an ensemble of GBDT methods and NN using keras_tuner. @jcerpentier

Episode 2 - Stroke Prediction - AUC metric.

Handling categorical features and a huge leaderboard shakeup.

1st place: one hot encoding giving better performance compared to other encodings, KNeighborsRegressor was used to impute BMI, compute risk factors, average ensemble of 5 best xgboost models. Randomized search over a hand-coded set of ranges. @kirkdco

5th place replaced rare categories (unknown, other) with appropriate values, added new features and select with RecursiveFeatureElimanation. Ensemble of logistic regression and GBDT models. @jcaliz

6th place made an extensive use of Optuna. He used Optuna to tune hyperparameters for LGBM, XGB, CB, Lasso and NN. Then he used Optuna to find weights to blend the 5 models. @viktortaran

8th place used a single LGBM model with ordinal encoding of a few categorical features. @k0takahashi

Epsiode 3 - Employee Attrition - AUC metric.

Integer datatype doesn't guarantee numerical features: do your EDA.

1st place added risk factors features and built a weighted ensemble of XGBoost + LightGBM + CatBoost. @bcruise

8th place reused the approach that won the first place in the previous episode with a focus on categorical encoding, chosing an appropriate choice of winsorization, ordinal and one hot encoding depending on particular features. @kirkdco

Episode 4 - Credit Card Fraud - AUC metric

Highly imbalanced data.

7th place was a weighted ensemble of 3 GBDT models, but interestingly each model was trained with a different cross validation strategy. @ramjasmaurya

10th place transformed time to hour and day and used division features from @sergiosaharovskiy. Model is CatBoost with custom focal loss tuned with Optuna TPESampler. @danmer414

Episode 5 - Wine Quality - Quadratic Weighted Kappa metric

An uncommon metric and the triumph or regression model for ordinal classification.

1st place didn't use original data and feature engineering! It used RAPIDS XGBoost - GPU. Optimize class cutoff: this is special, use regression for classification task and optimize to find threshold. @rapela

2nd place: xgboost with manual hyperparameter tuning, different testing folds in StratifiedKFold. @nhopet

3rd place was an excellent way of using public notebooks: a weighted ensemble of 6 public notebooks. @group16

4th place was a ridge regression based on a stack of 25 first level models types such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost with differently tuned hyperparameters and minimal feature engineering. @craigmthomas

Episode 6 - Paris Housing - RMSE metric

Noisy data, outliers, fake postcode - huge leaderboard shakeup except for one.

1st place didn't provide a solution.

2nd place ranked 1st in public and 2nd in private! The solution predicted price with 3 linear regression models, each for a newly created category high/medium/low price per square. The category was generated with an extra classification model. @johng29

3rd place used three models (CAT, XGB, RF) for the first level and LGBM as a metamodel. Model parameters were tuned by Optuna. @viktortaran

4th place applied a unique cross validation scheme based on PCA together with a simple random forest model. @icfoer

5th place built seperate models for different ranges of made feature and cross validate with multiple random seeds stratified k-fold. @mscgeorges

6th place was a simple manually tuned Extreme Gradient Boost Regressor. @iamvaibhav100

8th place: a single tuned XGB model with common FE such as scaling, remove perfect collinearity, cardinality reduction, power transform etc. @kirderf

9th place used AutoGluon but within a cross validation, so final prediction is an average of k models. @brendanmoore14

Episode 7 - Reservation Cancellation - AUC metric

No feature engineering - data leakage exploit.

1st place handled data leakage extremely well: pairs of observation that have opposite labels in train only, test only and train-test. Final model is an average of XGB and LGBM models with different categorical encodings and proportion of original dataset. @hardyxu52

2nd place was a hill climbing ensemble of 16 LGBM models with a large (20-30) number of validation folds. @jcaliz

3rd place implemented a 2-level stacked model including 123 GBDT models with different hypeparameters for the first level and a NN for the second level. @craigmthomas

4th place was a (sicpy minimize) weighted ensemble of Optuna-tuned Lightgbm, XGBoost, CatBoost. @ryanbarretto

9th place was a hill climbing ensemble of 7 XGB models with target encoding for categorical features. @sergiosaharovskiy

Episode 8 - Gemstone Price - RMSE metric

Getting overwhelmed with parallel competitions and running out of taglines.

1st place was a 2-level stacked model of Optuna-tuned models with two transformed datasets. Used RFECV to select a smaller set of models. @thierryneusius

2nd place: a 2-level stacked model similar to the previous episode. @craigmthomas

3rd place generated a large number of datasets with different combination of columns and used them to train an ensemble. @peridon

6th place was a weighted ensemble of AutoGluon and autoxgb. @kirderf

7th place was a unique approach of generating features. It used 7 high-scoring public notebooks to obtain predictions for the test. It then used the highest-scoring notebook to generate test predictions and used them as groundtruth to retrain 7 earlier models to 'predict' price of the training set. Those 7 predictions are used as 7 additional features. @jbomitchell

8th place was a masterpiece with lots of detailed tuning with domain knowledge. @sergiosaharovskiy

10th place used tidymodels package from R to build an ensemble without any feature engineering or duplicate handling. @nicobarea

### A structured review of winning solutions from the first 10 episodes

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e10/discussion/395484

Based on the detailed solutions from A compilation of winning solutions from the first 10 episodes, I extract the main points and organize them into topics.

Data

In every episode, the competition data is generated from some particular dataset. Adding the original data could help improve the model. There are different ways to use the original dataset: 

adding all

adding all except the part that are very different from the train set

adding just the positive class if data is imbalanced

However, the original data needs to be excluded from the validation fold to make the CV more aligned with the LB.

Adversarial validation can be performed to check if two datasets are similar. It's normally used to check if train and test sets come from the same distribution. But in our case, it can also be used to check if the original data is similar enough to be included in the training.

Standard data preprocessing includes missing values handling, standardization, outlier removal, (quasi-)duplicate detection. See Episode 7 - 1st place for an example of exploiting the leakage of labels in duplicate data @hardyxu52.

Techniques to handle imbalanced data, such as oversampling, oversampling, SMOTE, don't seem to work well.

Feature engineering

In Episode 1, it's essential to perform spatial feature engineering to generate relevant features (distance to city, distance to the coastline, etc.) based on raw longitude and latitude. However, in a few episodes, it seems not necessary to perform feature engineering - 1st place Episode 5 didn't use original data and addtional features @rapela.

Feature engineering can be more important for linear models compared to tree-based models @ambrosm.

There are many different ways to encode categorical features besides one hot encoding: ordinal, weight of evidence, target, etc. Even though, simple one hot encoding seems to work well.

Integer datatype doesn't guarantee numerical feature, it can represent categories.

Besides adding new features, dropping ones that don't contribute is also important, such as using RecursiveFeatureElimanation.

Cross validation

The #1 advice is to trust your local CV because the public LB only takes 20% of the test data. 

Standard approach is k-fold (regression) or stratified k-fold ( classification) CV. Common values are 5 or 10 but some solutions use 20-30 @jcaliz.

A pretty unique approach to perform CV based on clustering of PCA projection - 4th place Episode 6 @icfoer.

Modelling

AutoML framework like AutoGluon seems work very well: 1st place Episode 1 @kirderf, 9th place Episode 6 @brendanmoore14, 6th place Episode 8 @kirderf.

tidymodels package if you're an R fan - 10th place Episode 9 @nicobarea.

How to tune hyperparameters?

Hyperparmeters are often tuned by Optuna but an initial manual play with the values is recommended to get a good understanding.

A simpler approach to tune is using randomized search - 1st place Episode 2 @kirkdco.

Remember to test if the found hyperparmeters work well with different random seeds.

The most common winning formula found in every episode is a weighted ensemble. However, an ensemble needs to be diverse:

Model-wise: don't focus only on boosting models, a linear model (5th place Episode 2 @jcaliz) or neural network (2nd place Episode 1 @jcerpentier) might also help.

CV-wise: 7th place Episode 4 trained each model in an ensemble with a different cross validation strategy @ramjasmaurya.

Data-wise: 3rd place Episode 8 generated a large number of datasets with different combination of columns and used them to train an ensemble @peridon.

A just ensemble of notebooks with high score - 3rd place Episode 5 @group16.

How to find the weights of an ensemble?

Just simple mean.

Optuna is also commonly used to tune the weights of the ensemble - 6th place Episode 2 @viktortaran.

sicpy minimize - 4th place Episode 7 @ryanbarretto

hill climbing - 2nd place Episode 7 @jcaliz.

2-level stacking: use predictions from 1st level models as features to build a higher level model - 2nd place Episode 8 @craigmthomas.

It's not uncommon that a single model performs better than a complex ensemble:

LGBM - 8th place Episode 2 @k0takahashi.

XGBoost - 2nd place Episode 5 @nhopet.

Random forest - 4th place Episode 6 @icfoer.

Extreme Gradient Boost Regressor - 6th place Episode 6 @iamvaibhav100.

Generalized Addictive Models - 1st place Episode 10 - this is the key in Episode 10 @seascape.

Unique techniques:

2nd place Episode 6 predicted price with 3 linear regression models, each for a newly created category high/medium/low price per square. The category was generated with an extra classification model @johng29.

5th place Episode 6 built separate models for different ranges of made feature and cross validate with multiple random seeds stratified k-fold @mscgeorges.

7th place Episode 8 used 7 high-scoring public notebooks to obtain predictions for the test. It then used the highest-scoring notebook to generate test predictions and used them as groundtruth to retrain 7 earlier models to 'predict' price of the training set. Those 7 predictions are used as 7 additional features @jbomitchell.

### About Pulsars, Neutrons Stars, Quasars, Supernovas and Magnetars.

"Pulsars are neutron stars that emit rapid, periodic pulses of radiation. A neutron star is an extremely compact ball of neutrons created from the central core of a star that collapsed under gravity during a supernova explosion."

"Pulsars, in turn, are neutron stars with very intense magnetic fields, which rotate at very high frequencies and emit radiation. Thus, all pulsars are neutron stars, but not all neutron stars are pulsars."

"The neutron star retains much of its original angular momentum, and since it has a much smaller radius than its parent, it forms with a high rate of rotation. … This causes the beam to be visible once for every rotation of the neutron star, leading to the “pulsating” nature of its appearance."

"Quasars are very particular types of galaxy nuclei, they are the so-called "active nuclei" whose emitted light is not the sum of the lights of the stars that make up the nucleus itself. … Pulsars, on the other hand, are neutron stars rotating at a high rotational speed, which emit radio pulses (hence their name)."

"Planetary nebulae are formed when a star that has between 0,8 and 8 solar masses (M⊙) runs out of its nuclear fuel. Above the limit of 8 M⊙ the star would explode, originating a supernova."

"Quasars are the biggest energy emitters in the Universe. A single quasar can achieve luminosities thousands of times greater than that of a galaxy."

### 1st place solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e10/discussion/396345

My solution is here. I used GAM, however, with two additional features created with XGB and LASSO. That is, the final model is ensemble, although I think a little less "classic". Importantly, these additional features were not so much important here. The GAM model was my first approach, and without these features I was getting only slightly worse results. 

I chose this model for a few reasons:

We have very few variables that are already high-level features. Feature engineering is basically impossible, it's just a matter of finding the right relationship.

Boosting is based on trees, which, in my opinion, are not able to find "real" relationships. Because real world relationships are rather continuous in nature --- and decision trees only approximate it. Of course, they are great and you can approximate almost anything (e.g., very high-level interactions), but the world does not work in that way.

GAM has built-in regularization; it selects parameters by performing internal crossvalidation. This makes it very convenient, and I could freely add interactions and features created from the XGB and LASSO model.

### Don't use the wrong boilerplate code

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e10/discussion/393073

Some public notebooks in this competition show confusion matrices and roc curves. In a logloss competition, these metrics aren't helpful. In a logloss competition, you need to look at the calibration plot, and perhaps at a histogram of the predictions:

[图 1: inbox%2F7917824%2F6388e8c993573dc0eba3c7c7b6c5d202%2Fcalib(1).png]

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 4))
ax = plt.subplot(1, 2, 1) # calibration
plt.title('Calibration')
CalibrationDisplay.from_predictions(train[target], oof, n_bins=100, strategy='quantile', ax=ax)
plt.subplot(1, 2, 2) # histogram
plt.title('Histogram')
plt.hist(oof, bins=100)
plt.show()
