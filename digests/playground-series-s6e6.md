### 8th Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e6/discussion/716756

Wow, what a shakeup. For this month, I just want to share a few points I found useful:

Trust the CV

An easy way to checking your "real" score in the public leaderboard is to compare to constant winners like Chris, Optimistix, MILANFX. Judging from their positions I concluded that top 100 on public leaderboard are all overfitting (lol) and decided to basically ignore it for the duration of competition. Instead, I relied solely on CV score.

Choice of Weighted Log Loss as CV metric, and Some Maths

This is more non-conventional and I am not 100% sure whether it's correct. At the start of the competition, I discussed with Fable (yes) and decided to use weighted log loss as my CV metric for the entire competition, rather than balanced accuracy. The reason being that its less noisy, and actually somehow equivalent to balanced accuracy. See more details below (generated by Opus 4.8, I'm not that good at maths, also apologies that inline latex is not rendering).

Point #2 is very important. It means that I can train models that do not support weighted training, and divide their final probabilities by the sample prior ([0.653818, 0.202899, 0.143283]) and realign to get a "proper" probability distribution in the same space as those trained weighted.

Notation

$K$ classes; label $Y\in{1,\dots,K}$, features $X$. Class priors $\pi_c=\Pr(Y=c)$, empirical $\hat\pi_c=N_c/N$.

Posterior $\eta_c(x)=\Pr(Y=c\mid X=x)$.

A classifier predicts $\hat y(x)=\arg\max_c q_c(x)$ for some score vector $q(x)\in\Delta^{K-1}$.

1. Class-weighted log-loss is a proper surrogate for balanced accuracy

Balanced accuracy is ordinary accuracy on the class-balanced data. Define per-class recall $\mathrm{recall}_c=\Pr(\hat y(X)=c\mid Y=c)$, so

$$\mathrm{BA}(\hat y)=\frac1K\sum_{c=1}^K \mathrm{recall}_c .$$

Introduce sample weights $w(c)=\tfrac{1}{K\pi_c}$ and the tilted distribution $\tilde P(x,c)\propto w(c)\,P(x,c)$. Two facts follow immediately: the tilted class marginals are uniform, $\tilde\pi_c=1/K$, and the within-class law is unchanged, $\tilde P(x\mid c)=P(x\mid c)$ (the weight depends on $c$ only). Hence

$$\mathrm{Acc}_{\tilde P}(\hat y)=\sum_c \tilde\pi_c\,\Pr(\hat y=c\mid Y=c)=\frac1K\sum_c \mathrm{recall}_c=\mathrm{BA}(\hat y).$$

So balanced accuracy is just accuracy measured on the reweighted distribution where every class is equally likely — equivalently, weighted 0–1 accuracy with weights $\propto 1/\pi_{y_i}$.

The BA-optimal decision divides the posterior by the prior. Accuracy under any law is maximized by that law's Bayes rule, $\hat y(x)=\arg\max_c \tilde\eta_c(x)$. By Bayes' theorem on the tilted law,

$$\tilde\eta_c(x)=\frac{\tilde\pi_c\,P(x\mid c)}{\sum_k \tilde\pi_k\,P(x\mid k)}=\frac{P(x\mid c)}{\sum_k P(x\mid k)}\ \propto\ \frac{\eta_c(x)}{\pi_c},$$

so the balanced-accuracy-optimal classifier is

$$\hat y^\star(x)=\arg\max_c \frac{\eta_c(x)}{\pi_c}.$$

Weighted log-loss elicits exactly $\tilde\eta$. The class-weighted cross-entropy of a probabilistic predictor $q$ is

$$\mathcal L_w(q)=\mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim P}\big[w(Y)\,(-\log q_Y(X))\big],\qquad w(c)=\tfrac{1}{K\pi_c}.$$

Minimize it pointwise at each $x$ over the simplex:

$$\min_{q(x)}\ -\sum_c \frac{\eta_c(x)}{K\pi_c}\,\log q_c(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\ \textstyle\sum_c q_c(x)=1.$$

The Lagrangian stationarity condition gives $q_c(x)\propto \eta_c(x)/\pi_c$, i.e.

$$q^\star_c(x)=\frac{\eta_c(x)/\pi_c}{\sum_k \eta_k(x)/\pi_k}=\tilde\eta_c(x).$$

Because log-loss is a strictly proper scoring rule this minimizer is unique. Its argmax is precisely $\hat y^\star$.

Conclusion. Minimizing class-weighted log-loss yields a predictor whose argmax maximizes balanced accuracy. The two are not the same functional — a model can win on weighted log-loss yet lose on BA on any finite sample — but they share the same population optimizer at the level of the induced decision, and weighted log-loss is the vastly better thing to optimize and to validate on:

BA depends on the predictor only through its argmax, so it is piecewise-constant and non-differentiable (no gradient signal) and improper — it ignores calibration and margins, treating a confident-correct prediction and a barely-correct one identically.

Consequently BA is high-variance to estimate: flipping a handful of near-tie rows moves it. That is exactly the leaderboard noise we saw.

Weighted log-loss keeps the full probability vector, is strictly convex in $q$, rewards calibration, and has its optimum at the same decision. So we cross-validate on $-$(weighted log-loss), treat it as ground truth, and take the argmax only once, at the very end.

2. Unweighted training + divide-by-prior reaches the same balanced posterior

Section 1 showed that everything the metric wants is the tilted posterior $\tilde\eta_c(x)\propto\eta_c(x)/\pi_c$, obtained there by baking $1/\pi_c$ into the loss. Here we reach the identical object by a post-hoc correction instead — which is what you must do for learners that cannot accept sample weights (in our stack, RealMLP and TabPFN). Its equivalence to balanced accuracy is then inherited directly from Section 1.

Unweighted training recovers the true posterior. Ordinary (unweighted) log-loss is strictly proper, so its population minimizer is

$$\arg\min_q\ \mathbb E_{P}\big[-\log q_Y(X)\big]=\eta(\cdot).$$

An unweighted model therefore produces calibrated estimates of the natural posterior $\eta_c(x)=\Pr(Y=c\mid x)$.

Dividing by the prior converts $\eta$ into $\tilde\eta$. By Bayes, $\eta_c(x)=\pi_c\,P(x\mid c)/P(x)$, so

$$\frac{\eta_c(x)}{\pi_c}=\frac{P(x\mid c)}{P(x)}\ \propto\ P(x\mid c),$$

the class-conditional likelihood, up to the $c$-independent factor $P(x)$. Renormalizing over $c$,

$$\frac{\eta_c(x)/\pi_c}{\sum_k \eta_k(x)/\pi_k}=\frac{P(x\mid c)}{\sum_k P(x\mid k)}=\tilde\eta_c(x).$$

So dividing an unweighted model's output by the prior and renormalizing yields exactly the balanced posterior $\tilde\eta$ — the same object elicited by weighted log-loss in Section 1, hence the same BA-optimal argmax.

Two routes, one target.

Route A (weighted). Loss reweighted by $1/\pi_c$; the model outputs $\tilde\eta$ directly.

Route B (unweighted + divide-by-prior). The model outputs $\eta$; multiply by $1/\hat\pi_c$ (with $\hat\pi_c=N_c/N$) and renormalize to get $\tilde\eta$.

At the population level they are exactly equal. On finite data with finite-capacity, possibly misspecified models they differ slightly: Route A spends capacity matching the balanced conditional directly (rare-class examples get up-weighted gradients during fitting), while Route B fits the natural distribution and corrects afterward. For a well-calibrated learner they coincide; under misspecification Route A can be preferable because it optimizes closer to the metric. We used Route A wherever the learner supports sample weights (the GBMs, logistic regression) and Route B where it does not (RealMLP, TabPFN), so that every base model feeds probabilities on the same $\tilde\eta$ scale into the stacker.

Why this is the Bayes-optimal correction, not a trick. The rule $\arg\max_c \eta_c(x)/\pi_c$ is the classical prior-correction / threshold-moving result for imbalanced classification: dividing by the prior shifts each class's decision boundary to offset its base rate, so a rare class no longer has to clear the same posterior bar as a common one. Balanced accuracy is precisely the metric that asks for that shift — which is why the prior division falls out of the mathematics rather than being tuned in.

Final Submission

My final submission is a logistic regression trained on 27 stackers and 60 base models, based on Chris's GPU stacker notebook (big thanks to Chris and all the notebook authors!). I also tried ensembling with hill climbing in logit space, but it is not as good as logistic regression.

### 1st Place - "Mission 300+" Accomplished!

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e6/discussion/717510

This competition began well enough, but about two weeks in, the blind blenders started climbing the public LB with a vengeance, and any steady improvement in ranks based on CV-LB agreement became nearly impossible. At this point, I set myself the challenge of resisting the temptation to blend with a high scoring public nb just to see my name high up the list, and instead hold steady with the right approach even if I kept sliding down the public LB, potentially setting myself up for a big climb up the private LB. It was amazing to eventually manage the jump from 344 to 1 - a second consecutive #1 finish is especially gratifying. Before I go on, I'd like to thank those who generously shared their code and/or insights, including (but not limited to) :

@cdeotte, @omidbaghchehsaraei, @yekenot, @masayakawamata, @fachri00

Now for the details:

It was easy to get quickly started - with another 3-class, Balanced Accuracy challenge just 2 months after a similar task in April, there was a lot of readymade code to quickly adapt and reuse. Coupled with the generous gift of several great notebooks and a fast Logistic Regression stacker by @cdeotte, I quickly had a decent ensemble of 30+ models humming, with a reasonable agreement between CV and LB. But soon the public LB became inundated with a deluge of blind blending notebooks - the seemingly distant barrier of 0.97200 on the public LB was swiftly overcome, and scores kept inching northwards. Around the midway mark, I reached CV = 0.970506, LB = 0.97171 with 49 models. This proved to be hard to improve upon, and I began to see that the ceiling with a proper CV framework was within LB = 0.97200, and perhaps CV 0.97080, making it extremely unlikely that the top LB scores were achieved with anything but blind blending and LB probing. There were a subset of users and notebooks with particularly amusing statements and claims, declaring premature victory, accusing others of jealousy, claiming "advanced" classification, etc. Once such notebooks are available, it's tempting to blend your own models with them and achieve a score that keeps your name high up the public LB. Of course, that usually results in a crash on the private LB, as I learned pretty early on. So the question then becomes whether you want to do it anyway, but choose your final two submissions based on CV-LB agreement - tricky in case you forget to choose the right final submissions, but can be pulled off.

I decided to set myself a different goal this time - of resisting the temptation, staying calm as I inevitably fell down the public LB, and hopefully reap the eventual benefits of a robust approach to climb up the private LB. I was visualizing a jump of > 300 ranks, but of course it wasn't entirely in my hands - if my best public LB happened to be in the top 300, then so be it. As I fell to 340+ on the final day, I was fairly confident of achieving the goal, since I only had to place in the top 40 or so (the higher the better, of course). Jumping all the way to #1 was of course the icing on the cake!

A few technical notes:

Model Diversity : As usual, I tried many diverse models and approaches, including XGB, LGBM, CatBoost, RealMLP, TabM, ET, HGB, RF, YDF, FT-Transformer, TabNet, and many more. I took advantage of AutoML libraries like Autogluon, Light AutoML, FLAML and PyTabKit. My best scoring solo was a CatBoost based on @cdeotte's CAT-v3, with additional feature engineering and HP tweaking. Claude Code made it easier than ever to keep adding new, diverse models.

Feature Engineering : Once again, I created several notebooks with "Heavy FE" of the kind seen in excellent notebooks by @cdeotte, @masayakawamata, @mahoganybuttstrings and others in past playground episodes, with 200-400 features; and also reused excellent features from others.

 Ensemblers : I tried many, including Logistic Regression (LR) with Logits, XGB, LGB, CatBoost, Simple MLP, RealMLP, FLAML & Autogluon. LR with Logits & MLP were the best overall.

 Final Push The midway mark of CV = 0.970506, LB = 0.97171 wasn't hard to beat, but CV = 0.97060, LB= 0.97180 sort of became the wall. No matter how many new models I added, those two numbers could not be beat. New models would come and displace old ones in importance, but the overall score remained within the above limits. As ensemble size grew towards 100 and beyond, performance actually deteriorated. So I returned to earlier points a few times, and resumed adding models from there, rather than keep growing ensemble size to 150 and beyond. An interesting pattern which emerged was that MLP often achieved better CV, but lower LB compared to LR-Logits. In the end, I had two LR-Logits ensembles which scored 0.97179 on the LB, with CV 0.970510 (49 models) and 0.97573 (78 models), respectively. The best MLP ensemble had a higher CV at 0.970598, but LB of "only" 0.97172 (89 models). And then there was my best LB submission of 0.97188, which was achieved via voting over 3 submissions - I decided that it was just a lucky combination which benefited from getting a few extra cases of the rarest class right, and was unlikely to translate to a better score on the private LB. In the end, I chose the LR-Logits submission/ensemble with the higher CV among the two that had the highest LB score, and the MLP one with the highest CV. It was the MLP submission that won me the competition - a classic case of the Kaggle wisdom of "Trust your CV" paying off yet again.

### Formulae for spectral_type and galaxy_population

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e6/discussion/703535

While the competition dataset has dropped some features from the "original" dataset, it also contains additional features spectral_type and galaxy_population not present in the "original" dataset. If you tried to append the "original" dataset to the train set during training, you would encounter difficulty.

It appears that spectral_type and galaxy_population are post-synthesis feature engineering and are the results of simple thresholding. You can apply the same feature engineering to the "original" dataset before appending.

def spectral_type(g, r):
 return pd.cut(
 r-g, 
 [-np.inf, -1, -0.5, 0, np.inf],
 labels=['M', 'G/K', 'A/F', 'O/B']
 ).astype(str)

def galaxy_population(u, r):
 return pd.cut(
 u-r, 
 [-np.inf, 2.2, np.inf],
 labels=['Blue_Cloud', 'Red_Sequence']
 ).astype(str)

X = pd.concat([train, test])
assert spectral_type(X.g, X.r).equals(X.spectral_type)
assert galaxy_population(X.u, X.r).equals(X.galaxy_population)
