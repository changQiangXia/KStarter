## write-up 正文（6 篇）

### 1st Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/chaii-hindi-and-tamil-question-answering/discussion/287923

Thanks to Kaggle and the hosts for this competition! Thanks to everyone in the community I engaged with in the forums and discussions, I learned a lot from you.

CV Setup

When I'm writing this summary, I only know my public LB rank, so this paragraph may turn bitter. I decided to fully trust public LB in this competition and didn't even track local CV. The train dataset was small and confirmed to be noisy, while the public LB was bigger and contained 3-way annotations, which meant higher quality. I expected a higher correlation between public and private LB than between my local CV and private LB.

Architecture

All my models are standard QA models (AutoModelForQuestionAnswering) and the postprocessing is directly reapplied from the HF example notebook by Sylvain Gugger that I also used in my public EDA. I used XLM-Roberta Large, MURIL Large and Rembert, and blended the predictions from several models per backbone. In the next stage, I used majority voting at word level across the 3 backbones to decide which words will make it to the final prediction.

For diversity, I submitted another ensemble without blending single models, only doing majority voting. It had worse score on public LB, but same on private - both my chosen subs are .787 private. My best private sub is .796. I also have a single model/fold (MURIL) that scored .802 on public LB and .783 on private.

Data Recipes

I started the competition, like most of other participants, with XLM-Roberta Large pretrained by Deepset on English SQUAD and finetuned on chaii data. Later, I started to pretrain my own backbone models (I mean QA pretraining, not MLM). I focused on TyDi dataset, because the host mentioned that chaii data was collected in a similar way. I went through several experiments with different pretraining and finetuning protocols and the key insight was that the performance of pretrained backbone often exceeded its finetuned version. Based on this observation, I decided to drop the 2-stage approach and train my models in 1-stage. However, I wanted to control the order in which I provide different datasets to the model. I call this data recipes because it feels like cooking. 

Here is an example of a data recipe that I used in my final experiments: 

Take the English, Bengali and Telugu subset of TyDi dataset and 2/3 of chaii train dataset. Tokenize it with sequence length 256 and remove 90% of negative examples (negative sampling). Add tokenized English SQUAD and Hindi parts of MLQA and XQUAD. Shuffle all together and put aside. 

Repeat step 1, but replace English SQUAD with subset of Natural Questions. Use sequece length 384 for tokenization (progressive resizing, see below). 

Concatenate the data collected in steps 1 and 2. 

Train. 

Some more details:

Because I moved the data combination/shuffling logic into the dataset, I used sequential sampler and 1 epoch for training. HuggingFace datasets library made this super easy. 

I used negative sampling, usually 0.1, for TyDi, chaii and NQ datasets because they came with longer contexts.

I initially experimented with some auto-translated datasets like Tamil SQUAD but they didn't help my performance, so at the end I only used original language datasets and those translated manually (Hindi MLQA/XQUAD). 

I tried various languages from TyDi and found that English/Bengali/Telugu worked best.

I wanted to add some more data, so I took Google Natural Questions that contained a short answer and trained a simple Roberta-base classifier to distinguish NQ data from TyDi English dataset. I selected a subset of NQ that was most similar to English TyDi. 

Since I was not tracking CV, I initiatially used all chaii data. Later on, I moved to a 3 fold setup - for diversity and for early stopping. If an interim checkpoint had a much better CV than the final checkpoint, I would usually test it on public LB. 

Image-Like Augmentations

This part is heavily inspired by fastai - during one of the Deep Learning courses @jhoward talked about his work transfering the pretraining method from Computer Vision to NLP (ULMFiT) and mentioned that there are many other opportunities by following this approach. I focused on the augmentation techniques. 

Random Cropping

This is a standard augmentation in CV, but I haven't seen it applied to NLP, although it fits perfectly here: we need to chunk a very long text into a fixed token length fragments. Rather than doing it once and showing the same examples in each epoch, why not make it a dynamic and random crop? Initially, I implemented a function that took a random crop with padding around the answer text for positive examples, and a random crop for negative examples. Once I moved to data recipes described above, I realized that the same can be simpler achieved by using a different stride and negative sampling every time I add a dataset to my recipe. 

Progressive Resizing

This is another trick that Jeremy teaches in the fastai lectures, applied commonly for images. In case of progressive resizing, I use different sequence lengths during training. E.g. start with 256, then move to 384, then move to 448. 

Cutout

In computer vision, we sometimes black out patches of image as augmentation. In NLP, we can do the same by replacing some tokens with [MASK]. I saw that @cdeotte used this technique in Tweet Sentiment competition. I used 0-10% cutout in my models. 

Fine-tuning

I didn't experiment too much with different hyperparameters for finetuning, I usually started with the defaults described in each backbone paper and often stuck with that. I did most of my experiments on the XLM-Roberta backbone. MURIL had the best performance on the public LB (I believe due to tokenization), although the training run sometimes collapsed (a restart usually helped). 

Here are my default hyperparameters for each backbone (batch size = 8):

XLM-Roberta: gradient accumulation: 4, learning rate: 1e-5, wd: 0.01

MURIL: gradient accumulation: 4, learning rate: 3e-5, wd: 0.01

Rembert: gradient accumulation: 16, learning rate: 1e-5, wd: 0.01

Post-processing

Other than the standard postprocessing (I didn't modify the default functions from the official HuggingFace QA notebook), there was an opportunity to improve predictions by addressing XLM-Roberta and Rembert tokenization issues. These models tend to merge some punctuation marks with subwords, while annotators often split them. I initially used @abhishek's method of simply stripping punctuation, and in the last day made some modifications similar to the method shared by @nbroad. I add dots after BC/AD, I add parenthesis if included within the stripped prediction etc. I didn't see an impact in my score by adjusting this, but my position shifted from 3 to 2 based on this postprocessing. This didn't work well on private LB though (-0.004). I didn't do any such postprocessing for MURIL because it handled it well with its tokenizer. 

Frameworks

I went all in HuggingFace in this challenge and can't imaging doing it another way. Other than AutoModelForQuestionAnswering, I used the HF Trainer initially, and later realized they have a QA version of the trainer, which has EM/F1 measures implemented for validation. I used EM/F1 for early stopping rather than observing the validation loss. Last but not least, HF datasets was a huge enabler for me, its caching works like magic.

Things that Didn't Work

Label Error Corrections

Quite early in the competition, I tried to correct the noisy labels by doing out of fold predictions, looking at the model errors and replacing the label with prediction if - based on Google Translate - I thought it's better than original. However, this degraded my performance on public LB and I dropped this. I missed the opportunity to correct the answer start, I only tried it in the last 2 days after @zacchaeus published his corrected dataset. The models trained with it appeared to perform better, but they didn't help my ensemble at the end. 

MT5

I was quite bullish on MT5, especially when I tried a pretrained checkpoint from HF Hub and it scored .713 without any finetuning. Also, it looks like MT5 XXL is SOTA for this type of challenges with limited data. However, the largest model I was able to run in Kaggle kernel was MT5-Large (is it possible to run a bigger one?). I got MT5-Large to train on Google Colab (thanks to AdaFactor) as I was travelling during this time, but I couldn't surpass the .713 score of the pretrained backbone. 

Jaccard Loss

I implemented my own version of jaccard loss (Lovasz-Hinge) but it didn't improve my performance, even when combined with CrossEntropy. It's very likely that I made some implementation error, if you'd like to take a look and give me feedback I'd be happy to share that implementation. 

XLNet Head

I also tried the XLNet head where end-logits are predicted by taking into account start-logits, using beam-search on inference. It didn't work too well for me, could be another implementation error. 

Tokenization

I didn't venture to tamper with the tokenizers, but I tried to split some punctuation marks before running the tokenizer - this didn't help my score either.

We think most of the top teams achieving >0.81 use the TyDi QA dataset. It may be due to the language similarity among Bengali, Telugu, Hindi, and Tamil. However, we suspect some possibility that some questions are shared even though they are translated to Hindi or Tamil since TyDi QA is owned by Google. This is just a guess.

Model Training

We just did 2 epochs of training with the whole data (no fold out) on the huggingface trainer. We do not distinguish between Hindi and Tamil. For more details, The training code should be included in each dataset. Please have a look.

Loss function

While the original cross-entropy score has the best performance, the loss function described in https://github.com/heartkilla/kaggle_tweet (used in the previous competition) worked to obtain more diverse models. In our kernel whose name ends "exp33" is trained with the "Jaccard-based Soft Labels Since Cross-Entropy".

Local Evaluation (CV) Strategy

Nothing. We tune everything based on Public LB. I did not even track local scores. This is the first time since I started Kaggle that I have not tracked local scores at all.

Post-processing

As shown in this kernel (https://www.kaggle.com/nbroad/chaii-qa-torch-5-fold-with-post-processing-765), post-processing has a large impact on this competition. Since the test data is annotated by 4 people, they are not very likely to forget the trailing periods like “​​ई.पू.”, i.e., answers should be in the most formal format. Besides, we processed the Devanagari numbers (०१२३४५६७८९). We replace them with Arabic numerals (0-9) if the selected answer consists only of Devanagari numbers. It seems like the annotators agreed that they use Arabic numerals when the answer is a year. Without the postprocessing, our score dropped to 0.806. So, it pushed our score by around 0.02.

### 5th Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/chaii-hindi-and-tamil-question-answering/discussion/288049

First of all thanks to Kaggle and the organizers for this competition. 

Data Strategy

The competition data was not up to the mark and thus, there was no way you could build a good CV that correlates with the leaderboard. Unclean public training data and clean hidden private test data only meant we could use either different models and combine them together or use a single strong model like muril-large and train it on a variety of datasets shared by others. We did both.

The first step was to divide the training data into 5 folds. After that, we trained our final models on subset of SQuAD v2, Google-translated SQuAD v2 shared by @tkm2261, all TyDi QA, hindi parts of MLQA, XQUAD and 5-10x oversampled Chaii dataset for each fold. We found that oversampling Chaii dataset helped us to improve CV and LB score. Please note that for CV calculation we used only the original validation folds.

All Models were trained for 1-2 epochs with early stopping based on CV Jaccard score with max_length = 384 and doc_stride = 128. We did try other lengths such as 400, 512 and other strides like 192 but they didn’t perform very well with our cross-validation strategy.

Models

XLM Roberta Large (Public LB - 0.800)

Muril Large (Public LB - 0.802)

RemBERT (Public LB - 0.803)

Ensemble

Since different models have different tokenizations, simple average ensembling was not possible so tried using prediction scores to select best predictions from different models. This gave us a little improvement.

The next thing we tried was to normalize the scores of all models to the same scale. We saw a little improvement using this too but we knew that this is not the best way to combine models.

The vanilla version failed as different models had different scales for logits. So to overcome this I came up with a custom softmax which could normalize the logits across different context splits. Base code for this is attached below:

def CustomSoftmax(examples, features, raw_predictions):
 all_start_logits, all_end_logits = raw_predictions

 # Build a map example to its corresponding features.
 example_id_to_index = {k: i for i, k in enumerate(examples["id"])}
 features_per_example = collections.defaultdict(list)
 for i, feature in enumerate(features):
 features_per_example[example_id_to_index[feature["example_id"]]].append(i)

 for example_index, example in enumerate(tqdm(examples)):
 feature_indices = features_per_example[example_index]
 start_logits_sum = 0
 end_logits_sum = 0

 logCs = None
 logCe = None

 # Find maximum logit value across context
 for feature_index in feature_indices:
 if logCs is None:
 logCs = all_start_logits[feature_index].max()
 else:
 logCs = max(logCs, all_start_logits[feature_index].max())

 if logCe is None:
 logCe = all_end_logits[feature_index].max()
 else:
 logCe = max(logCe, all_end_logits[feature_index].max())

 # Subtract maximum value among all logits before applying exponentiation for numerical stability
 # And take sum across all context splits
 for feature_index in feature_indices:
 all_start_logits[feature_index] -= logCs
 all_end_logits[feature_index] -= logCe
 start_logits_sum += np.exp(all_start_logits[feature_index]).sum()
 end_logits_sum += np.exp(all_end_logits[feature_index]).sum()

 # Softmax
 for feature_index in feature_indices:
 all_start_logits[feature_index] = np.exp(all_start_logits[feature_index])/start_logits_sum
 all_end_logits[feature_index] = np.exp(all_end_logits[feature_index])/end_logits_sum

 return all_start_logits, all_end_logits

This was the final idea we had in the last two days and it boosted our public score from 0.809 to 0.821!

Post-processing

We followed a similar preprocessing technique as used in public notebooks. Though we observed that removing a few of the symbols were hurting the performance so we decided to use a custom punctuations list ('!"#$&\'()*+,-/:;<=>?@[\\]^_`{|}~'). 

We also tried to solve the issue of tamil predictions as indicated by @jdoesv in https://www.kaggle.com/c/chaii-hindi-and-tamil-question-answering/discussion/264831 with the help of stemming tamil words in the answers it helped to boost Muril performance from 0.802 -> 0.804 on the public LB.

In the end, we didn’t add many rule-based post-processing as we thought this might overfit the public leaderboard. 

Our best private submission would have scored 0.779 but we did not select it. It’s funny why we didn’t select that submission. It had a small bug. However, even with that bug, that submission was our best private submission :)

We also had a single 5 fold muril-large model that would have placed us “in the money”.

You can also try one of our best muril-large models on Hugging Face Hub: https://huggingface.co/abhishek/muril-large-chaii

Final thoughts

It was a fun competition but we feel that it could have been better if the provided training data was better or at least similar to the hidden test dataset. We gave up many times.

We also tried a meta-model to re-rank the answers generated by different models but it was quite late in the competition (on the final day) and we didn’t have enough time to experiment with it further.

### 36th Place - Trust Your CV - Optimize PP

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/chaii-hindi-and-tamil-question-answering/discussion/287919

Thank you Kaggle and host for a fun and interesting competition.

Thank you @kishalmandal for the wonderful public notebooks. I joined a few days ago and immediately studied Kishal's top scoring public notebooks.

Final Submission 1 - Best LB

My first final submission was my maximum public LB of 0.795. I took Kishal's LB 0.792 public notebook here and changed the weights to 

start_logits = (start_logits1 + 0.85*start_logits2 + 0.85*start_logits3
 + start_logits4+ 1.7*start_logits5 )/5.4
end_logits = (end_logits1 + 0.85*end_logits2 + 0.85*end_logits3 
 + end_logits4 + 1.7*end_logits5)/5.4

This achieved public LB 0.795 which was 60th place on public LB. But when i calculated the local CV score, it was only CV 0.677. This was very concerning. (And as it turns out, this submission had private LB 0.718 and had 728th place 😮 so it was overfitting public LB)

Final Submission 2 - Best CV

For my second final submission, I downloaded @kishalmandal train notebooks here and here. I optimized the hyperparameters, learning rates, and learning schedules and achieved local CV 0.697.

Modify Boost Post Process +0.004!

Next, I adjusted the popular public post process. I changed the first line which uses logits to the second line which uses the Bayesian product of probabilities (and gives start position a slight emphasis over end position).

"score": start_logits[start_index] + end_logits[end_index]

from scipy.special import expit #THIS IS SIGMOID FUNCTION
"score": expit(1.2*start_logits[start_index]) \
 * expit(end_logits[end_index])

This boosted CV by +0.003, public LB by +0.003, and private LB by +0.004! 

Best Final Submission

My best final submission turned out to be my second submission which was my best CV and not my best LB. This model had CV 0.700, Public LB 0.784, and Private LB 0.744. Once again, "trust you CV" was the best strategy!

UPDATE - Post Comp Reflection

My solution is only XLM-Roberta. After reading top solutions, it appears that ensemble with Muril-Large (and maybe Rembert) was the secret sauce to achieving a higher LB score! And TydiQA external dataset.
