### Congratulations & Announcing Winners

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle-challenge-strategy/discussion/742692

Thank you to everyone who took part in the Pokémon TCG AI Battle Challenge.

In this Challenge, 6,807 teams competed in the Simulation Category, and 942 writeups were submitted to the Strategy Category.

We were impressed not only by the strength of the agents at the top of the Simulation Category, but also by how openly and thoughtfully teams shared their work. Many writeups reached a level of depth comparable to research papers, and many teams shared not only what worked, but also the approaches they tried along the way and ultimately decided not to use.

How the Strategy Category Was Judged

Each writeup was evaluated by the judges according to the Model, Deck, and Report criteria published in the competition rules.

Judges first scored submissions independently. They then discussed the strongest submissions together before reaching the final evaluation.

As part of that discussion, we considered not only the final Simulation rating, but also strength and stability over the course of the competition, as well as the originality of each team's Model, Deck, and overall approach, and the reasoning behind those choices.

Please note that we do not plan to publish individual judging scores or detailed score breakdowns.

What the Strongest Writeups Had in Common

Looking back at the writeups that scored highly across the Model, Deck, and Report criteria, we found several recurring qualities that stood out.

1. They used match results to drive the next round of improvement

The strongest teams did not rely on the leaderboard alone to guide development.

They had their own ways of evaluating changes, such as self-play arenas, leagues against frozen opponents, or fixed evaluation sets built around specific opponents. They used these environments to identify weaknesses, make changes to their model or deck, and then check whether those changes actually improved performance before submitting again.

Many writeups analyzed matchup results, differences between going first and second, or changes in rating over time. What was sometimes missing, however, was what the team actually changed as a result of that analysis.

The analysis itself was useful, but the strongest writeups made the connection between analysis and the next improvement clear.

2. They made it possible to see what was actually working

We also rated highly the writeups that went beyond reporting the final model's performance and showed what had been tried along the way, and how much individual components contributed.

Examples included comparisons between general and specialized policies, search versus no search, performance differences between the two submitted decks, or changes in rating and win rate over the course of development.

This did not require a large-scale ablation study. Even a single comparison with one component removed can make it much easier for the reader to understand whether a particular idea was actually helping.

3. Many of the strongest writeups also discussed approaches that did not work

Many of the top writeups did not only describe the methods that made it into the final submission. They also mentioned ideas they had tried and ultimately decided not to use.

These varied from team to team and included approaches such as end-to-end deck construction, league training, value-based MCTS, look-ahead search, and critic-based reranking.

There is no need to document every failed experiment. But even a short explanation of what was tried and why it was not retained helps the reader understand how the final approach was reached.

4. They clearly explained the deck's game plan and the role of key cards

For the Deck criterion, we focused less on how extensively the full card list was documented and more on whether the writeup clearly communicated what the deck was trying to do.

It was not necessary to explain all 60 cards in detail. What mattered was that the overall game plan was understandable, and that the role of key cards was explained — especially when a card had been included to answer a particular matchup, threat, or line of play.

A card list on its own gives limited insight into the reasoning behind the deck. Even when the deck performed well, it was difficult to fully evaluate the deck-building decisions if the strategic intent was not clear.

5. They used figures and charts effectively

The strongest writeups also made effective use of figures, charts, and other visual evidence.

This did not mean using a large number of visuals. In many cases, a few well-chosen figures were enough — for example, rating progression, matchup win rates, or a diagram of the training or inference pipeline.

When the claims made in the text could also be supported by numbers or figures, the writeup became much easier to follow.

What Was Missing from Some Otherwise Strong Writeups

There were also several recurring patterns in writeups that contained interesting work but were harder to evaluate highly.

1. The analysis stopped at the diagnosis

Some writeups identified issues such as weak matchups, differences between going first and second, or periods where the rating dropped, but did not explain what was changed in response.

The analysis itself was valuable, but what we wanted to understand was the full loop: what was observed, what was changed, and whether the change helped.

2. Only the final result was visible

In some cases, the writeup explained that imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and search had been combined, and then reported the final result.

What was harder to understand was how much each component contributed or how the agent changed over the course of development.

Even a small amount of information about how the system evolved makes it much easier to understand the final method.

3. The deck's strategic intent was unclear

Some writeups included the card list but gave little explanation of the deck's game plan or the role of its key cards.

For example, simply stating that a card such as Dragapult was included tells the reader much less than explaining that it was included to stop a particular line of play in a specific matchup.

That extra context helps the reader understand the reasoning behind the deck rather than only seeing the final list.

These were not additional judging criteria beyond those published in the competition rules. Rather, they were recurring characteristics we observed in submissions that communicated their Model, Deck, and Report particularly well.

Once again, thank you to everyone who participated, experimented, shared their work, and helped make this Challenge possible. We would also like to thank the Kaggle team for their support throughout the competition.

Congratulations to the winning teams, and to the entire community for taking on such a challenging competition.

The top 8 teams will be invited to the Second Round. Teams ranked 9th and below may be invited if one of the advancing teams is unable to participate.

The Second Round will be streamed on YouTube, and we look forward to seeing how the strategies developed during this Challenge evolve in the next stage.

### Welcome to the Pokémon TCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy Category Competition!

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle-challenge-strategy/discussion/708588

We are excited to launch the Pokémon TCG AI Battle Strategy Category competition alongside the Simulation Category competition.

This Strategy Category competition focuses on exploring ideas and insights behind building competitive AI Training agents for the Pokémon Trading Card Game (TCG). Participants are encouraged to analyze the strategies, learning methods, and design decisions that contribute to strong performance in environments with many unknown elements, probability, and complex strategic planning.

Beyond the goal of reaching high leaderboard results in the Simulation Category competition, the Strategy Category competition provides participants with an opportunity to share the thinking and experimentation behind their AI Training agents. We hope participants will use this as a chance to present creative approaches, interesting findings, and lessons learned during development.

As with many Kaggle competitions, we also hope this event becomes a place where participants can showcase their skills in reinforcement learning, game AI, and decision-making when dealing with unknown elements.

We look forward to seeing the ideas and insights that emerge from the community.
Good luck, and enjoy the challenge!
