### Explained: Why our usual methods don't work in this competition

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e20/discussion/432294

Many of us have applied standard machine learning methods (e.g., LightGBM and Optuna) to this competition and got disastrous scores. Can you explain why? Here's my explanation.

1. The satellite measurements are useless: These features are terribly noisy and unrelated to the target. A satellite measurement applies only to a single point in time whereas the targets are the CO2 emissions of a whole week. The time of the measurement is determined by the satellite's orbit. Its result depends on clouds, temperature, volcano eruptions and other factors. Furthermore, CO2 emissions come from different sectors (ground traffic, heating, power generation, air traffic, …), and these sectors don't contribute proportionally to NO2, CO or SO2. With a diameter of 200 km, Rwanda is a small country, and the wind replaces Rwanda's whole atmosphere with air from neighboring countries every day.

After dropping the satellite measurements, we have only four features left: latitude, longitude, year and week_no.

2. Location data is in the wrong form: The location data is given in two dimensions (latitude and longitude), and machine learning models naturally assume that nearby locations have similar emissions. Maybe all cities have similar emissions, all villages are similar, and all forests have similar emissions, but these three classes of locations don't form contiguous regions — a city may be in the middle of a forest — and at least two locations are outliers.

Keeping locations in latitude–longitude form is wrong; three better forms are:

Transform the locations into a five- or six-dimensional space with an SVD or NMF (discussion post).

Cluster the locations according to their emission patterns (notebook).

Use the locations as categorical features or calculate the predictions for every location independently.

3. The lockdown was a singular event: Nobody could foresee the lockdown, and cross-validating a machine-learning model on a validation set which contains the year 2020 is nonsense.

The lockdown hinders not only validation: What happens if the training dataset contains the lockdown period? Do we want our model to learn that the emissions of week 10 can be drastically lower than the emissions of week 9? No: Although such a reduction occurred in week 10 of 2020, it doesn't occur in week 10 of 2022, and we're using our model only for predicting 2022. We want our model to learn that Lake Kivu emissions triple every year from week 53 to week 0, but we do not want it to learn that Kihanga emissions can suddenly shrink in week 10. Standard machine-learning models cannot distinguish the two cases; they are programmed to predict averages (e.g., random forest or gradient boosting) or to extrapolate from averages (e.g., linear regression or Holt–Winters).

[图 1: inbox%2F7917824%2F56b51dd05d0705e4a7df2eb6b3ce8f17%2Ftwolocs(1).png]

What are our options? 

Having seen that traditional machine learning fails at distinguishing seasonal variations from lockdown effects, human intervention is necessary. We need to give our prior information about the lockdown to the model. The lockdown weeks of the training data are outliers and must be dropped or adjusted!

Typical approaches are:

As the lockdown reduced the emissions for most locations, the maximum emission of the three training years is a good baseline for predicting 2022 (notebook).

To reduce the variance compared to the first approach, we can scale the training data to the levels of 2021 (because this year is closest to 2022) or to the levels of 2019 (because this year is unaffected by Covid) (notebook).

By taking the maximum we ignore that the economy hadn't completely recovered from the lockdown at the beginning of 2022. This observation leads to the idea of using the recovery-from-lockdown phase (mid-2020 through end of 2021) as the basis for extrapolating into 2022 (discussion post).

[图 2: inbox%2F7917824%2Fc3938772596d3ba4d5bdad7ee4f13265%2Fseasonalcovid-3(3).png]

Advice:

Be creative

Validate your model on more than just the public leaderboard (e.g. on the second half of 2021)

Ensemble

### 4th place solution: using PCA (least shakeuped solution)

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e20/discussion/433567

The solution notebook is available here

Thanks to the organizers of this interesting competition, and thanks to all participants for the discussions!

I think there was much valuable information to learn and improve!

The pipeline

I started this competition by building a pipeline to make CV and test different approaches. This pipeline consequently used each available year (2019, 2020, and 2021) for validation while the others were used for training. Finally, all the data was used for training and prediction of the 2022 emissions.

Please, refer to the source code for details.

[图 1: inbox%2F4308868%2Ffd7788cd672c33c165a6f4d4933acb7d%2Fpgs320.png]

Using PCA

The core of my solution is using PCA. I decided to use 6 components, as I reported in this discussion by @ambrosm .

Each of the 6 components is processed independently by some (abstract) algorithm, and the results are taken to inverse PCA. Finally, we have the predictions.

[图 2: inbox%2F4308868%2Fb716fa92ff24fb8b2b67df9f7ee7c334%2Fpgs320-2.png]

Algorithms

Each algorithm I used has a base estimator as a parameter, which is used to make a prediction. With such an architecture, it's possible to switch between them and optimize CV score.

✓ PCA1 Algorithm

This algorithm is very simple: it takes emission data only and processes it with an estimator. It was reported in many discussions that all other columns are not needed. So this is an implementation of this idea.

✓ PCA2 Algorithm

This algorithm is different: it uses all columns. PCA is applied separately to each of them, i.e., we have n_columns * n_pca_components input features.

✓ PCA_SARIMA Algorithm

Instead of simple estimators, I used 6 independent SARIMA models in the PCA1 Algorithm architecture.

The best algorithm (both local CV and private LB) was the PCA2 Algorithm.

Estimators

I used Ridge/Lasso regression, RF, XGB, CatBoost, etc. There was not much difference between tree-based estimators, and they gave better results than linear regression. So I choose XGBRegressor(n_estimators = 300, max_depth = 4, learning_rate = 0.01, subsample = 0.5).

Postprocessing

I was inspired by @ambrosm 's post and other discussions, which propose to multiply the result by the constant. So, I made predictions with different multipliers: 1.00, 0.95, 1.05, 1.07, and 1.08.

However, somehow I chose the worst submission of these five as my final prediction!

[图 3: inbox%2F4308868%2Fc2b515813ff510427d1f87b374a11787%2Fpgs320-3.png]

It would be the 1st place, if I didn't use the multiplier at all!

### Dimensionality reduction: 5 dimensions are enough

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e20/discussion/429278

In this competition we are predicting 497 independent time series. Independent??? Did you check the independence of the targets? Everybody looks at correlations between features, but who did look at correlations among the 497 targets?

A PCA or an SVD of the 497 emission time series reveals that five components suffice to explain 100 % of the variance:

[图 1: inbox%2F7917824%2Ff14d54ec4b406406ffaf0f272ea2cbaa%2Fcum-exp-var.png]

This is quite remarkable: It means that the emissions of the 495 locations all are linear combinations of the emissions of only five basis locations!

We can project the time series of the 495 locations to the first two principal components and mark four extreme locations:

[图 2: inbox%2F7917824%2F0a1db2e06742089bbea889d1d838eda7%2Fprojection.png]

The four marked locations are indeed special:

Nyamasheke has the highest average emission and a pattern which differs from every other location.

Lake Kivu has high peaks every January and February.

Kihanga has no seasonal peaks.

Musanze has seasonal peaks every May and October.

[图 3: inbox%2F7917824%2F8d551630ae3e346a7f76a9580f6cd115%2Fbasis.png]

The time series of all other locations are linear combinations of these four plus a fifth basis location.

Insight:

We don't need to predict 497 independent time series for 2022. It suffices to predict 5 basis time series, and all others can be derived.

The huge dimensionality reduction (100 % explained variance ratio with only five components) wouldn't be possible if the measurements came from real sensors. It indicates that the emission data has been generated artificially.

I don't yet know how the information about the dimensionality reduction will help improve leaderboard scores, but I'm sure it will… 😉

I was very impressed with the "maximum" solution presented by @danbraswell, so I decided to simplify and improve it a bit. You can see the final result, which contains only 6 lines of code, here: https://www.kaggle.com/kdmitrie/pgs320-the-shortest-solution

The "improvement" (it is actually a LB overfitting, yes) is done by multiplying the solution by a constant.

I used different constants, and below is the LB dependence on its value. You can see, that it is perfect parabola with the best value near 0.99. For further improving we need a new idea!
