### Finding out whether two models in this competition are likely to ensemble well

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e6/discussion/512220

I have written several threads on the topic of ensembling, but the challenge seems to be different in this competition. When we predict continuous values or a single probability per data point, there are many ways to assess whether the two predictions are diverse: 1) correlation (there are several flavors of it); 2) cumulative distribution functions; 3) Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics; 4) a scatter plot of predictions. None of those apply when we have to compare three columns of probabilities. Or maybe they do, but I don't know enough statistics to use a simple approach.

So I went with a complicated approach, and it kind of works. First, I defined a confidence value for each 3-class prediction by using this formula:

df[] = * df.(axis=) - df.sum(axis=) + df.(axis=)

Dropout, Enrolled, Graduate contain predicted probabilities for the three classes. This formula gives a positive number in the 0-1 range. It will be zero if our prediction is a total guess [1/3, 1/3, 1/3] and it will be 1 if our predictions are absolutely certain (any of the [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0] or [0, 0, 1] cases).

Now we look into our out-of-fold predictions: 1) if they are correct, our confidence values is unchanged; 2) for incorrect out-of-fold predictions we add a minus sign to confidence values. That means our confidence values could be high and correct (that's the case most of the time) or high and incorrect, and we will be able to distinguish between them. And now for something completely different:

As the plot shows, this would be LightGBM vs Support Vector Classifier (SVC). But really, any other model, including neural networks, would give a similar plot against SVC. SVMs have fallen out of style as they are too slow for large datasets, but I will make a case for this method on the grounds of significant diversity. Also, they are not terribly slow when used with a GPU and Nvidia's RAPIDS, and I just might demonstrate that one of these days.

Let me do some annotation of the previous plot:

[图 4: LGB-vs-SVC-complex-confidence.png]

Green boxes show areas when one model is better than the other, but it makes no real difference. In the upper right corner LightGBM is better because its predictions are more confident than for SVC, but both are generally correct. Note that SVC confidence values top at ~abs(0.8) which translates to a max probability of 0.87-0.9. That means that for this dataset SVC never assigns probability > 0.9 for any data point. If our metric was log-loss, then averaging the two models in that region would give us a better score. Given that our metric is accuracy and both models are correct in that region of the plot, it makes no difference. Similarly, in the lower left corner green box both models are wrong, but SVC is "less wrong." An example data point for the upper-right green box would be id: 40002 from the train data, which is predicted as 0.01299,0.010502,0.976508 by LightGBM and 0.096031,0.091103,0.812866 by SVC. The former is clearly "more right" but it makes no difference when evaluating accuracy.

The upper-right red box is where SVC is considerably better than LightGBM, even though both of them make correct predictions because of positive confidence values. In the lower-left red box we have LightGBM making "less wrong" predictions than SVC, even though both models are wrong.

Where we are going to make hay is blue boxes. In the upper-left blue box, especially its top half, SVC is both confident and correct in its predictions, while the LightGBM model is the opposite. The roles are reversed in the lower-right blue box. Averaging those values might bring some data points from incorrect into correct category. Specifically, the upper-right quadrant of the upper-left blue box holds the most promise to get things fixed by averaging, because that is where SVC is both highly confident and correct (relatively high and correct probabilities) while LightGBM is incorrect but not too confident (incorrect but relatively low probabilities). The same is true for the upper-right quadrant of the lower-right box, but there aren't as many points in that region.

Please let me know if you have a different way (and likely better) to assess model diversity.

For the end a public service announcement: ensembling is fundamental and go out there and find some diverse models!

### 1st Place Second AutoML Grand Prix [Team AutoML Grandmasters] 

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e6/discussion/509631

Heyho everyone,

The following details our approach during this competition. In addition, we provide supplementary information and an aggregation of our main contributions into one code file via this GitHub repository.

The notebook by @gauravduttakiit really changed this competition because we found nothing in our offline evaluation that explained why this specific model and preprocessing was so good on the public leaderboard. Thus, it seemed like the notebook won a lottery ticket on the public leaderboard. 

An alternative theory was that renaming the columns had some unknown impact that we could not reproduce or pinpoint. We also investigated replacing values with Null (as described here), but this only gave a small increase in offline validation score, which we also got by other means. 

Thus, we opted to include it in an ensemble together with our own submission. As @roberthatch pointed out, this made the rest of the competition an averaging gamble. 

Although it seems we took a bit of a different approach to produce the average at the time. In detail, we opted to produce a weighted ensemble of the model created by @gauravduttakiit and our own submission. To do so, we took the notebook's data and used FLAML to get prediction probabilities of the winning lottery ticket model (loosely related, see this):

starting_points = {: gbm_hyperparameters}

Then, we averaged the prediction probabilities and finally converted the average into the final submission. We used a weight of 0.14 (our submission) and 0.86 (the winning lottery ticket model).

Details on Our Submission

For our own submissions, we used an offline evaluation setup to determine how to configure AutoGluon for a three-hour training run. 

Early Stopping Metric

We started using log loss as an early stopping metric in AutoGluon very early on. Importantly, when doing this with the following code, our evaluation metric (i.e., accuracy) is still the selection metric and used for post hoc ensembling!

After we saw the notebook, which used ROC AUC instead of Log Loss, we also implemented and tested how it would perform. This, however, did not improve performance. 

Overview of Other Offline Evaluation Results

In our offline evaluation, we also came to the following conclusions:

Treating ordinal features as numerical features is beneficial.

Using original data is better for model performance (even with its distribution shift).

Using sample weights does not help.

Using repeated cross-validation is beneficial. 

Using transductive learning is beneficial. Yet, we did not use it in our submissions as we did not trust the results of our test. 

We verified that gradient-bosted decision trees (GBDTs) and neural networks (NNs) are both capable of solving this task. 

Stacking

We noticed that NNs were much better than GBDTs among all stacking models. Based on our prior work on stacked overfitting, we knew this pattern and thus opted not to use GBDTs as stacking models anymore, which further improved offline performance.

Feature Engineering

We used OpenFE to obtain new features, which improved our offline validation score. We do not know if it made our final submission worse, as the lottery ticket model did not use similar features. 

EDA

We looked at the features with EDA, but nothing seemed off nor worthy of further manual investigation.

### [AutoML Grand Prix (Pt. 2)] Solution writeup

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e6/discussion/509642

As we know, the Playground Series datasets are synthetically augmented variants of long-standing popular tabular datasets, and this month was no exception. 

I am a firm believer in the power of feature engineering to find extra predictive power in datasets, and in view of this a good place for inspiration is to review the work of other people on the 'unadulterated' dataset. However, my off-line tests steadfastly refused to show better results when creating new features such as cross-terms, interaction terms etc. even for features that have shown strong promise on the original dataset.

Another route is to remove very weak features that are essentially noise, but that too degraded my baseline score. Based on the finding of last months' challenge, where the dataset was trivial but the color of the synthetic noise had some weak predictive power my hypothesis is that even useless features became predictive features due to this color, hence dropping them degrades performance.

Also, depending on which features to treat as categorical or not had strange effects (in the end I gave up exploring and went with the "AmbrosM" list). I was left wondering if the categorical data is actually augmented as categorical data or not.

In short; everything I tried in the time available was fruitless. However, as we all know Gaurav Dutta created and published a very strong Public LB notebook (whether it is a actually robust solution or not will have to wait for the Private LB…) 

In the end, with time running short, I blended his output file with a CatBoost baseline and a LightGBM baseline by simply taking the mode of the class predictions: my 'best-of-five' was notebook #2, which only blended CatBoost and gave a Public LB score of 0.83875. (Note: taking the mode of just two values is somewhat ill defined…)

To use up my very last submission of the day, just for fun, I changed the random_seed from 42 to 24, and upped the number of CatBoost estimators form 1000 to 1001, and my Public LB score dropped to 0.83679. With this in mind I am inclined to suspect that there was a fair degree of chance/luck at play; …there is nothing quite like combining the perfect storm of synthetic data with the accuracy score metric to make things "interesting"!

### The two most important features

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e6/discussion/509073

Plotting a shallow decision tree is a good method for quickly understanding what are the most important features of a dataset:

[图 1: inbox%2F7917824%2F701b518e0d3eb666023de93cc3756bc1%2Fdt.png]

The decision tree shows us that Curricular units 2nd sem (approved) and Tuition fees up to date are the two most important features:

If Curricular units 2nd sem (approved) is 5 or more, the student is classified as graduate.

If Curricular units 2nd sem (approved) is 0 or 1, the student is classified as dropout.

Otherwise, check the tuition fees. If they are up to date, predict enrolled; if they are not, predict dropout.
