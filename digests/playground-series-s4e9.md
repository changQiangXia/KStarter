### # 1 solution - stacked NN

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e9/discussion/537052

Did I really made it to the top? I am still surprised and excited.

Way to the solution: I spent the first two weeks with reading the discussions, playing around with catboost and publishing an ensemble. The plan was to collect diverse models and ensemble them with Ridge, with the same pipeline I used in this notebook. The final ensemble, which I chose as my first final submission, would have landed me on the second place and differed from the notebook in the following points:

I used 20 cv folds

I included original data in some of the models (even two times in LGBM)

I did compute a SVR with a rbf kernel as suggested by broccoli beef in this discussion post instead of the linear SVR.

I included all categorical features additionally as target encoded to catboost, but I used the median, not the mean for target encoding. I did this leakfree, meaning that I recomputed the targetencoded columns in each fold. Moreover, I used Catboost as classifier, not as regressor. Catboost predicted the outlier prices (see function bin_price). The hyperparameters were found by optuna. The oof predictions were not used in the ensemble. They were used as an additional feature in a LGBM (or the NN for my second final submission).

### Which Features Interact? and How To Leak Free Target Encode with RAPIDS!

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e9/discussion/533961

Interaction Starter Notebook

We can engineer new categorical interaction features by combining columns with code: train["brand-milage"] = train["brand"] + "-" + train["milage"].astype("str"). (This is after we bin milage as categorical with code train["milage"] = (train["milage"]//1000)*1000 ). This example combines brand and milage. I call this a bi-gram. Afterward it is categorical, so we then need to encode it with techniques such as target encoding. I trained a Lasso linear model with all bi-grams and tri-grams here

Strong Interaction Features

Once we train a model with all bi-grams and tri-grams, we can observe the feature importance of different combinations of features.

For example, less milage increases price. But this relationship is different for different brands. For example with electric cars, we observe that milage does not affect price as much. Therefore the interaction of brand and milage is important. In the below feature importance plot, we see that this interaction ranks 3rd out of 231 combinations!

We also see that 4th most important is model combined with int_col. This means that people prefer different interior colors for different brands.

We also see a strong interaction between accident and model_year. A new car (recent model_year) that has an accident reduces the price much more than an older car with an accident. This is because older cars are expected to have problems whereas newer cars are not.

When viewing feature importance for Lasso, the top of the list of important and the bottom of the list are important. The top indicate strong positive correlation whereas the bottom indicate strong negative correlation. The middle indicate no correlation.

In the bottom of the list below, we see that model and model_year combine to make a strong negative correlation with price. In other words, people prefer to buy certain models from certain model_years.

Lasso Model Interaction Feature Importances

The below plot is from notebook here. Note that both top and bottom of list have strong importance. A large positive bar represents strong positive correlation whereas a large negative bar represents strong negative correlation. Bars near length zero (in the y axis middle), are weak features with weak correlation. And any bi-grams and tri-grams that are not displayed in the plot below indicate they have exactly 0 feature importance (and therefore are removed from plot). The Lasso model naturally removes unimportant features.

[图 1: lasso_FI.png]

Leak Free Target Encoding

One way to encode categorical columns is to use Target Encoding. TE is a powerful technique explained in blog here (by fellow KGMON Jiwei) and in previous playground Kaggle notebook here (by fellow KGMON Theo). There is also a KGMON tutorial here.

Target Encoding uses targets, so we need to be careful to prevent leaks. When we use 5 Kfolds, we need to compute TE 5 times. During each Kfold iteration, we need to fit_transform TE on the train data, and then transform the valid data and test data. (i.e. we cannot simply compute TE once for all train.csv data before our 5 Kfold for-loop).

Also the process of TE fit_transform uses 5 new folds itself. So inside each of the 5 folds of our original K-Fold, TE creates another 5 folds to fit TE. The diagram below shows this and the code of my starter notebook here shows how to do this. 

Preventing leaks is important for maximizing LB score and creating leak free OOF especially if we plan to create meta model ensembles that train on OOF! Being leak-free is also important to computing our correct CV score which we use to make decisions like feature selection, model selection, etc.

### [AutoML Grand Prix] 1st Place Solution Team LightAutoML testers 

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e9/discussion/531884

[AutoML Grand Prix] 1st Place Solution Team LightAutoML testers 

Thanks for the competition and congratulations to all the participants for reaching the final stage of the AutoML Grand Prix. Next, I'll share the solution of Team LightAutoML testers (me, @alexryzhkov, @rinchin ).

Github repo with the training code.

TLDR: 24 base regression and classification models, GBDT + NN, blend and stack. 

We trained all models (catboost and lgbm for regression, denselight and fttransformer for both regression and classification) with original and clipped target (clip all price values higher than 500k for training fold) using original and augmented with kagglex (added only in train fold) datasets (thanks to @lashfire).

Our final ensemble with 10-Fold CV scores is:

[图 1: inbox%2F597945%2Fec16e57f8d54df381cbc2ca8fcecb9d1%2FFinal_solution2.png]

It may seem scary, but training of all models was completed in 5 hours on a single A100 GPU.

Feature generation:

To receive a better model we have created several new features for more proper car specifications description:

the engine column was converted into separated columns with the horsepower, litres, cylinder count and type, fuel type etc

from milage column we calculated the "significant" percent of figures - for 50000 it is 20% and for 12345 it is 0% (this can help to check how detailed the car description is)

from transmission column we got speeds count and transmission type

ext_col and int_col goes to lower case to merge similar values into one

NN models:

Target scaling was essential for training the neural networks, so we used the Robust Scaler and applied the inverse transform to the model predictions.

Another important observation that the price has only 1569 unique values and 222 after rounding them to the nearest 1000. So, we decided to train multiclass classification model. During inference we multiplied probabilities of each class by the corresponding unique target value and use this estimation as a final prediction. We choose NN as a base model due to computational complexity of training multioutput gbdt (which scales linearly with the number of classes) and we didn`t have enough time to integrate PyBoost (which has constant complexity) into our pipeline. Multiclass DenseLight achieved the best solo result in our ensemble. 

We also replaced default FTTransformer encoder model with HuggingFace Bert Tiny, initialising it with pre-trained text weights. It gave us a slight boost on single holdout score, but this result might be random (we didn`t check it on full CV :) ). Nevertheless, we choose this variant for diversification.

Clean OOF and Hill Climbing

I tried hard to build a high performance single model but ensemble diverse models always beat my single models so my strategy was ensemble.

The secret to ensemble is saving clean leak free OOF together with test predictions from every single model. Then we use hill climbing or a stage 2 meta model trained on OOF. Lastly we use the same model/technique on the test predictions.

Two HC Starter Notebooks

I published two hill climbing notebooks in 2 old comps, Melonoma prediction here and Novozyme theromstabilty prediction here. The former uses only positive weights and the latter uses both negative and positive weights.

Public Models

For ensemble, we want lots of diverse models. In addition to my own models, I forked the best public single models and added code to save clean OOF to disk. I ignored all blender public notebooks.

For public AutoML notebooks, I added code to save the OOF to disk for all the single models (made by the AutoML) and saved test predictions for all single models.

Private Models

I acquired 110 OOF from public notebooks and then created 40 single models of my own. Half of mine were hand crafted and half were AutoML.

Stack Regression over Classification

My most interesting and diverse model was 5 Fold XGB then inside every fold, I created 5 new folds and trained a classification NN. The NN predicted 50 target price bins using multi classification loss. I then used those 50 probabilities as features for the second level XGB model. The XGB model used regression loss. The model and OOF were leak free since the five NN 5 fold was nested inside the XGB 5 fold. This model was diverse and boosted hill climbing CV and LB.

Result

I think I pushed hill climbing too much and overfitted a little. I could have achieved a better private LB score if I was less aggressive with hill climbing.

I allowed negative weights and set tolerance to 1e-5. In retrospect, I think tolerance should be 1 because 1 is already the fifth significant digit (for this comp's metric). By using 1e-5, I was fitting to the tenth significant digit. 

This means that the hill climbing algorithm keeps running when it should stop because it begins fitting and adding noise. We only want to add a new model if it boost CV 1 unit like improve CV from 72100 to 72099. We dont want to add a model that boost CV from 72100 to 72099.99999. In other comps the metric is like 0.72009 so 1e-5 makes sense but in this comp metric is like 72009 so maybe 1 makes more sense.

Post Comp Analysis

If I used tolerance 1, then public LB would be 71901 and private LB would be 63003 and CV 72209. With tolerance 1e-5, public LB is 71887 and private LB is 63057 and CV 72081.

Using 1e-5 produced better CV score and better public LB score so it was hard to know what to do during the competition. Perhaps the clue that we are overfitting is that using 'tol=1e-5' vs 'tol=1' boost CV 72209 to 72081 but public LB only improved 71901 to 71887.

The difference in private LB is only 00050 out of 63000 which is about 0.1% so it isn't a big deal but in a competition it changes rank from 5th to 81st.

### #4 solution | Beating a dead horse: blending works, but "blending" doesn't

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s4e9/discussion/536973

There was a discussion here just hours before the deadline asking whether blending will work. I will summarize the gist of my discussion in that thread and give a broad overview of my solution.

Proper ensembling, which includes blending, has been used for a long time and on a variety of datasets and problems. It works. That's not me saying it, but rather a known fact.

Guessing weights to combine public solutions is not blending in the original sense, so I will call it "blending." That one may or may not work, and it is to a good degree dataset- and luck-dependent. The luck was not with us with this dataset, and I thought that would be the case after about 3 days working with this dataset - see here. This "blending" approach will work here and there, but not in general. Maybe that's good enough for some of us, but I learned after about 3 competitions that it is not my cup of tea.

I selected for scoring my 1st and 3rd best submissions, so very happy about that. Both of them were hill climbing ensembles of 32 models each. The better of the two ensembles (they differ by 8 RMSE points) was made completely of my models. The second ensemble included about half my models, and the other half came from public notebooks that I thought were done well, and most importantly the notebooks that had out-of-fold predictions. Can't do proper ensembling without those!

EDIT: Thanks to @roberthatch for reminding me here that I didn't give proper credit to the groups whose OOF models were part of my second ensemble. I also used LAMA's OOF models from here, @cdeotte from here and @ravaghi from here.

Ensembles must be diverse to work, so both of mine included Keras factorization machines (FMs), xLearn FMs, Lasso, CatBoost, LAMA NNs, AutoGluon ensembles, and a huge array of individual tree models that were pulled out from AutoGluon directories (a mix of RandomForest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost and LightGBM models). A couple of FastAI NN models, which I don't even know how to make, were pulled also from the cache of AutoGluon models. The first four approaches modeled all the features as categoricals, and generally worked the best. I also used AugoGluon-encoded datasets (with some NLP-generated features) for separate modeling with factorization machines, and that gave a small boost.

Tried feature engineering, which gave slightly better CV scores but not LB scores. Didn't use any of it in the end as I suspected overfitting. One thing I do regret is adding the original dataset too late, as it seemed to benefit the overall score. I had only 2 days to incorporate those models with original used car data, and they pushed my ensembles by about 20 points. I suspect that would have been more productive if I started earlier.
