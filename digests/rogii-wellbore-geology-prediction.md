### 6th Place - PF, PF, PF, PF, Physics and Row-Level Bagging

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rogii-wellbore-geology-prediction/discussion/733226

To the organizers — thank you for hosting such a wonderful competition. This is a largely unexplored problem, and although I am not well-versed in geology or time-series analysis, I found a great deal to learn. I finished 20th on Public and 6th on Private — a large shake-up — and I also reflect on what caused such a big move in the standings. I present the solution following the trajectory of the accuracy improvements, so I hope you'll take a look. I'm also looking forward to reading the other participants' solutions.

1. Summary

The task is to take the gamma-ray log (GR) measured along a horizontal well, compare it against reference data provided separately, and predict how high in the rock column the well is passing (TVT).

My solution relied, from start to finish, on a Particle Filter. I ran many particle filters with different settings to produce many TVT estimates, and fed a final set of 91 estimates into an NN model to get the prediction. The mental picture is that the NN fuses the 91 candidates row by row — it is doing "row-level bagging." This reaches about CV 5.4. I am not a model specialist, so rather than elaborate on the model I fought this by relentlessly improving those 91 inputs (i.e., the features).

The final score was Private 5.984 / Public 5.626 / CV 5.4577.

2-0. The starting point

2-2. Hot-rodding the Particle Filter (GPU + a smoother) — CV 7.5 / LB 6.724

At the 2-1 stage, just running 3 PFs (seed = 32) already took about 4 hours to score, dominated by the Particle Filter. So I rebuilt the Particle Filter to run on GPU. This made experiments and inference ~200× faster, letting me run far more trials.

A by-product of this rebuild was that I could add a smoother mechanism to the PF. An ordinary particle filter uses "only the past" to estimate the position at each step (causal). A smoother instead keeps the particles' ancestry (which particle came from where) and re-estimates the past after seeing a little of the future GR. Because it uses GR context on both sides, the estimate is more stable, and places that momentarily got lost in noise get corrected after the fact. What I use here is a fixed-lag particle smoother, following Kitagawa (1996) / Doucet & Johansen (2009) (see References).

Feeding this into the tabular model, a smoothing width of smooth = 192 gave CV −0.3 / LB −0.4. However, the longer the lag, the heavier the compute — each particle's ancestry must be kept over a long window — so it runs slower than the plain (no-smoothing) PF. Measured on an RTX 5070, one pass (one parameter set × the tw representation) over all 773 wells takes ~4.5 min for plain PF → ~4.9 min for fixed-lag 16 (+~10%) → ~13 min for fixed-lag 192 (~2.9× plain). A short lag is nearly free, but stretching it to 192 becomes clearly heavy (and is impractical without the GPU port).

2-3. Alternative GR representations (self / neighbor) — CV 6.98 / LB 6.026

From this discussion I learned that the typewell GR (henceforth twGR) is noisy, and that there is a notion of a "pseudo-typewell."

Thinking about how to apply this to our task, I noticed that neighboring train wells are provided (I call this neighbor). By overwriting the twGR with the known GR of a train neighbor within 1500 ft, you get a pseudo typewell GR (nbrGR).

There is a second idea of the same kind — self. It uses the well's own prefix (the known interval on the heel side). Since TVT and GR are both known there, I overwrite in the same way (selfGR).

The estimates inferred from these tvt–GR pairs are strongly complementary to the twGR estimate. The correlations are roughly selfGR ≈ 0.79 and nbrGR ≈ 0.72 against twGR — accurate on their own, yet sufficiently decorrelated. Different references fail in different ways, so their independent errors cancel downstream.

2-4. Injecting a physical constraint — CV 6.50 / LB 6.43

At this point CV (~7.0) and Public LB (6.0) diverged badly. Following the discussion argument to "combine physical constraints," I built a prior over TVT from the well geometry (X, Y, Z) and the known TVT alone, using no GR whatsoever.

The idea: even without GR, if you know how far and in which direction a well went (geometry) and how the surrounding wells are tilting (the dip of the strata), you can predict how TVT will change. I split each well into segments, estimate the local dip in each, share it spatially, and integrate the dip to reconstruct the TVT trajectory. Crucially, this must be built in a strict out-of-fold manner: mixing any information from the evaluation wells into the dip field or the calibration makes only the CV look unfairly good, and it then collapses on Private (the seed of a shake-down).

2-5. Fusing many leftover PFs with an input-tolerant ensembler — CV 5.94 / LB 6.17

I had a large pile of PFs that I had tuned with Optuna but that had not, on their own, led to any improvement. At some point, instead of discarding them, I fed them all in together — and the accuracy jumped, dropping CV below 6.0 (CV 5.94 / Public 6.17).

Why did it work? First, dissecting the error, the residual against a strong PF had self-correlation of almost 1.000 — meaning the independent per-row signal is basically exhausted, and the error lives in a per-segment choice of "which solution do we sit on." The problem was not row regression but, per segment, choosing which of many candidates to sit on.

So I built a candidate-curve ensembler that lines up each PF × representation TVT trajectory as "one candidate curve," and lets a lightweight attention soft-average the candidates per row. The tabular model could only concatenate a fixed-length set — limited in expressive power — whereas the ensembler can line up any number of candidates. That was decisive: it let me throw in all the PFs I had lying around that hadn't helped on their own, and that is what suddenly worked.

The candidates I fed in were 6 PFs (pf_1 / pf_2 / pf_3 / r0_seed32 / r1_seed32 / pfA) × 5 representations = 30. The five representations differ in which template the GR is matched against:

typewell: the given vertical-well template (2-3).

self: the well's own known prefix GR as a pseudo-template (2-3).

neighbor: a spatially near (XY) train well's GR as a pseudo-template (2-3).

The key design finding is that it acts as a broad soft average (bagging), not a one-of-N selector. Indeed, restricting to the top-k hurt, and softmax over all candidates was optimal. Since you cannot tell "which solution is correct" from GR alone, the right move is not to force a choice, but to average probabilistically and hedge.

3. Discussion

3-1. The biggest driver of the accuracy gains

I believe the single biggest driver was that the PF outputs themselves carried a lot of diversity.

Concretely: trajectories with the physical prior (the GR-free constraint) and without it, matching against a typewell or against a nearby well instead, trajectories derived from the prefix (the known interval), plus the overall lift from full smoothing — I ran everything I could think of through the PF. The result was a lot of diversity from the PF alone. Each individual candidate is not strong at all — around 10.0–7.8 in overall RMSE on its own.

Bluntly, what I am doing is bagging a large number of PF outputs. But it turned out to be a nice example of how simply lining up and fusing candidates that are wrong in different ways can push the accuracy this far.

3-2. The bias of the Public LB, and CV / Public / Private transfer

I once said in the discussions that "the Public overfits under a certain condition." My belief is that methods that depend only on the GR match overfit to the Public.

3-3. How to build a physical constraint that risks a shake-down

The physical constraint of 2-4 is very powerful, but built wrongly it easily overfits the Public/CV and collapses hard on Private (a shake-down). This is the single most important trick.

The constraint builds a "dip field of the wells" and its "calibration coefficients" from other wells' information. If you let any information from the well being evaluated (validation) leak into building the field or the calibration, you have peeked at the answer, and CV becomes unfairly good. Then you can no longer observe CV–LB transfer correctly, and a submission chosen by trusting the Public order collapses on Private.

The fix: always build it in a strict 5-fold (out-of-fold) manner. That is, construct the dip field, the calibration coefficients, and the neighbor pool from only the training-side wells of that fold, fully excluding the evaluation wells, and rebuild the field and calibration per fold. Since my goal was to grow CV into a trustworthy metric, this rigor was decisive. Whenever you inject a "learned prior" like a physical constraint, treat leakage as the seed of a shake and always build it with the evaluation targets excluded.

3-4. Why per-row regression (tabular alone) does not work

Early on, the tabular model (GBDT) led, but as accuracy improved it was fully replaced by a sequence model + candidate selection. The reason is fundamental.

In one line: it is not a problem you can solve one row at a time. Against a strong PF output, the remaining error is no longer "small per-row wiggles"; it is that a whole segment is shifted, as a block, one way or the other. Because of the periodicity of the strata, GR looks almost identical at two places, above and below — so telling "is this segment the upper one or the lower one?" from GR is essentially a coin flip (you can't).

A per-row feature vector carries no information about "which of the two solutions this whole segment sits on." So the tabular model plateaus no matter how many features you add. In contrast, lining up the PF's "single coherent trajectories" as candidates and soft-averaging them naturally expresses this "whole-segment choice." In short, tabular is not bad — per-row regression was the wrong frame for the shape of the problem (a whole-segment two-way choice).

3-5. Designing the CV so you don't shake down on Private

The thing I valued most for avoiding a shake-down was keeping the variance between folds small.

The reason is that Train / Public / Private are most likely split completely at random, and each is small — on the order of 150 wells. With a small random split, there is always a chance that the hard wells happen to cluster into one split and you draw a downswing. Whether Public or Private turns out to be a "bad draw" is not something you can control.

That is exactly why a design whose score never collapses on any fold or any split — i.e., one with no downswings — pays off. A model with low inter-fold variance won't miss badly whatever random 150-well draw Public / Private happens to be. Conversely, a model with the same average performance but high inter-fold variance always carries the risk of hitting a downswing somewhere and shaking down. Removing the downswings matters as much as raising the average — that is my conclusion.

Measured on the final submission, each of the 5 folds (≈155 wells each) lands roughly in the 4–6.7 range, and the overall CV averaged over the 5 folds barely moves — 5.40–5.43 (std ≈ 0.015) no matter how the split is re-drawn. In the early baseline (2-1) the per-fold RMSE swung from about 6 to 10, so the inter-fold spread has shrunk substantially. I believe this "resistance to downswings" is one reason it did not collapse on Private either.

3-6. Why the Optuna-tuned PF parameters didn't overfit the CV

I tuned the PF parameters by randomly selecting 600 wells (or 150 wells) from the 773. The natural worry is: "wouldn't that overfit to the wells you happened to pick?"

First, the PF is a deterministic method with not that many parameters, so it is inherently resistant to overfitting. That said, when I optimized on few wells, I did confirm that overfitting occurs.

However, that overfitting appears to be only against twGR (the representation that matches against the typewell's GR). I did the tuning on twGR and then reused those tuned parameters as-is for the other representations — nbrGR, selfGR, and so on (2-3). I believe that applying parameters overfit to twGR onto representations of a different nature is what diluted the overfitting.

3-7. Why the shake-up happened

The large turnover inside the gold zone was not caused by overfitting to the Public. It happened because there existed methods that were excellent for CV and Public but underwhelming on Private. The very methods of those who got shaken down are, in my view, the valuable ones. This is not the same as the public-notebook overfitting — please don't lump them together.

The final standings saw a large shake-up, and much of the Public top turned over. Looking at the solutions that survived at the top, teams that leaned on physics-based models — HMMs, particle filters — climbed almost across the board. I can't say for certain whether this was mere split noise or the result of deliberately choosing a different domain (i.e., a more physics-oriented formulation), but at the very least the dominant solution domain clearly differed between the Public top group and the Private top group.

After the competition I had a brief chance to talk with another team, and they raised the point that "approaches that don't rely on a physical model may be prone to CV overfitting."

Seen that way, that I ended up on the shake-up side (the one that rose) owed a good deal to luck. My read is that the Public-top solutions that did not strongly depend on a physical model — however excellent as methods — could not escape the accuracy drop from domain shift on Private.

4. What worked

The methods that were the reasons for improvement:

Optuna-tuning the Particle Filter's parameters (GR-likelihood σ, transition noise, resample, jump, …) — the foundation for good candidates.

Concatenating estimates from several PF parameter sets as input (3-input, 5-input — stepwise gains).

Porting the PF to GPU (~60× faster) — enabled far more trials and the smoother.

The smoother mechanism. Stabilizes the estimate with two-sided GR context; lag = 192 gave CV −0.3 / LB −0.4, leak-free and transferring cleanly.

Adding several alternative GR representations. Beyond twGR: self, neighbor (by XY distance), GR-similarity neighbor, and a self-graft (self extrapolated via calibration) — five in all. Complementary candidates, decorrelated (≈0.7–0.8) from twGR yet accurate.

Injecting a no-GR physical constraint into the PF likelihood (always built 5-fold). Aligned CV and LB and neutralized the Public leniency of GR-over-reliance.

An ensembler that soft-averages candidates as "one curve each" (a hedge, not a selection).

Stacking decorrelated configurations (only candidates that are wrong in a different direction help) — the sole headroom late in the game.

Feature-combine > ensemble (combine and retrain, rather than train separately and blend).

Full smoothing (whole-interval) — the ensembler transfers the candidate-quality gain fully.

Sequence-NN (TCN) residual blend (largest lift on weak backbones).

De-shrink post-processing (restore the averaged, shrunk prediction with a single global gain a≈1.03) — CV 5.587 → 5.572 (−0.015). A one-parameter global correction, so it is physically motivated and transfers robustly.

FFBS-type particle smoothing (Godsill et al. 2004). As a whole-interval ancestral smoother it did work (raises candidate quality). But it is too slow, so I did not adopt it in the final pipeline — I replaced it with the lighter fixed-lag / full smoothing.

"Keep the PF plain and single; put the cleverness downstream" / match the inference seed to the tuning (avoid overfitting).

5. What didn't work

Nearly everything I tried that failed or back-fired:

Tuning PF parameters per well-category → overfits; loses badly to a single fixed PF.

Naive averaging of PF outputs → averaging estimates committed to different solutions collapses to the mid-point and breaks.

selfGR as an output blend (a "prediction") → double-counts with existing post-processing; non-transferring and harmful (useful as a feature).

Strengthening the physical (spatial) constraint standalone → lowers CV but back-fires on LB (the higher the prior density, the worse the transfer).

Well-level constant-shift post-processing (vote-hedge, …) → OOF −0.075 becomes LB +0.184.

Sharpening the ensembler's selection (top-k / cross-attention / candidate-relation / external-leg injection) → all saturate at ~0.99 correlation with the base.

Trying to solve it with tabular (row regression) alone → the frame doesn't fit a whole-segment two-way choice.

A PF-free 2D image CNN (GR-match image → U-Net) → good single CV, but LB collapses to 9.46.

Baking the physical constraint into tabular features → kills the good GR-derived transfer (the two trade off).

Attacking the two-way choice directly (global-MAP / Viterbi / sparse mode-switching) → GR can't pick the branch; what "worked" was just the physical constraint underneath.

Conditionally pulling the prediction back to a known value → none of the criteria for which to pull ever hit.

Learning the blend weights (meta-learning) → loses to hand-set fixed weights.

Heavy sequence architectures (iTransformer / Mamba-family / deep TCN / BiGRU) → saturate re-processing the same candidates; poor speed/benefit.

Using neighbor distance as a proxy for "stratigraphic closeness" → distance ≠ stratigraphy (the best GR reference is ~5,800 ft away on median).

Backward data augmentation (a reversed-well synthetic task) → in a weak engine (early on) CV −0.05 transferred well to LB −0.16, but in the final strong PF/ensembler it no longer helped — it actually caused a +0.2 worsening (a reverse transfer). The stronger the engine, the more the DA gain decays or flips.

Pre-training on synthetic wells → it didn't work. But this was probably because my generation (how the synthetic data was made) was poor — I can't conclude the approach itself has no promise.

### 3rd Place Solution

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rogii-wellbore-geology-prediction/discussion/733319

First of all, we would like to thank Kaggle and the competition hosts for organizing this excellent competition and providing such an interesting challenge. We are also deeply grateful to our team member @takoihiraokazu for their ideas, experiments, discussions, and persistence throughout the competition.

What made this competition especially enjoyable was the opportunity to explore an unusually diverse range of approaches—from probabilistic physics models such as HMMs and Particle Filters to GR-correlation models, neural sequence models, and dynamic ensembling. Investigating how these very different methods complemented one another was both technically challenging and genuinely fascinating.

Solution

TL;DR

The core idea of our solution is to generate several physically plausible TVT trajectories, complement them with neural models that make different kinds of errors, and combine the candidates at every position using SoftMax Gating.

Physics models: generate TVT trajectories from GR and well geometry using an HMM and a Particle Filter (PF).

Neural Network from Last PS: predict the future absolute offset and uncertainty relative to the last known TVT immediately before Prediction Start (PS).

Delta Neural Network: predict row-to-row TVT changes and integrate them to reconstruct a trajectory.

Adaptive 1D SDF: directly match horizontal-well GR and trajectory against typewell GR/TVT candidates. It does not use HMM or PF predictions.

Ensemble: dynamically mix five candidates—HMM, PF, two neural networks, and Adaptive 1D SDF—with a sequential SoftMax Gate.

Validation: run five-fold GroupKFold by well under five different split patterns, giving 25 checkpoints per model family.

Our fold-safe Local CV used for model selection was RMSE 5.2884. The submitted notebook scored 6.043 Public / 5.836 Private.

Solution Overview

[图 1: inbox%2F282250%2F43f69126892c72dda69ff6054624c6b2%2FExp417%20solution%20overview.png]

Before PS, TVT_input is visible for each horizontal well; after PS, TVT is hidden. We can still use the well trajectory (MD, X, Y, Z), horizontal GR, and the associated typewell information (TVT, GR, Geology).

Pure time-series extrapolation accumulates even a small formation-dip error over a long horizontal section. Pure GR matching is also ambiguous because similar GR patterns, GR bias, missing values, and thickness changes can produce several plausible alignments. We therefore used three stages:

Match GR with HMM and PF while preserving trajectory continuity.

Use physics predictions, trajectory, GR, and typewell information in the neural networks to correct systematic physics-model errors.

Dynamically combine five complementary candidates with a SoftMax Gate.

Validation Strategy

GroupKFold by Well

Rows from the same well are strongly correlated, so a row-wise split causes severe leakage. We used five-fold GroupKFold with well_id as the group and always placed every row of one well in the same fold.

The training set contains 773 wells and 3,783,989 rows. A split contains 155 / 155 / 155 / 154 / 154 validation wells. We evaluated OOF predictions using row-level RMSE, matching the competition metric.

What “Fold-Safe” Means

In this solution, fold-safe means more than splitting by well. The entire pipeline is closed within each fold so that validation labels—or intermediate predictions from models trained on a validation well—cannot flow back into feature generation, normalization, or gate training.

We applied the following safeguards:

Well-level split: all rows from the same well remain in one fold, preventing row-wise leakage.

Fold-local preprocessing: feature means, standard deviations, and related statistics are fitted only on the training folds and then applied to the validation fold.

OOF candidates: training inputs for the NNs, Adaptive 1D SDF, and CNN/BiLSTM gates use only predictions from fold models that did not train on the corresponding validation well.

Synchronized base-model and gate folds: base models and gates use the same well assignment within each split, preventing the gate from seeing in-fold base predictions.

OOF-only blending: final CNN/BiLSTM weights and model-selection decisions are determined only from OOF predictions, never by fitting to the Public LB.

Fold averaging at test time: every fold model is applied to the hidden test set, followed by averaging across five folds and five split patterns.

For example, the NN candidate supplied for a gate-validation well W is generated by an NN trained without W. The gate itself is also evaluated without training on W. Without this two-stage OOF protocol, the gate could exploit in-fold base-model errors and produce an unrealistically optimistic Local CV.

Why 5 Splits × 5 Folds?

The two “fives” serve different purposes:

5 folds: create leakage-free OOF predictions for every well and average five models at test time.

5 splits: change the GroupKFold well assignment itself and reduce dependence on a particular fold composition.

We used default, split101, split102, split202, split303. Well labels were deterministically reordered from each seed before applying GroupKFold. Pairwise Adjusted Rand Index values between the five assignments ranged from approximately -0.0013 to 0.0038, indicating nearly independent assignments.

Each split trained NN49, NN31, Adaptive 1D SDF, CNN Gate, and BiLSTM Gate in five folds:

Physics Models

HMM

[图 2: inbox%2F282250%2Fcf365e6a1fd7a8c1efe4cc3e31ca3999%2Fimage%20(4).png]

Instead of predicting each row independently, the HMM estimates the entire well as one continuous path. Its hidden state combines:

TVT position: which typewell TVT corresponds to the current horizontal-well row.

Formation rate: d(TVT + Z) / dMD.

GR bias: a slowly changing offset between horizontal and reference GR.

Reference family: a typewell-oriented or sibling-lateral-oriented reference curve.

Reference GR

Training wells identified as sharing the same typewell were grouped as siblings, and their lateral GR curves were aggregated into 0.25 ft TVT bins. The base HMM used two reference mixtures, with typewell/sibling weights of 0.2 / 0.8 in the two orientations, while reference identity remained part of the hidden state.

We also built a (TVT_input, GR) template from the target well’s known prefix and mixed it into the HMM reference according to the number of observations. This self-reference exploits the fact that GR calibration is shared between the known prefix and future section.

Initial State and Prefix-in-Model

Estimate the initial formation rate robustly from the last 256 known-prefix rows.

Prepend the last 128 known-prefix rows to the HMM sequence.

Clamp the prefix TVT states around the observed TVT_input values.

Compared with initialization from only the PS point, this carries the recent dip and GR alignment into the HMM posterior.

Emission and Transition

The GR emission used a Student-t distribution with one degree of freedom, making it more robust to outliers than a Gaussian likelihood. Transitions encouraged smooth changes in position, rate, and bias. Forward-backward smoothing then produced a posterior mean conditioned on the complete sequence.

Finally, we projected the predicted path using the 1st–99th percentile formation-rate range estimated from the last 256 known rows. The absolute rate was capped at 0.25, and the integrated TVT correction—not the correction per row—was limited to 10 ft.

Particle Filter

[图 3: inbox%2F282250%2F8b99c5b1b027f8ac8a47caaf225345c5%2Fimage%20(5).png]

The PF tracks many stochastic TVT trajectories, complementing the smoother HMM with multimodal hypotheses and uncertainty features.

Each particle state approximately contains:

formation position TVT + Z;

formation rate;

GR bias;

hard/soft observation family;

typewell-oriented/sibling-oriented reference flag.

The main settings were 500 particles, 240 seeds, three seed bases, rate momentum 0.9995, rate noise 0.001, and position noise 0.005. At every row, we evaluated horizontal GR against reference-GR likelihoods and applied systematic resampling when the Effective Sample Size fell below its threshold. Reference and bias families were inherited after resampling.

We used two observation models:

f015: 15% of particles belong to a hard family whose likelihood is gradually strengthened near the beginning.

soft: all particles allow GR bias.

Seed-level paths were weighted using full-sequence likelihood and smoothed by tracing particle genealogy backward. We stored the following as NN features:

weighted mean;

spread, skewness, and IQR;

q10, q25, q50, q75, and q90;

previous-row and next-row differences of those statistics.

The standalone PF candidate was 0.6 × f015 + 0.4 × soft. The HMMPF physics feature used 0.6 × three-family HMM + 0.4 × PF. The HMM contributes a smooth, stable global path, while the PF contributes alternative hypotheses and uncertainty.

Neural Networks

[图 4: inbox%2F282250%2F1af741aba62f039d302b230ee74c7e6b%2Fimage%20(2).png]

Neural Network from Last PS

This model anchors every hidden row at last_known_tvt, immediately before PS, and predicts:

target = TVT - last_known_tvt

Predicting an anchor-relative offset instead of absolute TVT removes much of the well-to-well TVT-level variation.

Features

The 49 features consist of:

Raw/mask: X, Y, Z, GR_filled, GR_missing, known_tvt_mask.

PS-relative geometry: md_from_ps, z_from_ps, Z_diff_1.

PF distribution: f015/soft mean offsets, spread, skewness, IQR, and q10–q90.

Local dynamics: previous/next differences for X/Y/Z/GR/TVT_input and PF statistics.

Physics candidates: HMMPF offset, MultiGrid HMM offset, and their gap.

Correlation candidate: anchor-relative Adaptive 1D SDF prediction.

Architecture

Input projection: 96 channels.

16 full-resolution dilated-TCN residual blocks.

Kernel size 7; dilation cycle 1,2,4,...,128, repeated twice.

MaxViT1D local-window/sparse-grid attention after blocks 4, 8, 12, and 16.

Downsampled typewell cross-attention after block 8.

Typewell encoder: residual encoder plus gated BiLSTM.

Output head: gated one-layer LSTM.

Gaussian head predicting both mean and variance.

The loss was Gaussian Negative Log-Likelihood over valid hidden rows:

L_GNLL = 0.5 × [log(σ²) + (y - μ)² / σ²]

The model outputs μ and a raw variance, with σ² = softplus(raw_variance) + 0.001 to guarantee a variance floor. At inference, μ is used as the TVT candidate. The predictive standard deviation is not added to the candidate; it is supplied as a confidence feature to the SoftMax Gate.

We trained for 100 epochs with batch size 16, Scheduler-Free AdamW, learning rate 0.002, weight decay 1e-4, dropout 0.1, and EMA decay 0.995. We also masked short contiguous GR blocks during training.

Standalone OOF RMSE on the default split was 5.4723. Across the three-split ensemble, switching the same NN from RMSE to Gaussian NLL improved RMSE from 5.4176 to 5.3645.

Delta Neural Network

Whereas the Last-PS NN directly predicts a long-range offset, the Delta NN predicts:

target_t = TVT_t - TVT_(t-1)

The deltas are integrated from the known TVT at PS to reconstruct the path. This representation captures local slope changes well, but small biases accumulate over long distances, giving it a different error pattern from the Last-PS NN.

The 31 features emphasize differences and uncertainty rather than absolute levels:

previous/next differences of X/Y/Z/GR/TVT_input;

PF spread, skewness, IQR, and their adjacent differences;

previous/next differences of HMMPF, MultiGrid, and Adaptive 1D SDF offsets;

TVT_input_observed_delta_last.

The architecture belongs to the same dilated-TCN cross-encoder family as the Last-PS NN, but it is an independently trained model with a delta target. Its loss is delta RMSE over valid rows:

L_delta = sqrt(mean((ΔTVT - ΔTVT_pred)²))

We used dropout 0.5, weight decay 0.01, and manifold mixup on the head representation with probability 0.5 and alpha=0.2.

Default-split OOF RMSE was 5.7703. Although weaker as a standalone model, it was retained because its local-slope representation was complementary. A Gaussian-NLL Delta pilot degraded performance, so the final model kept RMSE loss.

Training Augmentation

The two neural networks share an architecture family but use different augmentation suited to their targets:

Last-PS NN: mask short contiguous GR blocks and pass the missing/mask indicators to the model. This discourages dependence on isolated GR peaks and encourages recovery from trajectory, physics candidates, and surrounding context.

Delta NN: apply manifold mixup to the head representation with probability 0.5 and alpha=0.2 for the Beta distribution. This smooths the local-delta representation and reduces overfitting to fine-scale slopes of individual wells.

Augmentation was applied only to training folds, never to validation OOF or test inference.

Adaptive 1D SDF

[图 5: inbox%2F282250%2Fd2a1eb87ed53c06ea521d603a3f74426%2Fimage%20(3).png]

Adaptive 1D SDF is a correlation model that directly estimates which typewell TVT candidate corresponds to each horizontal-well row. It is independent of the HMM and PF predictions; the models meet only later in the SoftMax Gate.

Encode horizontal GR and trajectory using a four-block TCN.

Encode typewell GR/TVT context using a three-block TCN.

Construct a query-key cosine-correlation volume with embedding dimension 128.

Compare 256 TVT candidates on the typewell.

Fuse GR-value cost with trajectory progress, required formation rate, and graph-edge features.

Estimate well-specific GR-bias correlation and scale from the known prefix.

Apply SoftMax with temperature 1.75 and output the expected TVT.

Its loss was:

L_SDF = L_candidate_CE + 0.25 L_SmoothL1(TVT/10) + 0.10 L_delta + 0.05 L_RNC

L_candidate_CE: cross-entropy for the typewell candidate row nearest to the ground-truth TVT.

L_SmoothL1: regression loss on expected TVT from the candidate SoftMax.

L_delta: auxiliary loss for TVT differences along the packed sequence.

L_RNC: Rank-N-Contrast loss that teaches the embedding the ordering induced by TVT distance.

We trained for 100 epochs with batch size 8, hidden size 64, dropout 0.5, and EMA 0.999.

Its standalone RMSE was weaker than the other candidates, but it solved the task through direct horizontal/typewell correspondence rather than physics-state estimation or direct regression. This increased candidate diversity, and the SoftMax Gate could select it locally even when its average weight was small.

Ensemble SoftMax Gating

The gate received five candidates:

Three-family HMM.

PF.

49-feature Last-PS NN.

Adaptive 1D SDF.

31-feature Delta NN.

For every row, the gate used not only candidate values but also:

each candidate’s offset from the last known TVT;

deviation from the candidate mean;

one-step candidate changes;

candidate standard deviation and range;

MD/Z distance from PS, MD/Z steps, and 3D step length;

GR, GR difference, and GR-missing flag;

normalized position within the hidden section;

Gaussian predictive standard deviation from the Last-PS NN.

The gate outputs logits and applies SoftMax across candidates:

prediction_t = Σ softmax(logits_t)_k × candidate_(t,k)

The CNN Gate used hidden size 32, kernel size 7, and two residual blocks. The BiLSTM Gate used hidden size 32 and a one-layer bidirectional LSTM. Both were trained for eight epochs with:

L_gate = MSE(y, Σ w_k × candidate_k) + CE(w, best_candidate)

The first term is the regression error after convex mixing. The second is classification loss using the candidate with the smallest absolute error at that row as the target.

Why SoftMax Gating?

Fixed weights treat the area immediately after PS, long extrapolation ranges, missing-GR regions, and regions with large candidate disagreement identically. A SoftMax Gate can vary weights continuously using within-well context and candidate disagreement.

Nonnegative weights summing to one also constrain the output to a convex combination. With only 773 wells, this reduced extreme extrapolation compared with a high-capacity unconstrained stacker. We first generated physically plausible candidates and trained the gate to decide which candidate to trust, rather than generate an entirely new TVT trajectory.

The limitation is that a convex combination cannot correct drift shared by all candidates beyond their range. OOF oracle analysis confirmed this ceiling, so SoftMax represented a deliberate preference for robustness over maximum flexibility.

We also tested an unconstrained BiLSTM refiner that directly predicted last_known_tvt - target_tvt instead of mixing candidates. Gaussian NLL improved this direct regressor by 0.0140 over MSE, but its CV was 5.4023, 0.1226 worse than the SoftMax BiLSTM’s 5.2797, and it lost in all five folds. Constraining the model to select among plausible candidates generalized better than allowing unrestricted correction outside their range.

We averaged the five-split CNN and BiLSTM predictions separately and selected the final blend using OOF only. The saved deployment weights were CNN 0 and BiLSTM 1, making the final submission effectively a 5 splits × 5 folds BiLSTM SoftMax Gate ensemble.

Private-LB Insights

This section summarizes interventions that produced measured Private-LB improvements across our team’s submission history, whether or not they were included in the final notebook. Comparisons from a different solution branch are explicitly identified. Ideas that improved only Public LB or Local CV are excluded.

1. Reference-GR Design Was the Most Important HMM Decision

The largest Private gains came not from small NN-architecture changes but from which GR curves were used as HMM references:

use sibling-lateral GR from wells sharing the same typewell, not only the typewell itself;

aggregate sibling GR in fine TVT bins;

mix the target well’s known-prefix GR as a self-reference;

preserve reference families as separate HMM states or separate HMMs instead of averaging them away;

add HMMs with different error structures, such as Local-DTW.

In another solution branch, switching to a spatial-distance-weighted sibling HMM improved Private LB from 5.817 to 5.691 (-0.126). Rational distance weighting improved it from 5.817 to 5.720 (-0.097). Although these were not identical to the linked notebook, they consistently showed that reference quality and diversity dominated Private generalization.

2. Using the Known Prefix Well

In one branch, expanding initial-rate estimation from the last 30 known rows to the last 256 improved Private LB from 5.854 to 5.812 (-0.042). The longer window was less sensitive to short-term fluctuation immediately before PS and gave a more stable estimate of the well-specific formation trend.

Prepending the known prefix inside the HMM and clamping its TVT state was also more stable than using only a last-value anchor. Long hidden sections made drift the dominant Private-test error, so small improvements in the starting condition accumulated across many rows.

3. Averaging Five GroupKFold Patterns

Increasing the same Gaussian-NLL configuration from three to five split patterns improved Private LB from 5.903 to 5.836 (-0.067). Adding OOF diversity across different well assignments transferred better to Private than selecting models against Public LB.

4. Physics Refinements That Helped Private Outside the Final Solution

In separate branches not necessarily included in the final notebook, adding Rao-Blackwellized PF bias improved Private LB from 5.691 to 5.688 (-0.003), while fold-specific HMM reference pools improved it from 5.726 to 5.718 (-0.008). The gains were small, but both support the same direction: adapt the reference or latent bias at the well level.

Our main takeaway is to prioritize the right reference GR, stable initial-rate estimation, diverse HMM error modes, and well-level CV diversity before adding model complexity. The neural networks and SoftMax Gate were powerful, but the physics pipeline provided the foundation for Private generalization.

### 2nd Place Solution: AnchorCNN — Conditional Probabilistic Path Modeling

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rogii-wellbore-geology-prediction/discussion/733432

First of all, thank you to the hosts for organizing this competition.
(I also thank my faithful partner Claude Code Agent :D)

My public LB rank kept stagnating, which made the final stretch a really tough fight. I wanted as many people as possible to know about this solution, so I was determined to finish near the top. I kept training models until 3 hours before the deadline, and I'm happy that it resulted in 2nd place on the private LB.

TL;DR

I trained an anchor-grid CNN that predicts conditional move distributions P(dTVT|TVT) on physically consistent synthetic data, decoded the expected path exactly with dynamic programming, and selected models with validation designed to be robust to a handful of dominant wells.

Key insight from the data

The formation surfaces interpreted by experts (the ANCC, BUDA, etc. columns in the train data) have a characteristic "sequence of straight lines" shape. Writing z_layer for the depth of the formation surface (so that its increment dz_layer is the formation-slope component), the identity

dTVT = dz_layer − dz (1)

holds (it follows directly if you define z_layer := TVT + z − b_well; z is the measured TVD of the trajectory and is known for every row even at test time). Looking at real samples, dz_layer/dMD is staircase-shaped: constant over most intervals, with occasional steps. Since the identity fixes the coefficient at −1, wherever there is no step, the shape of dTVT matches −dz exactly (this is why the two curves run parallel in the figure below; steps appear in only about 10% of the columns).

[图 1: inbox%2F4910466%2F9954341cc673109084b67a9c7a4ba6ca%2Fdzlayer_vs_dtvt.png]

→ By feeding dz/dMD (dip z) as an input feature, the model's job shrinks to "estimating the smooth structural slope dz_layer", strongly steering it toward formation surfaces that experts would plausibly draw.

Synthetic data

My synthetic data generation rests on the following generative assumption (a layer-cake idealization: rocks at the same stratigraphic depth share the same properties):

GR_obs = f(TVT) + r(TVT) (2)

f: the TVT–GR profile of the typewell

r: residual noise

Since (2) is a function of TVT, it has a remarkable property: given any TVT trajectory, you can generate an unlimited amount of data consistent with this assumption. Where the assumption breaks (GR deviating per well even at the same TVT), I measured the deviation from real data and carried it into the synthesis through the residual r, as described below.

Data synthesis pipeline

The vertical material (the typewell's level→GR profile) and the horizontal material (the TVT trajectory along MD) are sampled from independent pools, and composed by reading out f(TVT) to form a pseudo (MD, TVT, GR) sample:

Sample one master from the master pool (54 systems)

Crop a window = the typewell profile f

Sample a TVT trajectory shape from the real-well pool (horizontal pool) and place it at a depth inside the level band

Read out the GR along the trajectory: f(TVT(MD)) (noise-free)

Add a residual r sampled from the residual bank (773 real wells) = the simulated (noisy) GR_obs

[图 2: inbox%2F4910466%2Fd2976eee71822612c1ec1a97ec755041%2Fsynth_pipeline.png]

The next three subsections detail these stages in pipeline order.

Synthesizing typewell GR (steps 1–2)

As pointed out in a discussion comment [3], the train typewells can be consolidated into a small number of master well series. My own verification consolidated them into 54 systems, confirming that every typewell in a system is fully contained in a single master TVT–GR series. Cropping arbitrary windows from these master series generates a typewell profile f for any level range.

Generating TVT trajectories (step 3)

Feeding curves that could never appear in train makes the model learn spurious features, so instead of free-form curves, trajectories are generated in two steps from real train trajectories.

(a) Shape: deform the shape of real trajectories with vertical shifts and mixup of two trajectories:

TVT_mix = λ·TVT1 + (1−λ)·TVT2 (3)

(b) Placement into a system (re-skinning): assign the shape to a typewell system and decide the depth to place it at. The quantile the trajectory occupied within the drilling band of its source system (the distribution of levels where wells of that system are actually drilled) is mapped to the same quantile of the target system's drilling band. The shape is preserved, the depth becomes realistic for the target system, and the GR is re-generated from the target system's master series (= a new "skin").

 
Note (consistency of z): while transforming trajectories, z_layer — not z — is kept as the internal representation, and z is derived at the very end as z = z_layer − TVT. This keeps identity (1) consistent by construction, no matter what transform is applied.

Adding residuals (step 5)

Examining the residual r = GR_obs − f(TVT) of real wells, it decomposes into a smooth well-specific mean profile that emerges when you aggregate over TVT, plus a remainder:

r(TVT) = δ_w(TVT) + ε (4)

δ_w: the systematic component — the well-specific signature of "how much this well's GR deviates from the typewell at each depth" (the mean of r aggregated over TVT)

ε: the remaining non-systematic noise; not white, it carries autocorrelation

For each synthetic well, noise was injected in one of two ways:

Pasting real residuals: paste a real well's residual sequence r as-is (this carries over both δ_w and ε with all their real-data properties)

Synthesis: draw δ_w as a smooth random function of TVT, and mimic ε with an AR noise model fitted to real data

This produced synthetic data that carries realistic "well-specific GR profile quirks".

Augmentation

Augmentations were applied to both real and synthetic wells at training time. The main ones:

Geometric: MD flip, level flip (with label sign flip), random crop along MD, level shifts (±32 ft)

Structural: flips and scaling in z_layer space (stretching the structural slope). Consistency with identity (1) is preserved by the z_layer internal representation

Length normalization and stitching: short wells are extended to a target length by copy&paste of themselves or fragments of other wells, with seams smoothly joined by short splines (100 ft). I also used a copy aug that redundantly extends a segment inside the eval zone to X + reverse(X) + X (figure below; values stay continuous at the seams, only the gradient flips)

Observation quality: specaug that masks spans of GR (up to 21 columns) in exactly the same format as real missing data

Modeling

I started from the CNN that @hengck23 shared in the discussion [1] (unfortunately, the paper's authors had not released a detailed implementation of the model [2]).

GR matching in this task is fundamentally ambiguous. The typewell GR profile contains repeating, similar-looking stripes, so locally, multiple level hypotheses — assumptions that "the well is at this depth right now" — match the GR equally well. In other words, the posterior is multimodal, and there are multiple plausible trajectory candidates (multi-path). I confirmed this in early experiments: a model that regresses a single teacher path indeed collapses onto the average of these modes — a path that corresponds to none of the hypotheses — and is therefore mismatched with the task.

My reading of [2] is that its essence is to model multi-path as a conditional probability model P(dTVT|TVT): holding, for every level hypothesis simultaneously, the distribution of "if the well is at depth TVT now, how much does it move up or down over the next interval". So I designed a probability model that expresses this explicitly.

AnchorCNN

The basic design philosophy takes inspiration from the anchor-based / anchor-free family of object detectors (YOLO [4], CenterNet [5], etc.). CNNs are good at encoding local features, and the move direction dTVT at a specific coordinate (MD, TVT) is easy to predict from local features. So I mapped the conditioning part — "at coordinate (MD, TVT)" — onto CNN anchors. Here an anchor means the embedding vector at coordinate (i, j) of the CNN feature map. Coordinate (i, j) corresponds to a level hypothesis (being at level i in MD column j), and from each anchor the model reads out a move distribution conditioned on that position.

Concretely, the outputs of every efficientnet-b0 stage are fused by an FPN-style decoder (each stage projected to a common width by 1×1 conv, interpolated to a single resolution, and summed), then mapped onto a (B, 21, 128, 336) state grid (vertical = level at 2 ft/bin, horizontal = 32 ft/column). From the anchor at each grid point, the model predicts the discrete move distribution P(dTVT | anchor). The move vocabulary — the values dTVT can take — has 21 classes: {0, ±2, ±4, …, ±20} ft/column (i.e. {−10, …, +10} bins of 2 ft).

[图 5: inbox%2F4910466%2F197506000b320097cfcff6a0e7af3191%2Farchitecture.png]

Training

Supervision is teacher-forced along the ground-truth path: at each column, the anchor lying on the ground-truth level receives a cross-entropy loss against the quantized ground-truth move (plus an auxiliary intra-bin position loss). Each epoch mixes the real training wells (~620 per fold) with 2,048 synthetic wells generated on the fly. Models were trained for 120 epochs with a constant learning rate of 1e-3 after warmup (WSD schedule with no decay) and an EMA of the weights. The full 5 folds × 3 seeds took about 10 hours on a single GPU.

Path generation

Starting from the PS point, there are two ways to generate paths from the probability field on the state grid:

Rollout: sample stochastically from P(dTVT | anchor) to generate a bundle of random paths

Path expectation (DP): marginalize the state grid with dynamic programming to compute the expectation of the path distribution exactly. Starting from a point mass at the PS level, the marginal p_k over levels is propagated column by column, and the prediction is its per-column mean:

p_{k+1}(s) = Σ_{s'} p_k(s') · P(dTVT = s − s' | anchor(s', k)) (5)
TVT_hat(k) = Σ_s p_k(s) · TVT(s) (6)

No sampling is involved — (5)–(6) are computed exactly.

Initially I planned to generate N=1024 rollout paths and filter them with a reranker, but that turned out not to work, so the submissions use the "average path" computed by DP. For what it's worth, Viterbi (maximum-likelihood path) and other mode-tracking decoders were consistently worse than the expectation.

Ensembling and TTA

The final submission averages: 5 folds × 3 seeds = 15 checkpoints of DP paths × MD-phase TTA (8 views) × 2 grid resolutions (32 ft/column and 16 ft/column). The whole ensemble scores the 200 hidden test wells in about 40 minutes on the Kaggle T4.

MD-phase TTA: the MD resolution of the CNN input is 32 ft (one column), so a phase ambiguity below 32 ft — where exactly the columns are cut — remains. Relative to the GR correlation length (~18 ft), the per-column box averaging is coarse, and the input values themselves change depending on where you cut the columns (aliasing). To compensate, I ran inference at 8 sub-resolution phases, shifting the column-grid origin by {0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56} ft, and averaged the predictions.

Robust validation and model selection

The competition metric, pooled RMSE, is proportional to the sum of squared errors, so it is easily dominated by "catastrophic wells". The metric moves far more when you fix a few badly failing wells than when you further improve wells that are already doing fine.

The question is whether an improvement on a few wells is systematic or just luck. For example, if the direction of improvement across the 5 dominant wells is random, it should be considered chance, not a real improvement.

At the same time, the data is small: 773 train wells, and only 148 wells in the private test. This makes statistically careful model selection essential.

Robust acceptance test (leave-largest-contribution-out)

When comparing two models base and treat, compute the per-well difference of squared-error sums (SSE)

g_w = SSE_treat(w) − SSE_base(w)

and remove wells in descending order of |g_w| (i.e. the wells contributing most to the difference). Define k* as the number of removals at which the improvement first disappears. Unless k* was large enough — i.e. unless the improvement did not hinge on the luck of a few wells — I rejected the candidate.

The figure below shows a real example (removal curves of two candidates against the baseline). The orange curve appears to improve OOF by −0.28 at k = 0, but removing just 8 of 773 wells almost erases the gain — the improvement hinged on the luck of a few wells, so this candidate was rejected. The blue curve decays gently and its gain survives even after removing 52 wells (the size of the public LB), so this candidate was adopted.

What didn't work

I'll list only the rejections that became design forks.

Selecting from a path bundle (reranker, reinforcement learning): generating a rollout bundle (N=1024) does put very good paths inside the bundle — an oracle that picks the best path in hindsight beats the deployed score by several ft. Trying to capture that gap, I built a reranker (a model that picks good paths from the bundle) and tried reinforcement learning (GRPO), but both selectors overfit the evaluation data easily and never consistently beat the DP expectation. In the end I abandoned the "select from a bundle" design and committed to DP marginalization (expectation decoding).

Explicit formation modeling: modeling the formation surfaces themselves from neighboring wells captures the coarse topography of the field, but could not fix the catastrophically bad wells. My guess is that the teacher labels (expert interpretations) do not reference the formations directly — they are relative judgments based on GR matching. This result settled the design: no explicit formation model; commit entirely to the probability field over GR matching.

Final thoughts

What this model imitates is the interpretation work of geosteering experts — and the ambiguity in that work is intrinsic to the task. Rather than forcing the ambiguity to resolve prematurely, this approach keeps it alive as a probability distribution over the candidate moves (P(dTVT|TVT)). I believe this is its most practically valuable property: where the GR matching is ambiguous, the model can present alternative path hypotheses and flag low-confidence intervals for human review. And if independent external data (e.g., offset wells or seismic) becomes available, it can be used to reweigh the hypotheses and narrow down which of the ambiguous paths is the most plausible.

What did not work

A good solution is also a museum of failed ideas.

Softly averaging geological sheets

This produced physically impossible “middle layers.” Hard commitment worked better.

Trusting the neural branch probabilities as perfectly calibrated

The network often separated repeated layers too confidently. A separate raw-GR alignment posterior
was more reliable than trying to hedge using those probabilities.

Median filtering the final curve

A short median filter helped the two selection folds, but after freezing it slightly worsened the

A more complicated quadratic correction field

Curved corrections had huge oracle potential, but legal GR evidence could not reliably identify the
extra curvature. More flexibility was not automatically more accurate.

A fully autoregressive local tracker

With a trusted anchor it was extremely accurate, but small anchor mistakes accumulated during
rollout. Local matching was easier than recovering absolute geological identity.

First find several plausible paths. Then use geology, continuity, and clean sensor evidence to
choose one complete path.

The neural network was the map reader. The HMM kept the journey on one road. The actual-GR posterior
checked that we had not chosen the correct-looking road on the wrong geological floor.

Or, in child-sized language:

 
Draw a careful line, do not jump between pages, and check the real clues before declaring that
you found the treasure. 🏴‍☠️

### 26th Place - Stacked UNet + Synthetic Well Pretraining

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rogii-wellbore-geology-prediction/discussion/733136

Thank you to the hosts and everyone that competed. This was a challenging competition and I was unfortunately unable to stay in the gold zone after the shakeup, but I learned a lot the last few months and look forward to reading other peoples solutions.

Final result was CV 5.038 / 6.60 on the private LB.

TLDR

My approach treats each well as an image: rows are bucketed TVT depths, columns are bucketed MD steps. The vertical axis is a fixed 256 ft window centered on the last known TVT (the projection-start anchor), split into H bins of 256/H ft each (256×1.0 ft for the main models, 224×1.14 ft for one diversity member). The horizontal axis is the well's entire MD range split into W = 2H equal columns, so one column aggregates a handful of consecutive log rows. A stacked UNet consumes this image and outputs, for every MD column, a softmax distribution over the depth rows, trained with cross-entropy against the binned true TVT. The final prediction is the expected value of that distribution (soft-argmax), which gives sub-bin, continuous depth from a classification head - one forward pass predicts the entire well.

The 5 input channels:

Typewell GR - the typewell log interpolated onto the row grid, so each row carries "the GR the stratigraphy says lives at this TVT." Constant across columns; z-scored per well.

Horizontal GR - the lateral's GR averaged per MD column, so each column carries "the GR the bit actually measured here." Constant down rows; z-scored per well.

Hint channel - the known pre-projection-start trajectory rasterized as a one-hot curve (pixel lit at the true depth row for each labeled column, zeros after projection start). This anchors the prediction and turns the task into continuing a visible curve into the unlabeled region rather than finding it from scratch.

Cost volume - the squared difference between channels 1 and 2 at every (row, column): a full correlation surface where dark valleys mean "the GR measured at this MD matches the GR the typewell expects at this depth." This is the classical geosteering cross-correlation panel, handed to the network as a feature instead of decoded by hand.

dZ channel - per-column average dZ/dMD (the borehole's local vertical gradient / inclination proxy), z-scored and clipped, constant down rows. Tells the network how steeply the well itself is moving through the frame.

Intuitively: channel 4 paints where the well could be, channel 3 pins where it was, channels 1-2 are the raw ingredients so the net can second-guess the hand-built cost surface, and channel 5 supplies the trajectory physics. The model's job reduces to tracking the plausible ridge of the cost volume forward from the hint, which is why a segmentation-style backbone with a per-column classification head fits the task naturally.

Model Specifications

I started with a standard ImageNet-pretrained ResNet34 from SMP. When I hit a wall around 7.5 CV, I started experimenting with larger backbones. I went straight to the EffNet B7/B8 to test if capacity alone could increase performance - it did.

Then I went to an EffNetV2 Large, then the XL, and lastly the EffNet L2, which is the largest EffNet member - when stacked, it was just under 1 billion parameters. The recipe that allowed me to do this was: larger backbone -> bigger pretrain size -> heavier augmentations. The actual performance increases plateaued with the XL, but I trained an L2 purely for diversity, as the L and XL are too correlated. The B7/B8s were not decorrelated enough to compensate for their higher CV (around 5.8), so they were dropped from the final ensemble.

Other than that, the model was the standard SMP UNet class, with the decoder channels expanded from (256, 128, 64, 32, 16) to (512, 256, 128, 64, 32) - the rest are the default settings (this improved CV in early experiments).

Stacking / Re-feeding

With this trend of increased capacity netting lower CV, I decided to try a stacked UNet approach. Stage 1 sees the 5-channel image and produces a first per-column depth distribution. Stage 2 sees the same 5 channels plus stage 1's softened output as a 6th channel - a "belief plane" painting where stage 1 thinks the trajectory runs - and produces the refined answer. The handoff is softened with a temperature (T=1.5) and the gradients between the two UNets are detached (allowing gradient flow led to overfitting), so stage 1 is trained by its own cross-entropy loss on the same target (at half weight) - stage 2's loss never touches it. Each stage independently learns to predict the trajectory; stage 2 just gets to see stage 1's opinion while doing so. I got this stacked UNet idea from reading Ruby's solution to the Yale/UNC-CH competition.

The interesting part is inference. Since stage 2 was trained to consume a belief plane, you can feed it its own output and run it again: predict, soften, re-feed, repeat. So, I checked to see if I could re-feed k times and get further improvements - it did at k=2. To push this further, during training, the model sometimes ran multiple rounds in-graph (with probability 0.5) precisely so it would learn to refine multiple rounds of beliefs, with the hope being I could push k higher - it worked to k=3, with performance degrading beyond that.

This self-refeed lets the model reconcile its trajectory estimate with the GR evidence again. This mechanism worked for the EffNetV2 L and XL models, with the optimum at k=3 refeeds. For the L2, refeed was actively worse, with the model quickly overfitting with each k>1.

Pretraining on Synthetic Wells

Real training data is only 773 wells — small for a standard pretrained CNN. So every model is first pretrained on synthetic wells, then finetuned on real ones. The first versions of the UNet model (ResNet34) trained jointly with synthetic wells at a lower loss weight, but that did not fly with the larger models, and pretraining ended up being the best approach. It also saved time, as I could run multiple finetune experiments on the same pretrained models.

A synthetic well starts from a real typewell column: the stratigraphy is borrowed, then a fake lateral is drilled through it — a generated trajectory wandering up and down through the layers, plus geological events (faults, unconformities), measurement noise, and calibration drift. Rendering that through the same image pipeline gives unlimited (well image, true depth curve) pairs.

I learned via brute force experiementation that synthetic data should not imitate real data. I repeatedly tried to close statistical gaps between synthetic and real wells, and it made things worse almost every time. What worked was a curriculum reading: harder-than-real on the geology (big fault throws, aggressive trajectories) and cleaner-than-real on the matching signal (crisp GR correspondence between lateral and typewell). The synthetic world's job is to teach the skill, not to pass a statistical test against reality.

The synthetic pool is generated per fold from that fold's training typewells/geology only, so validation wells are unseen even at pretraining; the deployed models use a pool from all training wells, applying the same rule at test scale. I am not sure if this is strictly necessary, but I played it safe at the cost of additional pretraining. Furthermore, each seed was given its own pretraining phase, to avoid "getting lucky" pretrained weights that were then inherited by each finetune phase. 

Augmentations

Real wells are scarce, so the finetune phase leans hard on augmentation. All of it operates on the raw well before image rendering, so the rendered image stays self-consistent:

MD warp: smoothly stretch/compress the well along its length, so the same geology appears at different lateral scales.

Bed warp: smoothly perturb bed thicknesses in the typewell, so the model can't memorize exact layer spacings.

Horizontal flip: reverse the well direction (with the dZ channel's sign flipped to stay physical).

Hint drop: randomly delete parts of the known-trajectory hint, so the model can't lean entirely on the anchor and must keep reading the GR correlation.

Anchor jitter: randomly offset the vertical window relative to the last known TVT, so the model can't learn that the trajectory always starts at the center row and must locate the anchor from the hint channel instead.

Training also uses an EMA of the weights (decay 0.997) as the actual checkpoint - the raw weights are noisy; the averaged copy is consistently better. I also used a small batch size of 8 to help with regularization.

Seed Noise

From the start, I noticed a wide range of CV results with different seeds and generally volatile training. Pretraining, EMA, and augmentations tightened up the per-seed spread, but in the end, I elected to ensemble across 7 seeds per fold. 5 seeds was where the actual seed ensemble boost plateaued, but I added another 2 for peace of mind. I typically had anywhere from 0.2-0.6 ft of ensemble boost compared to the mean across seeds, depending on the variance of seed models across that particular fold. EffNets also helped - I tried many other backbones and could not get healthy training from them, so I settled on staying in the EffNet family.

CV Results

My CV was grouped by well, stratified by bucketed TVT range, to try to balance fold difficulty. TVT range (how far a well strays from its anchor) is a strong predictor of how hard a well is, and with 773 wells across 5 folds, an unstratified draw can pile the hard wells into one fold and make scores swing on luck rather than model changes.

I claimed at one point on the discussion board that you could achieve sub-5 ft RMSE with the right model. When I said this, my best model CV was 4.98 - however, there was one fold where the results looked a bit lucky to me, so I retrained it and settled on the "fairer" looking weights, which raised my CV to 5.136, which is the lowest I was able to get any single model. I have never dealt with this "luck" factor before, so am unsure if this was a principled move.

Final Ensemble

Despite the larger-capacity UNets, inference was relatively quick with this "well image" approach, so I was able to ensemble many UNets.

Model 1: EffNetV2 L, k=3, 5 folds with 7 seeds per fold, with CV 5.157

Model 2: EffNetV2 L (smaller image), k=3, 5 folds with 7 seeds per fold, with CV 5.252

Model 3: EffNetV2 XL, k=3, 5 folds with 7 seeds per fold, with CV 5.136

Model 4: EffNet L2, k=1, 5 folds with 7 seeds per fold, with CV 5.26

The final blend was an even-weight ensemble, as the optimized weights offered a marginal improvement and I did not want to overfit seed noise. The final CV was 5.038 and it scored 6.60 on the private LB. 

During the last week, I tried to train a GRU but could not get below CV 7.8. Interestingly, the same binning of TVT / MD + CE loss was much better than direct regression with Huber loss, and the stacked approach also helped. In the end, I abandoned this approach, so the final ensemble is not particularly decorrelated - I am sure it could have been pushed further with more competitive non-UNet models.

### 1st Place Solution

I'd also like to thank everyone who shared ideas publicly, especially @tuckerarrants , who motivated me to explore neural networks early in the competition, and whoever originally shared the particle filter baseline (unfortunately I couldn't trace the original author).

Most of the code was written with Codex using GPT-5.5/5.6. For the full implementation, I recommend giving the code to an AI coding agent and asking it to explain the details. The repository also contains many inactive experimental features, which can safely be ignored.

Task Formulation and Loss

I formulate the problem as a 2D alignment task and use cross-entropy loss as the main objective.

The alignment grid is:

Horizontal well: 345 positions (downsampled by 32), consisting of 1,024 visible plus 10,000 target region.

Typewell: 400 positions covering ±100 ft around the last visible TVT with 0.5-ft resolution.

Throughout this write-up, all TVT values are relative to the last visible TVT, which serves as the anchor.

The training target is an exponentially smoothed probability distribution centered around the ground-truth alignment and normalized along the typewell dimension.

Additional losses:

Huber loss on the expected TVT path, computed from the predicted probability map and grid TVT values.

GR penalty loss: mean(probability × grid_GR_gap), which provides a small additional improvement.

Model

I use a standard 2D U-Net with a ConvNeXt backbone:

timm/convnext_small.in12k_ft_in1k_384

Standard residual blocks in the decoder.

Replacing LayerNorm in ConvNeXt with BatchNorm consistently works better, although BF16 training is required to avoid NaN losses.

For downsampling and upsampling, simple average pooling + interpolation performs better than learnable alternatives.

Particle Filter

This component was largely developed with the help of AI coding agents since I was not very familiar with particle filters. The standalone local CV is approximately 7.4 RMSE.

Compared with the public baseline, I believe the major improvements come from:

Allowing low-probability large jumps

Calibrated typewell GR

FFBSi smoothing

Blending diverse cfg profiles

Updating particles in bins (64 samples) instead of raw resolution

There are also many smaller implementation details that can be found in the released code.

XY Neighbor Information

Assume the local geological surface satisfies

 
S = TVT + Z + C

and S is locally linear, then

 
ΔTVT = aΔX + bΔY − ΔZ

where (a, b) are estimated by weighted least squares using (x,y) neighbors.

The implementation includes anisotropic distance, singular-case handling, regularization, and several other engineering details. However, these contribute only marginal improvements over the simple formulation above, so I won't discuss them further.

The standalone local CV is approximately 11.4 RMSE.

Adding XY-neighbor information consistently improved local CV but hurt the public leaderboard, so I performed several analyses.

XY-neighbor prediction alone scores 12.9 LB which lies in high prob region for 50 wells sample.

I replaced XY based predictions with GR + z_diff predictions whenever neighborhood statistics exceeded the 95th percentile of the training distribution.

The neighborhood statistics include:

Mean neighbor distance

10th percentile neighbor distance

Prefix/visible neighbor weight ratio

Distance(query, neighbor center) / average distance(neighbor, neighbor center)

Average abs cosine corr((x_diff,y_diff),(x_nbr_diff,y_nbr_diff))

None of these statistics explained the leaderboard degradation, and I believe they sufficiently describe neighborhood quality.

Therefore, I attribute the discrepancy to inconsistent labels and chose to trust the local CV, which consistently improves by about 0.3 RMSE.

These same neighborhood quality statistics are also used later in the ensemble.

Data Augmentation

The two most important augmentations are Z-shift and GR transform.

Z-shift

Randomly sample a TVT path while keeping TVT + Z unchanged.

The sampled path is generated using block bootstrap on real TVT differences.

GR is regenerated by matching the typewell using:

TVT + TVT noise

where the TVT noise consists of smoothed white noise.

Rare fault jumps are also simulated, introducing offsets in TVT + Z.

GR transform

Apply

 
GR' = a × GR + b

to the typewell GR, forcing the model to rely more on shape than absolute values.

What didn't work

Transformer-based backbones 

In my experiments, CNN-based backbones consistently performed better, and only ConvNeXt contributed to the final solution.

Pretraining on simulation data first

Training with simulated/augmented data and real data jointly worked better for me than a two-stage approach that first trains on simulation data.

Using PF features in the final single model

Initially, PF features provided a significant improvement, reducing CV RMSE from 7.7 to 6.7. However, after adding more augmentations and switching to stronger pretrained backbones, PF features no longer improved my best single model and I kept them in my final ensemble for diversity.
Considering that my PF-only approach achieved around 7.4 RMSE in CV, a stronger PF-based method may still provide additional gains.

Fancy loss weighting strategies

I experimented with various loss weighting schemes, but none of them provided consistent improvements.

Removing visually bad-labeled wells

Removing wells with visually poor labels did not result in stable gains.

Scaling up to larger models or higher resolution

Further increasing model size or input resolution did not improve performance in my experiments.

### [7th Place Solution] HMM + UNet (agent is all you need)

来源：https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rogii-wellbore-geology-prediction/discussion/733154

I and and my teammate (@ ejixxx) thank the hosts and everyone who contributed to public discussions and code. It is a tough but interesting journey in this competition. Without any geology knowledge, we are excited that agents can help us achieve this place. We did not even know the details or how our solution worked in the end. What we mainly did was to stop agents from cheating and point the potential resources to agents to obtain new ideas. The final results show that most of our recent solutions can achieve gold medals, which is impressive that agents can provide such a solid solution. What we learned from this competition is not only that agents are very powerful, but we also realised several mistakes that agents might make and how we alleviate them. The typical mistakes are:

Cheat with data leakage. For example, borrow the bundle from public code, and blend it with our local solution without realising that the splits might be different. After this, we will ask agents many times to ensure their local improvements.

Give up very quickly. Agents, especially Claude agents, since they usually copy context during the conversation, making them get more and more confirmed once they think the goal cannot be achieved. The only solution for this is to start a new session. If you are unsure about the details and just tried to give general ideas like try UNet, Transformer, and etc, would be better to do several rounds of verification and confirm with agents. For example, we already tried UNet earlier in our 7-8 public LB solution and they didn't show much improvements on the public LB. When in the 5-6 public LB region, UNet helped a lot.

Agents cannot devise new ideas. Agents are good at replicating but not at innovation. We need to explicitly prohibit agents from infinitely small perturbations like meaningless hyperparameter tuning.

Agents need resources provided by humans. We find that the websearch and literature search of Claude agents are not very smart. They usually use similar, long, and specific keywords for the search, which makes the search results very narrow. We alleviate this problem by directly specifying the resources, the keywords, and papers that can help agents get more insights.

One thing we are not satisfied with is that we did not develop a unified agent workflow for the Kaggle competition. One harness (e.g., AutoResearch/Tree-based AI Scientists) is not enough to obtain a very high place, but we believe there should be a method/workflow that can help agents achieve very high performance without humans involved. Anyway, the above is our write-up for how to use agents to build our solutions; the following are the explanations for the solution built by agents.

1. The pipeline

[图 1: inbox%2F7470058%2F37ed4948d9ec1f2612034e89184990c3%2Ffig0_pipeline.png]

Every stage is scored the same way: GroupKFold(5, seed 42) out-of-fold over all 773 training wells, through the shippable inference path — the model that runs on a fresh unseen well, never a post-hoc blend that couldn't be reconstructed at inference.

[图 2: inbox%2F7470058%2Fc5cb7f0e3e8c3a4362365f4c2cdea8ff%2Ffig1_stages.png]

The HMM alone is 5.676. Learned emissions take it to 5.301. The refiner stage — the part that took the longest to get right — takes it to 5.209, and the gated hedges close at 5.2025 pooled / 5.2128 near-end.

2. The idea that made stage 3 work: pretrain on fabricated mistakes

The refiner is a second-pass UNet that sees the first-pass prediction as an extra input channel and is asked to fix it. The obvious way to train it — finetune on real wells with the out-of-fold column as input — fails, and the diagnosis is the interesting part:

 
A fold net decodes its own training wells at RMSE 3.20 but held-out wells at 9.34.

So during training the conditioning channel is nearly perfect, and the cheapest way to cut the loss is to copy it. The net never learns when the input is wrong, because in its training distribution it almost never is. Trust calibration is destroyed by memorization. The fix: learn trust on wells that cannot be memorized. We generate ~6000 synthetic wells and fabricate the conditioning channel:

u_ship = truth + amplitude × low-passed random walk (anchored at the tie-in)
amplitude ~ lognormal(median ≈ 2.9, σ = 0.68) (matches the measured
15% of wells drawn as "good ship" (amplitude 0.3–1.0) real error distribution)

Pretrain 60 epochs on these, then finetune only 8 epochs on real wells. Now the net has seen thousands of wells where the input column is confidently wrong in realistic ways, and it has learned where to distrust it — a skill that survives the short real-data finetune. The pretrained state is reusable across conditioning generations, which made the whole later campaign cheap (10 min per refiner variant). v9 ships the plain version of this: two such refiners, averaged.

3. The gated-signal principle

Several auxiliary signals looked worthless: the neighbour-well field prediction correlates with the composed column's residual at r ≈ −0.02. Blending it in flat does nothing. But the two refiners disagree precisely where they are unsure. Using their per-node
standard deviation as a gate, g = σ/(σ+1), and applying the hedges only through that
gate:

p ← mean(refiners)
p ← p + 0.10·g·(HMM core − p) shrink toward the structural prior
p ← p + 0.15·g·clip(neighbour − p) the "dead" signal, revived
p ← p + 0.15·g·clip(fuse leg − p)
p ← p + 0.05·g·clip(neighbour − p)

Each stage is worth little alone; gated, together they are worth 0.006 pooled and 0.013 on the hard near-end wells. The general lesson: a signal that is useless on average can be useful exactly where your ensemble admits it is lost.

4. Why it is called "wall-survival"

Every submission we made in the preceding 24 hours scored nothing — they all hit the 9-hour runtime wall. The hidden set is much larger than the 3-well stub the notebook sees, so the binding metric is seconds-per-well, not stub time. v9 is the version that was engineered to survive:

numba-JIT forward-backward for the HMM: banded tridiagonal transition kernel, unique-emission-row exponentials, float64 running state. 25 s → 12 s per decode, output-exact to 4e-14 (verified against the numpy path before shipping).

1-flip decode on the main and fuse legs (drop the flip-TTA — a real accuracy cost, taken deliberately).

CPU-warm / GPU-decode overlap: a prefetch thread decodes the first wells' UNet legs while the forkserver workers are still warming.

fp16 autocast with NaN guards, per-family decode chunking, batched pip installs, gc.disable() around model load.

Result: T4 stub 5 m 54 s, ≈50–65 s per hidden well — enough margin for a hidden set several times larger than anything we had evidence for. It scored; the fancier variants that came later were built on top of this runtime foundation.

5. The honest postscript: which ruler was telling the truth

We finished the competition with five constructions of increasing sophistication. They disagree about which is best, and which ruler you ask decides the answer:

[图 3: inbox%2F7470058%2Fbba471dcca4583e964f3506db0c1254b%2Ffig2_rulers.png]

Local CV said: v18 best, v9 clearly worst (5.083 vs 5.203).

Public LB said: exactly the opposite ordering — v9 best (5.518), v18 worst (5.590).

Private LB said: local CV was basically right after all — v9 is last (6.057), the
enriched constructions cluster at 6.009–6.015.

We chose v9 because of the public LB, reasoning that the extra pool enrichment in the later versions was fitting the out-of-fold error structure and not transferring. A paired bootstrap over our 773 wells said the public inversion had only a 1.75% chance of being sampling noise — so we trusted it.

That reasoning was wrong in an instructive way. The public split was small enough to produce a fully inverted ranking, and our bootstrap measured our well distribution, not the public split's. The leak-free local CV had the ordering right the whole time.

It didn't cost us: the private spread across all five was 0.048, and the rank is identical for any of them — v9 held 7th place. But the takeaway for the next competition is clear: a rigorously leak-free local CV over 773 wells beats a public score computed on a fraction of that, and small public-LB differences are not evidence. The one thing the public LB did establish reliably was the constant offset (local + ≈0.31), not the ordering within a 0.1 band.
